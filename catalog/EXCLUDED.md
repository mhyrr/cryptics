# Excluded candidates

Considered and left out, with the reason. Add to this file rather than
silently skipping something, so the next session does not re-propose it.

Reasons: `script-awaits-bilingual` (fails the material test), `hoax` (with the
debunking cited), `solved` (with the solution cited; move to `entries/` with
`status = "solved"` only if we want it as a calibration case), `not-a-text`
(pure image or object), `open-by-design` (author intended irreducible
ambiguity and said so), `out-of-scope` (other).

| Candidate | Reason | Note / citation |
|---|---|---|
| Linear A | script-awaits-bilingual | No bilingual; ~1,400 short inscriptions. Corpus too thin for method alone. |
| Indus script | script-awaits-bilingual | Average inscription ~5 signs; Farmer–Sproat–Witzel "non-linguistic" thesis still live. |
| Rongorongo | script-awaits-bilingual | ~26 objects; Fischer's reading not accepted. |
| Proto-Elamite | script-awaits-bilingual | Numerical/administrative content largely understood; the rest awaits more tablets. |
| Cretan hieroglyphic, Cypro-Minoan, Byblos syllabary | script-awaits-bilingual | Same class. |
| Phaistos Disc | script-awaits-bilingual | One object, 45 distinct signs in 242 tokens and 61 sign-groups (123/119 and 31/30 per side); the script is not attested elsewhere at any length, so the corpus cannot constrain a reading. Authenticity was once doubted but is now generally accepted. Same class as Linear A. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaistos_Disc |
| Bern Riddles (Aenigmata Bernensia / "Riddles of Tullius") | solved | The 63 riddles circulate with their subjects attached (1 Pot, 2 Lamp, 3 Salt, … 62 Stars), and the standard translation project prints those solutions; the answers are transmitted, not deduced. What is open is authorship, origin (a Lombard? Bourges? c. 700 CE) and date — an attribution question with almost no evidence, not an unresolved constructed meaning. Ed. Glorie CCSL 133–133A (1968), Strecker MGH (1914), Bitterli (2023). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bern_Riddles and https://theriddleages.com/ |
| Empedocles, Strasbourg papyrus (P.Strasb. gr. Inv. 1665–1666) | out-of-scope | Published by Martin and Primavesi in 1999; adds over 50 lines of *On Nature* and is read. The live dispute is editorial arrangement — one poem or two, and where the ensembles belong — which is a question about the order of a legible text, not about a meaning that has not been recovered. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empedocles |
| Sibylline Oracles | out-of-scope | Books 1–8 and 11–14 survive in a 14th–16th century manuscript tradition and are fully edited (Geffcken 1902) and translated; no section is undeciphered. The disputes are date, Jewish versus Christian redactional layers and provenance — source criticism, not recovery of meaning. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibylline_Oracles |
| Book of the Dead spell 17 | out-of-scope | The spell is fully translated and its ancient glosses ("what does this mean?") are themselves the transmitted commentary; the residue is theological reference and lost cultic context, not an unread text. Reconsider only if someone shows that specific passages resist translation rather than interpretation. |
| Silk dress cryptogram (Bennington/Maine dress, 1888) | solved | Two sheets of coded word-lists found in a hidden pocket of an antique silk dress (found 2013 by Sara Rivers-Cofield); long on "top unsolved codes" lists. Solved in 2022 by Wayne Chan (Univ. of Manitoba): a US Army Signal Service weather-telegraphy codebook, each word standing for a measurement. Verified by matching against the surviving codebook, which is the strongest class of confirmation. Worth **promoting to `entries/` as a calibration case** — a cipher that fell to identifying the codebook rather than to cryptanalysis — but I could not fetch a working source within budget (the Wikipedia title 404s and the Univ. of Manitoba news URL redirects), so it is parked here rather than asserted. Solution published in *Cryptologia* (2022); citation to be pinned. |
| Smithy Code (Mr Justice Peter Smith, 2006) | solved | Italicised letters planted in the High Court judgment in *Baigent v. Random House*; solved within days by Dan Tench of the Guardian using a Fibonacci-keyed variant of the Caesar/Vigenère family, yielding a message about Admiral Jackie Fisher and the *Dreadnought*. Author-confirmed. No residue. |
| Oak Island 90-foot stone | not-a-text | The inscribed stone is lost; every version of its "inscription" derives from late, mutually inconsistent second-hand reports, and the best-known transliteration ("forty feet below two million pounds are buried") traces to a 19th-century schoolteacher's reconstruction. Fails the material test: there is no object and no reliable witness to the signs. |
| Picatrix (*Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm*) | out-of-scope | Meaning not in dispute: a talismanic-astrological handbook, critically edited in Arabic (Ritter, Warburg 1933) and Latin (Pingree, Warburg 1986) and fully translated into English (Attrell & Porreca, Penn State UP 2019). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picatrix — The residual questions are philological, not interpretive: the author (Ibn Khaldūn's Maslama al-Majrīṭī is chronologically impossible against the text's own 348 AH completion date; Maribel Fierro proposes Maslama b. Qāsim al-Qurṭubī, d. 964), the etymology of "Picatrix," the lost Castilian intermediary of 1256–58, and the 200+ largely unidentified source texts. That last one is a genuinely attractive mass source-parallel sweep and would justify re-admission as an `attribution` entry if someone wants it; it is not an unresolved intended meaning. |
| Zohar authorship | solved | Fails admission test 3: the field is convinced. Traditional attribution to Shimon bar Yochai is "universally rejected by modern scholars"; Scholem's case for Moses de León (c. 1240–1305) writing it in Castile c. 1280–86 rests on Aramaic grammatical errors, Arabic and Spanish calques, ignorance of the land of Israel, dependence on the Babylonian Talmud, references to the Crusades and to Tiberian vocalization, and Isaac of Acre's report of de León's widow. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohar — The live residue is single-author versus circle (Liebes, Meroz) and whether there ever was one "original book" (Abrams); that is a real dispute but it is not an unresolved constructed meaning. Daniel Matt's Pritzker edition is the working text. |
| Shams al-Maʿārif (al-Būnī) letter squares | out-of-scope | The squares are not encrypted: they are magic squares and *abjad*-value arrangements over divine names, a documented technique of ʿilm al-ḥurūf. The live problem is recension history — the printed *Shams al-maʿārif al-kubrā* is a compilation mixing genuine Būnī material with later additions (al-Bisṭāmī among them), and Coulon argues the work is pseudepigraphic on anachronism grounds, with Noah Gardiner's "corpus Būnianum" work the standard treatment. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_al-Ma%27arif — A philological project, not a puzzle with a hidden determinate message. **Stronger candidate inside the same corpus:** the *Birhatiya* oath (*al-Qasam al-Birhatiyya*), a long string of unintelligible names recited for eight centuries with no accepted language of origin. Not admitted here only because no source for it could be verified within this pass's budget. |
| Sefer Raziel HaMalakh | out-of-scope | A medieval Ashkenazi-Hasidic grimoire (Eleazar of Worms the likely compiler; not datable before the 13th century), in ordinary Hebrew and Aramaic, containing mystical midrash on creation, angelology, zodiacal magic, gematria, divine names and amulet instructions. No undeciphered script or layer is reported in the scholarship. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sefer_Raziel_HaMalakh — Its problems are recension and transmission problems, including the relation to the Latin *Liber Razielis* made for Alfonso X. |
| Ezekiel's merkabah (Ezekiel 1, 10) | out-of-scope | The Hebrew is translated and the vision's imagery is traced to Neo-Assyrian and Babylonian iconography. What is unresolved is what a prophet saw and what it meant theologically — a question of religious interpretation, not of a constructed meaning withheld from the reader. The later *Hekhalot* and *Shiur Qomah* literature is a separate candidate if anyone wants it. |
| Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas) | out-of-scope | Fully translated (Birrell, Strassberg, Richard Smith); its difficulty is that its referents — creatures, mountains, foreign peoples — are unidentifiable in the real world, which is a problem of ancient geography and zoology rather than an unread text. Also the *Tianwen* entry's principal comparative source, where it is treated as evidence and explicitly not as an answer key. |
| Kālacakra "coded" passages (Vimalaprabhā and related) | out-of-scope | The Kālacakra literature's technical registers, deity correspondences and Śambhala geography are translated and commented on within the Tibetan scholastic tradition itself; its obscurity is doctrinal and initiatory, with the commentary supplied. No determinate concealed text is at stake. The nearest genuine puzzle in the area is the Kālacakra's own "twilight language," which is covered by the Caryāgīti entry. |
| Zhang-zhung texts (Bon canon) | script-awaits-bilingual | A language problem, not a text problem. The Zhang-zhung material in the Bon canon is lexical and formulaic, its grammar is barely attested, and Dan Martin has argued that much of what is labelled Zhang-zhung in later Bon works is artificial or reconstructed by the tradition itself. The *mDzod phug* offers a Zhang-zhung/Tibetan bilingual, but its reliability is exactly what is in dispute, so the decisive evidence is not in hand. |
| Popol Vuh | out-of-scope | Not an unread text. The Ximénez manuscript (Newberry Library, digitized) is a K'iche'–Spanish parallel text and the K'iche' is well understood; Tedlock's and Christenson's translations are standard. The open questions are about the hieroglyphic or pictorial sources behind the alphabetic version — a source-criticism problem. |
| Dresden Codex | out-of-scope | Maya script is deciphered and the codex's astronomical apparatus — the Venus table, the eclipse/lunar table — has been read since Förstemann (1880); SLUB Dresden has digitized it. **But note the residue:** the Serpent Series, its very large distance numbers and ring-number base dates, and the 819-day count remain genuinely unresolved specialist problems with a determinate arithmetic answer (Beyer, Thompson, the Brickers, Michael Grofe). That would be a legitimate entry — a small, numerical, fully digitized target — and it is parked here only because no source covering the Serpent Series could be verified within this pass's budget. |
| Song of Songs | open-by-design | The Hebrew is read; what is disputed is genre and reference — wedding cycle, anthology of love lyrics, cultic drama, allegory of Israel or the Church. Two millennia of allegorical readings are readings of a text that is not withholding anything, and no construction of a hidden meaning by its author is in evidence. Fails admission test 1. |
| Codex Gigas | out-of-scope | Contents fully identified and read: Vulgate, Josephus, Isidore's *Etymologiae*, Cosmas of Prague, medical texts, the *Chronicle of Bohemia*, calendar and necrologium, plus the famous devil portrait. Digitized in full by the National Library of Sweden. The "mystery" is popular — the legend of a night's work with the devil's help, and the missing leaves — not scholarly. |
| Hávamál, Ljóðatal (and Vǫluspá) | open-by-design | The Ljóðatal (Codex Regius, GKS 2365 4to; stanzas 147–165) enumerates eighteen *ljóð* by number and describes only what each one does — "the songs themselves are not given, just their application or effect described." The withholding is the text's form, not a loss or a cipher: Odin declines to teach the eighteenth. There is no determinate encoded content to recover, so the entry would fail admission test 1 in anything but a trivial sense. Vǫluspá's cruxes (the Dvergatal, the identity of *inn ríki* in st. 65, the Codex Regius / Hauksbók divergence) are source-critical and interpretive, not an unresolved constructed message. Reconsider only if someone argues that the Ljóðatal numbering encodes something checkable. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A1vam%C3%A1l |
| Ruthwell Cross | out-of-scope | The Anglo-Saxon runic inscription is legible and read, and corresponds to lines 39–64 of *The Dream of the Rood* as preserved in the Vercelli Book; there is no undeciphered text. The live scholarly questions are whether the runic panels are contemporary with the 8th-century cross or a later (possibly 10th-century) addition, and whether editors have improperly "corrected" the runic readings from the Vercelli manuscript — a text-critical and dating dispute, not a recovery problem. The cross was smashed in 1642 and reassembled in 1823 with a replacement crossbeam, which is why the dating argument is hard. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruthwell_Cross |
| Pearl | out-of-scope | Complete in one manuscript (BL Cotton Nero A.x), 101 stanzas and 1212 lines, with elaborate but fully described numerical structure (five-stanza sections except XV, concatenation linking each section to the next, the 1212 lines read against the heavenly Jerusalem's twelve-thousand furlongs of Revelation 21). No cipher or hidden text is claimed in the scholarship. The debate — elegy versus allegory, and the significance of the 101st stanza — is ordinary interpretive disagreement of a kind the catalog would otherwise have to admit for most of medieval literature. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_(poem) |
| Rígsþula | out-of-scope | Preserved in the Codex Wormianus (AM 242 fol., 14th c.) and breaks off mid-narrative on the last surviving leaf as Konr is directed toward conquest — genuinely incomplete, but incomplete by physical loss, with no encoded or withheld meaning in what survives. The scholarly disputes are date and provenance (10th to 13th century) and whether the three estates are presented as separate kinds or as a common stock; both are interpretive. Fails admission test 3. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%ADgs%C3%BEula |
