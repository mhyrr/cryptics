# Splendor Solis

## What it is
An illuminated alchemical treatise in German, attributed to Salomon Trismosin, said to have been Paracelsus's teacher. Its core is a sequence of twenty-two elaborate paintings in book-of-hours borders — armoured figures, a drowning king, seven flasks bearing the planets, a rising sun over a city — accompanied by a German text of alchemical parables. Roughly twenty versions survive. The best known is British Library Harley MS 3469, dated about 1582 and the source of nearly all modern reproduction; the earliest witnesses date to 1532–1535. Other copies are in Berlin (Kupferstichkabinett 78 D 3), Paris, Kassel and Nuremberg.

## What is unsolved
Who "Salomon Trismosin" was — the name is generally taken for a pseudonym and there is no documentary trace of a person, which also unsettles the Paracelsus connection. Second, what the twenty-two plates encode: the death-and-rebirth-of-the-king narrative is legible in outline, but the specific referents (the seven planetary flasks, the hermaphrodite, the children's and women's-work scenes) are read differently by different interpreters, and the relation of image to the accompanying text is loose. Third, the date: the pictorial style has been argued to point earlier than the manuscript evidence allows, and no witness has been securely tied to an author's original.

## What survives
About twenty manuscript versions. Harley MS 3469 is digitized (thirty-four images on Wikimedia Commons, derived from the British Library's imaging); the Berlin and Paris copies are partly digitized. The German text exists in printings from the later sixteenth century onward and in English as *Splendor Solis: Alchemical Treatises of Solomon Trismosin*, translated by Julius Kohn ("J. K.") in 1920, long the standard English access; the 2019 Watkins facsimile of Harley 3469 with commentary by Stephen Skinner, Rafał Prinke, Georgiana Hedesan and Joscelyn Godwin supplies a modern translation and study. There is no critical edition collating the twenty witnesses and no machine-readable transcription of the German across copies.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Jörg Völlnagel's *Splendor Solis oder Sonnenglanz* (Deutscher Kunstverlag, 2004) is the art-historical monograph and the necessary first reading: it establishes the manuscript family, the borrowings from Dürer, Holbein and Cranach in the borders and figures, and the workshop context. Joachim Telle's work on German alchemical literature places the text in its Paracelsian reception. The 2019 Watkins volume is the best current synthesis in English and is explicit that Trismosin is unidentifiable. The field's position: the *images* are now reasonably well accounted for as products of a specific south-German workshop culture quoting known prints, while the *program* — what the sequence is instructing — remains interpretive, and the author remains a name without a person.

## What a solution would have to do
For the author: a documentary attestation of Trismosin, or a demonstration from the text's dialect, sources and printing history of who actually composed it. For the images: extend Völlnagel's source-identification to every figure in all twenty-two plates, then show that the identified print sources were selected according to a rule that tracks the text's argument — because if the borders and figures are workshop stock, a reading that treats each detail as encoded is reading the model book. For the program: a plate-by-plate correspondence to the accompanying German parables, tested by predicting the plate that belongs to a parable withheld from the fitting, plus an account of why copies disagree in their plate sets. Any chemical reading must name substances consistent with 1530s German practice and explain the seven planetary flasks as a set, not one at a time.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: real and open, but the outline narrative is agreed and the dispute is over referents and authorship rather than over whether the thing can be read at all.
- material 4: about twenty witnesses, the principal one digitized, a modern facsimile with translation; held from 5 by the lack of any critical edition or cross-witness transcription.
- solvable 3: the German text is readable and determinate; whether the plate sequence encodes one intended operation divides serious readers.
- compute 3: image-source matching against digitized German print corpora (Dürer, Holbein, Cranach) is a genuine and partly undone computational job, and a stemma across twenty witnesses is available in principle — but most witnesses are not digitized, so the data is not ready.
- verifiable 3: a print-source match is checkable image to image and could win consensus; the program reading would not become mechanical.
- crowding 3: one art-historical monograph, one modern scholarly facsimile, and a long popular reproduction history; the obvious approaches have been started but not finished.
