# Lycophron, Alexandra

## What it is
A single unbroken speech of 1474 iambic trimeters in which a guard reports to Priam the prophecy uttered by Cassandra — Alexandra — about the fall of Troy and the fates of the Greek and Trojan heroes, running forward through the Persian Wars to Alexander the Great and to Rome. It is famous for being unreadable without help: names are replaced by riddling periphrasis, and the vocabulary is thick with hapax legomena and invented compounds. Its author was already called "the obscure" in antiquity, and a modern assessment calls it "may be the most illegible piece of classical literature, one which nobody can read without a proper commentary."

## What is unsolved
Two things, and they need separating. The periphrases themselves are largely decoded: the Byzantine scholia of Isaac and John Tzetzes supply the referents, and the modern commentaries have worked through the mythography. What remains open is (1) who wrote it and when — the traditional attribution is to Lycophron of Chalcis, the tragic poet of the Alexandrian Pleiad under Ptolemy Philadelphus (285–247 BCE), but verses 1444–1450 describe Rome's rise to world power and Roman victories over Macedonians, which fit the aftermath of Flamininus' victory at Cynoscephalae in 197/6 BCE far better than the third century; and (2) whether the obscurity is a system with a determinate program — a political statement, a cultic or genealogical argument, a display piece for the Alexandrian school — or whether the difficulty is the point and the poem is designed to resist paraphrase. The second question is the one that decides whether this entry is a puzzle at all.

## What survives
The complete poem, in a good medieval manuscript tradition, with two layers of ancient and Byzantine apparatus: paraphrases and the Tzetzian scholia, which are themselves a primary source for lost mythography. Critical editions: Eduard Scheer (1881–1908, with the paraphrases and Tzetzian scholia), Carl von Holzinger (Teubner, 1895, German translation and commentary), George Mooney (1921), André Hurst and Antje Kolde (Budé, 2008). Simon Hornblower's commentary with translation is the current standard in English (reprinted 2017). A. W. Mair's 1921 Loeb Greek-and-English text is out of copyright and freely available (see `digitized`). The text, the scholia and the whole comparative Greek corpus are digitized, which puts this entry in a different material class from every other item in this category.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The field is split on date and authorship and says so. Stephanie West (2006) defends the attribution to the tragic poet and treats the Roman passages as interpolations. Peter Marshall Fraser (2000) argues for composition in the immediate aftermath of Flamininus' victory and for deliberate pseudepigraphy. Thomas Nelson and Katherine Molesworth (2021) propose that "Lycophron" is itself a pen name, a joke on the sophist Lycophron's reputation for stylistic frigidity. On the poem's character, the dominant reading treats the obscurity as Alexandrian learned display rather than encryption; Evina Sistakou and Hornblower have pushed the aesthetic and the historical readings respectively. A newcomer reads Hornblower's commentary, Hurst and Kolde's Budé, and Fraser and West against each other on the date.

## What a solution would have to do
On the date and authorship, this is the rare literary-historical question with a genuinely computable side. A claim must (i) treat the Roman passage as evidence, either by showing on independent grounds that it is an interpolation — which means metrical, lexical and stylistic separation of those verses from the rest, measured, not asserted — or by accepting a post-197 BCE date for the whole; (ii) survive a stylometric and metrical comparison against dated third-century and second-century Greek verse, run on the digitized corpora, with the method stated and the null model given; (iii) explain the pseudonym if it posits one, including how a second-century poem acquired a third-century attribution that every ancient source repeats. On the program, a claim that the poem encodes a determinate message must state what is encoded, show that the encoding is systematic rather than local (the same device used the same way across all 1474 lines), and predict the referent of a periphrasis the scholia get wrong or leave blank — a testable move, since there are such places. A claim that the poem is obscure by design should be scored as a real answer, not a failure, and would be supported by showing that its periphrastic devices are not invertible without external knowledge the poet could not assume.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the poem is read, and what is open is date, authorship and intent — real disputes with a real residue, but the text is not a locked box.
- material 5: a complete 1474-line text with ancient scholia, multiple critical editions, and full digitization. The best material score in this category.
- solvable 3: the referents have determinate answers, but the further question about a program is exactly the case where serious scholars hold both "designed meaning" and "designed difficulty"; the score reflects the open question, not the decoded surface.
- compute 4: stylometry and metrical statistics on a complete text against a large digitized Greek corpus bear directly on the dating dispute and have barely been attempted; a lexical sweep for the hapax legomena across the corpus is likewise undone. Not 5 because no computation can establish intent.
- verifiable 3: a consensus on date could form within years from metrical and lexical evidence; a consensus on the program probably could not.
- crowding 2: a substantial modern literature (Hornblower, Hurst and Kolde, Fraser, West, Sistakou) and centuries of commentary; not 1 because the quantitative approaches are untouched.

## Unverified claims
- Whether any passage of the Alexandra is still considered unexplained by the commentaries. The encyclopedic source does not address it; the claim that the riddles are "largely decoded" rests on the existence of the scholia and Hornblower's commentary rather than on a statement that no crux remains. Anyone attacking this entry should first count the places where Hornblower declines to identify a referent.
- Whether stylometric or metrical dating work on the Alexandra has been published. None was found, but no search of the specialist literature was possible in this pass, so the `compute` and `crowding` scores are the least secure numbers here.
- The precise wording and attribution of the "most illegible piece of classical literature" judgement, quoted at second hand.
- Whether the Hurst–Kolde Budé or Hornblower prints a materially different text from Scheer.
