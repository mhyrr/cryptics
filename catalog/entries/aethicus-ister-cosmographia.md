# The Cosmographia of Aethicus Ister and its invented alphabet

## What it is
A Latin prose account of a journey around the world, purportedly by a philosopher named Aethicus of Istria, transmitted inside a frame in which a figure called Hieronymus — meant to be read as St Jerome — says he has excerpted and censored an older heretical work. Both the traveller and the epitomator are fictions; the text is, in the standard characterisation, a medieval forgery. It describes peoples, places and marvels, incorporates Alexander legends, and presents a cosmology that has been read as flat-earth, possibly in mockery. It dates to the seventh or eighth century. At the end of the work, in a number of manuscripts, stands an alphabet of invented letters with names, presented as Aethicus's own script.

## What is unsolved
Three linked questions. (1) **Who wrote it, where, and why.** The candidates named in the literature include an Irish author, an author connected to Archbishop Theodore's school at Canterbury (Michael Herren), Virgil of Salzburg, an author of late Avar background (Heinz Löwe), and a Scythian author from what is now Romania (Franz Brunhölzl). (2) **What the alphabet is.** Löwe identified a striking correspondence between Aethicus's letters and the Old Turkic script; others have compared runic, Greek and Hebrew letterforms. Whether the alphabet is a genuine borrowing, an invention built out of available letterforms, a cipher for writing Latin, or decoration has not been settled; its purpose within the text is unexplained. (3) **What genre the whole thing is.** Straight forgery, scholarly hoax, satire of cosmographical learning, a school exercise in the Hisperic manner, or a serious compilation dressed in a false frame — the choice is unresolved, and it is the same question that hangs over Virgilius Maro Grammaticus, with which the *Cosmographia* is often paired.

## What survives
A substantial and complete text in multiple manuscripts: one copied at Tours, the majority produced in German centres. Editions: Heinrich Wuttke (1854); Otto Prinz, *Die Kosmographie des Aethicus*, MGH Quellen zur Geistesgeschichte des Mittelalters 14 (1993), the standard critical text; and Michael W. Herren, *The Cosmography of Aethicus Ister: Edition, Translation and Commentary* (Brepols, Publications of the Journal of Medieval Latin 8, 2011), which supplies the first full English translation and is where work must start. The invented alphabet is transmitted in the manuscripts, so its letterforms are recoverable from them, but the forms vary by copy — which is itself evidence about how well scribes understood what they were copying. No open digital critical text was located.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The *Cosmographia* was taken as a genuine ancient text through the Middle Ages and was mined by later cosmographers; the modern history is a sequence of localisation arguments — Löwe's Avar/Salzburg thesis, Brunhölzl's Danubian one, Herren's Canterbury/Insular one — none of which has become standard, and each of which rests on different classes of evidence (onomastic, linguistic, alphabetic, source-critical). The consensus is narrow: the frame is fictional, the text is seventh- or eighth-century, its Latin belongs to the same broad Insular-influenced world as the Hisperic texts, and the alphabet is not a known script used for a known language. Herren's 2011 edition reset the field by making the text readable in English with a commentary; the questions above are live.

## What a solution would have to do
The alphabet is the tractable part and should be attacked first, because it is a bounded, checkable problem. (1) Collate the alphabet across every manuscript that carries it and publish the letterforms, names and order per witness, with a stemma — this establishes what the author actually wrote as opposed to what scribes made of it. (2) Test the derivation claims quantitatively: compare the reconstructed archetypal forms against Old Turkic, Greek, Latin, Hebrew, runic and the ornamental alphabets circulating in Insular manuscripts, using a stated shape-similarity metric and a null model, and report how many letters match each candidate above chance. Löwe's correspondence claim is exactly the kind of assertion that should be measured rather than admired. (3) Test whether the alphabet is usable: does the letter inventory fit Latin phonology, or does it fit some other language's, and does any manuscript contain text written in it? If a passage in the script exists anywhere, that is the decisive evidence. (4) On authorship and origin: a claim must rest on the text's sources — which identifiable works the author had read — rather than on the marvels he reports, and a source census against the machine-readable Latin corpora is doable and would constrain the milieu directly. (5) Any genre claim must explain the alphabet's presence, which the localisation arguments generally treat as a detachable curiosity.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the text is edited and translated and its fictional frame is agreed; what is open — authorship, origin, the alphabet's nature, the genre — is a substantial residue rather than a wide-open central question.
- material 4: a complete, substantial text in multiple manuscripts with a modern critical edition and English translation and commentary. Not 5 because there is no open digitization and the alphabet's manuscript variation is not published in collated form.
- solvable 3: the text plainly means things, but whether the alphabet and the frame encode anything determinate is exactly what serious scholars disagree about.
- compute 3: the letterform comparison and the source census are both genuinely computational and mostly undone; but they constrain rather than decide, and the genre question is a judgement.
- verifiable 3: a source census or an above-chance letterform match would be recognised by consensus; only the discovery of connected text in the script would be mechanical.
- crowding 4: a handful of serious treatments — Wuttke, Prinz, Löwe, Brunhölzl, Herren — and a small field; the collation and quantitative work described above appears not to exist.
