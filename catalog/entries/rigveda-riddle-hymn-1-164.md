# Rigveda 1.164 — the asya vāmasya riddle hymn

## What it is
A long hymn in the first book of the Rigveda, opening *asyá vāmásya palitásya hótuḥ*, ascribed to the seer Dīrghatamas, who is credited with hymns 1.140–164. It is not a hymn of praise but a sequence of enigmas — a *brahmodya*, the Vedic riddle-contest genre — built out of paradoxical images: a wheel with twelve spokes and 720 sons; a one-footed, two-footed, three-footed and nine-footed creature; two birds in a fig tree of whom one eats the sweet fruit and the other only looks on; the father known by way of what is above and the son by way of what is below. It contains the most quoted line in the Veda, 1.164.46: *ékaṃ sád víprā bahudhā vadanti*, "the existent is one, the inspired poets speak it in many ways."

## What is unsolved
What each enigma answers to. Some are agreed: the wheel with twelve spokes is the year, the 720 sons the days and nights, and several stanzas are self-glossing. Others are not. The two-birds stanza (1.164.20–22) has been read as sun and moon, as two kinds of priest, as the individual and the universal self, and as a deliberately underdetermined image; the stanza sequence itself may not be a single composition, and where the hymn's own answers stop and the tradition's guesses begin is not settled. The deeper open question is whether the hymn is a riddle-contest with recoverable answers, a liturgical text whose enigmas are the point, or an anthology of both.

## What survives
The Rigveda is the best-transmitted ancient text in existence: memorized phonemically, with accent, by an unbroken recitation tradition, and independently confirmed by the Padapāṭha and Krama analyses. Machine-readable editions of the Śākala saṃhitā are freely available (TITUS Frankfurt; GRETIL Göttingen; the metrically restored van Nooten–Holland text; VedaWeb at Cologne, which aligns the text with Geldner's German and Jamison–Brereton's English). The indispensable modern translation is Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton, *The Rigveda* (Oxford, 2014); Geldner's German remains the standard philological version; Renou worked on the enigmas specifically. Middle-Vedic prose — the Brāhmaṇas, the Upaniṣads, and the Atharvaveda parallels — is also fully digitized, and that is where the ancient answers, where they exist, are recorded.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Nineteenth-century Indology read 1.164.46 as evidence of a late monistic layer and dated the hymn accordingly; that argument has been abandoned, and the hymn is now treated as belonging to the Rigvedic corpus proper. The modern approach is to read the enigmas against the *brahmodya* genre: Louis Renou's work on the Vedic enigma, Willard Johnson's "On the Ṛg Vedic Riddle of the Two Birds in the Fig Tree" (*JAOS*, 1976) and his later work on riddle-contests, and Jan Houben on the *brahmodya* as a ritual verbal duel. Jamison and Brereton's commentary gives the current cautious position stanza by stanza: some answers are secure, some are guesses, some stanzas they decline to resolve. Nobody claims the whole hymn. The most useful three items for a newcomer are Jamison–Brereton's translation and notes, Johnson's JAOS paper, and Renou.

## What a solution would have to do
A solution here is stanza-level, not hymn-level, and the honest target is a defensible answer for every enigma with its evidential warrant stated. Concretely: (1) for each stanza, an answer supported by a parallel in the Rigveda's own formulaic vocabulary or by a Brāhmaṇa/Atharvaveda gloss, distinguishing ancient answers from modern conjecture; (2) an account of the stanza order — whether the sequence is a composed unit, a *brahmodya* round with a known structure, or an anthology, tested against the metre, the accent, and the distribution of formulae; (3) an explanation of the self-glossing stanzas that says why some riddles carry their answers and others do not; (4) for any stanza where competing answers exist, a criterion that selects between them other than theological taste, which is what has decided the two-birds stanza for two millennia; and (5) restraint about 1.164.46, which is not a riddle and whose apparent monism has been read back into the hymn far too often. A reading that solves the two birds and nothing else is a footnote, not a solution.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: resolved in outline with a real residue — the calendrical enigmas are settled, a dozen or more stanzas are not, and the genre question is open; a full reading would matter to Indology and little beyond it.
- material 5: the best case in the catalog. The text is phonemically exact, complete, accented, multiply digitized, and surrounded by a fully digitized comparative corpus.
- solvable 4: the *brahmodya* genre has answers by definition and the hymn advertises its own design with question-and-answer formulae; not 5 because some stanzas may be deliberately aporetic, and the ritual riddle-duel may have valued unanswerability.
- compute 4: the whole Vedic and middle-Vedic corpus is digitized and searchable, the cruxes are formulaic, and a systematic formula- and collocation-level sweep for parallels and glosses — ranking candidate referents rather than arguing them one at a time — is a well-posed and largely undone move. It can eliminate readings; it cannot decide.
- verifiable 2: a proposed answer is adjudicated by plausibility plus parallels, and competing readings have coexisted for centuries; stanzas with an ancient gloss would score higher on their own.
- crowding 2: a large philological literature (Geldner, Renou, Brown, Johnson, Houben, Jamison–Brereton) and an enormous devotional and popular one; the obvious philological approaches have been tried.
