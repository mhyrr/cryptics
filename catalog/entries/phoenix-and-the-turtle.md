# Shakespeare, The Phoenix and the Turtle (1601)

## What it is
A 67-line poem — thirteen trochaic quatrains and a five-stanza "Threnos" — printed in 1601 as one of a group of "diverse Poeticall Essaies" appended to Robert Chester's long allegorical poem *Love's Martyr*, printed by Richard Field for the bookseller Edward Blount. The appended poems are signed by Shakespeare, Marston, Chapman, Jonson and "Ignoto." Shakespeare's contribution stages the funeral of a Phoenix and a Turtle-dove whose love was "married chastity" and who leave no posterity. It has been called the first great published metaphysical poem in English.

## What is unsolved
Who the two birds are, and what occasion the volume commemorates. This is a referent problem, not a semantic one: the poem's syntax and argument are clear, but every noun that would anchor it — the Phoenix, the Turtle, the "bird of loudest lay," the Eagle, the Crow — is a figure whose external identity the text withholds. A second, dependent question is why Shakespeare, Jonson, Marston and Chapman all contributed to a volume by an otherwise obscure Welsh-connected poet, and what Chester's own allegory was about.

## What survives
The 1601 quarto of *Love's Martyr* survives in a small number of copies (Folger, British Library, National Library of Wales among them); a 1611 reissue with a cancel title page, *The Anuals of Great Brittaine*, also exists. The poem's text is short, stable and essentially uncontested; it is in every modern Shakespeare edition and in Wikisource and Gutenberg transcriptions. A. B. Grosart's New Shakspere Society edition (1878) reprinted the whole Chester volume and is the standard way scholars read the context; the Chester poem itself is far less studied than the appendix. Chester's dedication is to Sir John Salusbury of Lleweni, Denbighshire.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Four families of identification have dominated. (1) Carleton Brown (1913) read the birds as John and Ursula Salusbury, on the strength of Chester's dedication — a reading embarrassed by the Salusburys' ten children against the poem's childless "married chastity." (2) Katherine Duncan-Jones and H. R. Woudhuysen read the Phoenix as Elizabeth I and connect the volume to her 1601 "Golden Speech" figure of a symbolic marriage to her people. (3) A. B. Grosart (1878) and William Matchett (1965) read Essex and Elizabeth, which sits awkwardly with Essex's rebellion and execution in February 1601. (4) Clara Longworth de Chambrun (1930s) and, in a much fuller archival form, John Finnis and Patrick Martin (2003) read the birds as the Catholic martyr Anne Line, executed at Tyburn in February 1601, and her exiled husband Roger. The current position is that none has carried the field. New Critical readers made a virtue of the impasse; Helen Hackett's formulation is that the poem "incites deciphering, but at the same time firmly rebuffs it," and James P. Bednarz warns against "appliqué literalism" — treating a Neoplatonic argument as a coded newspaper.

## What a solution would have to do
A candidate identification would have to: (a) account for both birds, not one, and for the childlessness and the "married chastity" together, since these are the constraints that kill the Salusbury reading; (b) explain the *volume*, not just Shakespeare's poem — why Chester, why 1601, why a dedication to Salusbury, and how the other four poets' contributions fit the same occasion, since they were commissioned as a set; (c) survive the bibliographical evidence about when the volume was set and printed, which bears on whether the February 1601 executions could be in view; (d) produce external documentary support (a letter, a payment, a household record) rather than resemblance alone, because resemblance is cheap at this length; and (e) explain the Eagle, Crow and "bird of loudest lay" as well as the two principals. Absent (d), the field will continue to register the poem as an occasional work whose occasion is lost.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the referents are wide open and a documented occasion would change how a canonical poem and a strange collaborative volume are read; not 5 because the poem's argument is understood and the stakes are local to Shakespeare studies.
- material 4: the text is complete, stable and digitized, and the whole containing volume survives; short of 5 because the load-bearing evidence is external archival material that is patchy.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both "a specific commemorative occasion" and "a Neoplatonic exercise with no biographical key"; Chester's dedication is real evidence of an occasion, but 67 lines under-determine it.
- compute 2: a source-and-parallel sweep of Chester's allegory and of 1590s phoenix-and-turtle emblem literature is worth doing, but identification is a documentary judgement.
- verifiable 2: without new archival evidence competing readings persist; a manuscript naming the occasion would raise this to 4.
- crowding 2: a large critical literature and famous attempts by capable scholars; not 1 because the Chester volume itself remains comparatively unworked.
