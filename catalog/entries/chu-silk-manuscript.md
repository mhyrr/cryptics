# Chu Silk Manuscript (Zidanku) — the unread graphs and the twelve month-deities

## What it is
The oldest surviving Chinese manuscript on silk: a sheet 47 cm by 38 cm carrying 926 characters in three sections, looted from a tomb near Zidanku ("bullet storehouse") east of Changsha in Hunan sometime between 1934 and 1942. The tomb dates to about 300 BCE. Around the margins are a colour-symbolic tree in each corner and twelve masked zoomorphic figures, each labelled and each corresponding to a month. The three text sections are an inner long text warning of cosmic consequences if the calendar is not properly calibrated, an inner short text on the divine creation of the seasons, and a surrounding text giving the twelve monthly deities with their calendrical rules and prohibitions. It has been in the Arthur M. Sackler Gallery, Washington, since 1965.

## What is unsolved
Individual graphs and, through them, the identities of the twelve month-deities. The manuscript contains what paleographers call descendantless graphs — forms with no identified equivalent in the standard character set, probably reflecting Chu-region peculiarities — and the month-deity names are among them. Compounding this, exposure to light after excavation rendered parts of the silk illegible, so that some readings rest on early photographs and on infrared imaging rather than on the object. The larger interpretive question, unresolved, is how the text relates to the figures: whether the manuscript is a single integrated ritual almanac, a composite of a cosmogonic myth and a hemerological calendar, or a diagram with captions.

## What survives
The manuscript itself, light-damaged, in the Sackler Gallery, with related papers in the Freer Gallery of Art and Arthur M. Sackler Gallery Archives. Infrared photography has been the principal tool for recovering faded characters. Noel Barnard produced the first English translation and a two-volume study (1973); Li Xueqin and Li Ling are the standard Chinese authorities; Constance Cook and John Major's *Defining Chu* (1999) carries the accessible English treatment. The comparative corpus is the reason this problem is now more tractable than it was in 1973: the excavated Chu bamboo-slip corpora — Baoshan, Guodian, the Shanghai Museum slips, Qinjiazui — have vastly enlarged the attested inventory of Chu graphs since Barnard wrote, and most of them have published transcriptions.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Barnard's transcription and translation established the text; Li Xueqin, Li Ling and Rao Zongyi corrected much of it and fixed the three-section structure; Cook and Major's volume represents the current English-language synthesis. The consensus is that the manuscript is a hemerological and cosmological text — an almanac with a cosmogonic preface, plausibly connected with the *Yueling* tradition of monthly ordinances and with the Chu ritual calendar — and that its overall sense is understood. What is not settled is the reading of a number of graphs, the names and nature of the twelve deities, whether the figures illustrate the text or the text captions the figures, and how much of the faded material can still be recovered. A newcomer reads Cook and Major, then Li Ling, then Barnard for the history of the transcription.

## What a solution would have to do
The realistic target is the graph inventory, and it is now a well-posed comparative problem. A contribution would: (1) publish a fresh transcription keyed to modern multispectral imaging of the object, stating for each character whether the reading comes from the silk, from an early photograph, or from conjecture — a distinction the existing editions blur; (2) for each descendantless graph, search the whole published Chu bamboo-slip and bronze corpus for the form, and report either an attestation with its context or a null result, which would itself be a finding; (3) constrain the twelve deity names by the internal structure, since the twelve entries are formulaic and the calendrical position of each month is known, so a proposed name must be consistent with the month's attributes, the corner-tree colour scheme, and the accompanying figure; (4) resolve the text-image relation by showing that the figure labels and the surrounding text agree or disagree graph by graph; and (5) not lean on the later *Yueling* and *Huainanzi* material as an answer key, since those are centuries later and their agreement is what a reading should predict, not what it should assume.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the text's genre, structure and general content are agreed, and the residue is a set of graphs and names — narrow, but it includes the twelve deities, which is the part everyone wants.
- material 3: a single witness, partly illegible through post-excavation light damage, with 926 characters and no open high-resolution or multispectral imaging located; substantial but access-limited, which is precisely the rubric's 3.
- solvable 4: a purposeful ritual-calendrical text whose graphs stood for determinate Chu words; not 5 because for the deity names the referent may be a local cult figure unattested anywhere else.
- compute 3: graph-matching against the now-large digitized Chu manuscript corpus is a genuine computational task with a clear success criterion, and image processing could recover faded characters; it is not higher because the enabling input — modern imaging of the object — does not appear to exist yet.
- verifiable 3: a proposed identification of a descendantless graph is checkable against every attestation of that form in the Chu corpus, and consensus on such identifications does form in paleography.
- crowding 3: a modest dedicated literature — Barnard, Li Xueqin, Li Ling, Rao Zongyi, Cook and Major — and no systematic re-examination against the post-1990s Chu slip corpora that would now make the difference.
