# Clavis Artis

## What it is
A three-volume illustrated alchemical manuscript in German Gothic cursive, pseudepigraphically attributed to Zoroaster and claiming to be a translation from Arabic made in 1236. Its reputation rests on its pictures: a large body of watercolours of dragons, hermaphrodites, serpents and coupled figures, interleaved with plain pen drawings of laboratory glassware and furnaces. It was almost certainly produced in Germany in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century. Copies are at the Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei in Rome (MSS Verginelli-Rota 15, 16, 17, the fully illustrated set), the Biblioteca Civica Attilio Hortis in Trieste (Ms-2-27, fully illustrated but more roughly executed), the Národní technická knihovna in Prague (C 1175, volumes 1 and 3, partly illustrated), and the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek in Munich (a single-volume unillustrated version). A Weimar copy was destroyed in the Herzogin Anna Amalia Bibliothek fire of 2004.

## What is unsolved
Almost everything, including the basics. Who wrote or compiled it, when and where; whether any Arabic or earlier German source underlies it or the 1236 claim is pure frame; what the watercolours depict, and in particular whether their sequence tracks the text or is an independent cycle; and whether the laboratory drawings record real apparatus in use. The most important unresolved fact is the most mundane one: the German text has never been transcribed or edited, so what the manuscript *says* is not in the literature at all. Its fame is entirely pictorial, circulated through Wikimedia reproductions of the Trieste copy.

## What survives
Four or five copies, listed above, of which the Trieste Ms-2-27 is digitized and widely reproduced on Wikimedia Commons; the Lincei set is the best executed. There is no critical edition, no transcription, no translation, and no scholarly monograph. Bruno Nardini's 1989 volume reproduces the illustrations in colour and translates the prefaces into Italian; Mino Gabriele included images in a 1986 exhibition catalogue. The evidentiary base for any work on it is therefore images of an untranscribed German hand — which is exactly the condition that has become tractable.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is barely a literature. Nardini (1989) and Gabriele (1986) treated the manuscript as an art object and did not edit it. Italian encyclopedic scholarship, on which the current account rests, dates it to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century on the evidence of hand, paper and pictorial style, and regards the Zoroastrian attribution and the 1236 translation date as fictions of the genre. No one has argued for an Arabic Vorlage. There is no consensus on the meaning of the images because no one has published an attempt grounded in the text. This makes the *Clavis Artis* the emptiest field on this list and, given modern handwriting recognition, the one where a first-order result is cheapest.

## What a solution would have to do
Transcribe it. Produce a full diplomatic transcription of at least the Lincei and Trieste copies — German Gothic cursive of this period is within reach of trained handwritten-text-recognition models, and a transcription would be checkable line by line against the images by any reader of German. Then: collate the copies to establish their relation and whether Munich's unillustrated version is an ancestor or a descendant of the illustrated ones; identify the text's sources by alignment against the digitized German and Latin alchemical corpora, which would date the compilation from below and test the 1236 claim; and only then read the images, showing for each plate which passage it illustrates, and reporting the plates that illustrate nothing. A claim that the manuscript translates an Arabic original must produce the Arabic, or a demonstrated Arabism in the German.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: genuinely and almost completely open, but only because nobody has looked; the stakes are inside the history of alchemy and the answer is likely to be "a late German compilation," not a revelation.
- material 3: several complete witnesses exist and one is openly digitized, but there is no transcription, no edition and no translation, and most copies are not imaged — access is the binding constraint.
- solvable 4: unlike a wordless emblem book, this is a text that says determinate things in a readable language; the intended meaning exists and is latent in an untranscribed hand rather than encoded.
- compute 4: handwritten-text recognition on early-modern German cursive plus a source-identification sweep is a ready, well-posed project with a concrete deliverable and no competition; not 5 because only one copy is openly digitized, so imaging must happen first.
- verifiable 4: a transcription is verifiable by any German reader against the facsimile, and a source match is checkable word for word.
- crowding 4: two Italian art-historical treatments and no monograph, edition or transcription; not 5 because the manuscript is at least catalogued and reproduced.

## Unverified claims
- The count of watercolours (often given as around 300 across the three volumes) was not verified and is therefore not asserted above.
- The precise dating argument (hand, paper, pictorial style) is inferred from the encyclopedic dating to the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century; no published codicological description was consulted.
- Nardini's 1989 title and publisher, and Gabriele's 1986 catalogue, were not obtained.
- Whether the Munich copy is complete, and whether any further copies exist beyond the five listed.
- Whether any transcription of any portion of the text has ever been published; none was found, which is the basis of the `compute` and `crowding` scores and should be re-checked before acting on them.
