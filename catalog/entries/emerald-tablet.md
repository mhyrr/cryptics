# Emerald Tablet (Tabula Smaragdina)

## What it is
A short aphoristic text, a page or less, attributed to Hermes Trismegistus and framed as an inscription found on a tablet of emerald. It states a correspondence between what is above and what is below, names sun, moon, wind and earth as father, mother, bearer and nurse of a "one thing," and closes with the *telesmus* and the "operation of the sun." It is the single most quoted text in Latin alchemy and the source of "as above, so below."

## What is unsolved
Everything about its origin and almost everything about its reference. (1) Origin: the oldest recension sits inside the pseudo-Apollonian *Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa* (*Book of the Secret of Creation*), attributed to Balīnūs, dated by Kraus to c. 813–833, by Weisser to c. 750–800, and by Ruska as early as the sixth to eighth centuries. Whether it translates a lost Greek or Syriac original, or was composed in Arabic, is unsettled and there is no Greek witness. (2) Genre: whether it was a cosmogonic text later read as alchemy, a talismanic text (the *telesmus* derives from Arabic *ṭilasm*), or alchemical from the start. (3) Reference: what "the one thing," the *telesmus* and the operation of the sun denote, if anything determinate. (4) Textual history: four Arabic recensions plus the Latin vulgate differ substantially, and no modern critical edition establishes their relations.

## What survives
Four Arabic recensions are known: in *Sirr al-khalīqa* (pseudo-Apollonius/Balīnūs); in Jābir ibn Ḥayyān's *Second Book of the Element of the Foundation* (shorter, with omissions); in the pseudo-Aristotelian *Sirr al-asrār* (tenth century); and in Ibn Umayl's *Kitāb al-māʾ al-waraqī wa-l-arḍ al-najmiyya* (*Book of the Silvery Water and the Starry Earth*). Latin: Hugo of Santalla's translation of *Sirr al-khalīqa* (c. 1145–1151), Philip of Tripoli's *Secretum secretorum* (thirteenth century), and the anonymous twelfth-century "vulgate" that became the basis of all later editions and translations. Julius Ruska's *Tabula Smaragdina: Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der hermetischen Literatur* (Heidelberg, 1926) prints and compares the versions and is still the standard edition; it is a century old and print-only. The Arabic witnesses are manuscript, several unedited.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Ruska (1926) established the Arabic priority and remains the edition of record; E. J. Holmyard's work in the 1920s brought the Arabic sources to Anglophone attention. Paul Kraus's *Jābir ibn Ḥayyān* (1942–43) fixed the Jābirian chronology that most datings depend on; Ursula Weisser's *Das "Buch über das Geheimnis der Schöpfung"* (de Gruyter, 1980) is the critical study of the oldest container text and proposes the earliest date. Martin Plessner and, more recently, Jean-Marc Mandosio have written on the Latin transmission and the vulgate's status. Françoise Hudry proposed a late-antique Latin origin, which has not carried. The field's position: Arabic transmission is certain, a Greek or Syriac antecedent is possible and unproven, and the meaning question is generally set aside as unanswerable in the terms in which readers ask it.

## What a solution would have to do
For origin, the decisive evidence is a text: a Greek or Syriac witness, or a demonstrated verbatim dependence of the tablet's clauses on a datable Greek or Syriac source — a match at the level of phrasing across a translation is checkable and would close the question. Short of that, a claim about origin must produce a stemma of the four Arabic recensions plus the Latin versions that explains every substantive variant by a transmission path, not a preference among readings; the current literature has no such stemma. For genre and reference, a reading must be tested against the tablet's own container: *Sirr al-khalīqa* is a long cosmological and talismanic work, and any claim that the tablet means a chemical operation must explain why its earliest frame does not. A reading that is compatible with all recensions has explained none of the variants; a reading must say which recension it reads and why that one.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: origin, genre and meaning are all open, the text is foundational to the history of alchemy, and a securely identified Greek or Syriac source would be a real event in the history of science.
- material 4: the text is complete, exists in five independent versions, and Ruska printed them; held from 5 because the standard edition is from 1926, is print-only, and several Arabic witnesses are unedited.
- solvable 3: the text was certainly composed to say something, but the field seriously entertains that it is a cosmogonic aphorism whose referents were never specific, and that its alchemical sense is a reception.
- compute 4: a machine collation of the five versions across Arabic and Latin, and a parallel sweep of the tablet's clauses against the digitized Greek Hermetic, alchemical and Syriac corpora, are both well-posed and undone; not 5 because the Arabic manuscripts must first be transcribed.
- verifiable 3: a verbatim source match would be decisive, and a stemma could win consensus, but the "meaning" question would remain a matter of plausibility.
- crowding 2: a century of serious philology by Ruska, Kraus, Weisser, Plessner, Mandosio, plus an enormous popular and occult literature; not 1 because the collation and the corpus sweep have not been done with modern tools.
