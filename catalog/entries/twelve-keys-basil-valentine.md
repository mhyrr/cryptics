# Twelve Keys of Basil Valentine (Zwölf Schlüssel)

## What it is
Twelve short allegorical chapters, each a "key" to the philosophers' stone, each accompanied in the illustrated editions by an emblem — a king and queen with a wolf and a crucible, a dragon and an eagle, a bird devouring a bird, a skeleton on a coffin, a lion swallowing a serpent, a final resurrection. They were first printed in 1599 as part of *Von dem grossen Stein der uhralten Weisen*, under the name of Basilius Valentinus, presented as a fifteenth-century Benedictine canon of St Peter's at Erfurt. The emblems most people know are Matthäus Merian's engravings from the *Musaeum Hermeticum* (Frankfurt, 1625; expanded 1678).

## What is unsolved
Two things, and one of them is unusually tractable for this genre. (1) Authorship: Basil Valentine did not exist — the name appears on no German or Roman monastic roll and in no document before 1600, and the biography and portrait were manufactured after the writings. Johann Thölde (c. 1565–1624), a Thuringian salt-works operator and chymical author, is the standard candidate, credited with at least the first five books published under the name; the Valentine corpus as a whole is now thought to be multi-authored, and which hand wrote the *Twelve Keys* is not established. (2) The operation: what the twelve keys instruct. Unlike most alchemical emblem sequences, this one sits inside a corpus with real, reproducible chemistry — the *Triumphal Chariot of Antimony* (1604) was used as a practical text into the eighteenth century — so the question of whether the keys encode a specific laboratory sequence is a live empirical question rather than only a hermeneutic one.

## What survives
The 1599 German printing and its many successors; the Latin translations from the early seventeenth century; the illustrated *Musaeum Hermeticum* versions of 1625 and 1678 with the Merian plates; and Arthur Edward Waite's English translation in *The Hermetic Museum* (1893), which is freely available and is what most readers use. The whole Valentine corpus was repeatedly reprinted and is largely digitized. Thölde's own signed works — notably *Haligraphia* (1603), on salt manufacture — survive and are the control set for any attribution test. There is no modern critical edition of the *Twelve Keys* and no collation of the 1599 text against the illustrated editions.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
John Maxson Stillman's early twentieth-century work established the negative case: no such person, no pre-1600 mention. Claus Priesner's studies of Thölde (in *Chemie in unserer Zeit* and the German biographical dictionaries) are the scholarly basis of the attribution. Lawrence M. Principe's work on early modern chymistry — *The Aspiring Adept* (Princeton, 1998) and his replication programme with William Newman — is the necessary methodological reading, because it demonstrates that allegorical early-modern recipes can be decoded into reproducible laboratory procedures when the substances are correctly identified, and it is the only approach that has actually converted an alchemical allegory into a checkable result. The consensus: a pseudonym, probably Thölde for part of the corpus, with the antimony chemistry genuine and the allegorical framing a marketing and secrecy device.

## What a solution would have to do
For authorship: a stylometric attribution of the *Twelve Keys* against Thölde's signed works and against the other Valentine books, with controls drawn from the Thuringian chymical print corpus of 1590–1620, reported with a held-out test; plus, ideally, documentary evidence from the Eisleben or Frankfurt book trade. A claim that the corpus is multi-authored must say which books cluster with which. For the operation: follow Principe's method rather than the emblem tradition's. Identify the substances named or implied in each key from the corpus's own explicit chemistry (the antimony, stibnite, and regulus procedures that the *Triumphal Chariot* states openly), then run the sequence in the laboratory and show that the phenomena the keys describe — the colours, the "wolf" devouring the metals, the residues — actually occur. A key that cannot be run is a reading, not a solution; and a reading must cover all twelve, in order, with the emblems accounted for as illustrations of the stated steps.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the authorship is resolved in outline (a pseudonym, probably Thölde) with a real residue over which books are his; the operation is genuinely open but the stakes are inside the history of chemistry.
- material 4: a complete short text with many printings, two illustrated states, a free English translation, and the author-candidate's signed works available as a control; held from 5 by the absence of any critical edition.
- solvable 4: the surrounding corpus contains verifiably real chemistry that later chemists used, which is strong internal evidence that these allegories have determinate laboratory referents; not 5 because the *Twelve Keys* specifically may be the corpus's ornamental front matter.
- compute 4: two well-posed computational moves — a stylometric attribution against Thölde's signed corpus, and a machine alignment of the keys' phrasing against the corpus's explicit procedures — on print sources that are digitized.
- verifiable 4: a chemical reading is testable in a laboratory and a stylometric attribution with held-out validation would be accepted as strong evidence; not 5 because neither yields a single mechanical key.
- crowding 3: Stillman, Priesner, Principe and Newman have all touched it; the replication method exists but has not been applied to the *Twelve Keys* as a sequence.
