# Rosarium Philosophorum

## What it is
An alchemical compilation printed in 1550 as part II of *De Alchimia Opuscula complura veterum philosophorum* (Frankfurt), famous for a sequence of twenty woodcuts with German captions: the mercurial fountain, the naked king and queen meeting, their intercourse in a bath, the shared coffin and the winged soul departing, the dew falling, the resurrection, and the crowned hermaphrodite. The title means "rose garden," in the sense of an anthology, not a prayer-bead rosary. The text itself is largely a cento of quotations from earlier authorities. Several of the woodcuts derive directly from the *Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit* (c. 1410).

## What is unsolved
(1) Composition: who assembled the *Rosarium*, when, and from what — no Latin manuscript predates the 1550 print, and the text's borrowings are only partly identified. (2) The woodcut program: whether the twenty images were designed as a sequence encoding one operation, or assembled from earlier picture cycles and fitted to a compiled text; and what each stage denotes chemically. (3) The image-text relation: the captions are German and the treatise Latin, which raises the question of whether the pictures and the prose were ever meant to be read against one another. (4) Whether the version most readers know — mediated through Jung — is a reading of the object or a reading of Jung.

## What survives
The 1550 Frankfurt printing, complete, in many copies and digitized; reprinted in the *Artis auriferae* collection (Basel, 1572) and reworked by Johann Daniel Mylius (1622), whose versions circulated widely. Many later manuscript copies exist across Europe from the late sixteenth to nineteenth centuries, in Glasgow, the Vatican and elsewhere; an early seventeenth-century partial English translation is attributed to a woman, possibly Jeanne du Port. Joachim Telle's *Rosarium philosophorum: Ein alchemisches Florilegium des Spätmittelalters* (VCH, 1992) is the modern facsimile edition with German translation and commentary and is the necessary primary resource. There is no open machine-readable Latin text of the 1550 print.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Telle (1992) is the scholarly foundation: he established the work's character as a late-medieval *florilegium* — an anthology of excerpts — and began the source identification. Barbara Obrist's *Les débuts de l'imagerie alchimique* (1982) traced the woodcut cycle's descent from earlier German illustrated alchemy, including the *Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit*. C. G. Jung's *Die Psychologie der Übertragung* (1946; *The Psychology of the Transference*, CW 16) reads the twenty woodcuts as a picture of the analytic relationship, and is the reason the images are famous; it is explicitly not an argument about chemistry. The field's position: the *Rosarium* is a compilation whose parts are recoverable, its images are inherited rather than invented, and the "program" that Jungian and esoteric readers treat as a unity may be an artefact of the 1550 printer's assembly.

## What a solution would have to do
This is a source-identification problem with a mechanical check, and that is what makes it attractive. A solution must align the whole Latin text of the 1550 print, sentence by sentence, against the digitized corpus it quotes — Arnald of Villanova, Senior Zadith, Morienus, the *Turba philosophorum*, pseudo-Lull, the *Aurora consurgens* — and report the residue that is not quotation. Only then can anything be said about what "the *Rosarium*" argues, because at present the argument may belong to its sources. For the images: establish the descent of each of the twenty woodcuts from its pictorial antecedent (Obrist's method, completed), and then show whether the compiler *chose* images to match the compiled text, by testing whether the caption of a withheld woodcut can be predicted from the adjacent prose. A chemical reading must name substances and account for all twenty stages including the two deaths and the dew.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: partly resolved in outline — it is a florilegium with inherited images — with a substantial residue about composition, program and referents.
- material 4: a complete printed text, digitized, plus a modern scholarly facsimile with translation and commentary; not 5 because there is no open machine-readable Latin text and no stemma of the manuscript copies.
- solvable 3: the quoted sources meant something determinate, but whether the compilation as such carries a single intended operation is exactly what is in dispute.
- compute 4: a mass text-reuse alignment against the digitized Latin alchemical corpus is the obvious undone move and would transform what can be said; not 5 because much of the source corpus exists only as uncorrected OCR of *Theatrum Chemicum* and similar volumes.
- verifiable 4: a quotation match is checkable word for word, and a pictorial descent is checkable image to image; both would be accepted.
- crowding 3: one scholarly edition, one art-historical monograph, an enormous Jungian and popular literature, and a source alignment nobody has completed.
