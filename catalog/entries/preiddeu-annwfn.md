# Preiddeu Annwfn (The Spoils of Annwfn)

## What it is
Sixty lines of Middle Welsh in eight stanzas of irregular length, each stanza bound by a single rhyme, in the Book of Taliesin (NLW Peniarth MS 2, first quarter of the fourteenth century). A first-person speaker, evidently Taliesin, narrates or alludes to a raid by Arthur on Annwfn, the Otherworld: three shiploads went and seven returned; the warrior Gweir was found imprisoned and singing before the spoils of Annwfn; a cauldron of the Chief of Annwfn, rimmed with pearl, which will not boil a coward's food, was taken; a richly harnessed brindled ox is mentioned; and the speaker claims his poetry comes from the breath of nine maidens who warmed the cauldron. The poem opens and closes with Christian praise of the Lord, and repeatedly reproaches unlearned monks.

## What is unsolved
What happened on the raid, and to what the poem is alluding. The poem does not narrate; it refers, as if to a story the audience knows and we do not. Specifically open: (1) what disaster befell the expedition, since only the ratio of departure to return is given; (2) who Gweir is and why he is imprisoned, and whether he is the Gwair of the Welsh triads; (3) how many caers (Caer Sidi, Caer Pedryfan, Caer Wydyr, Caer Rigor and the rest) are meant and whether they are distinct places or one place under successive kennings; (4) the sense of individual lines, several of which have no agreed construal; (5) whether the poem is an allusive summary of a lost narrative, a bardic self-authorisation in narrative dress, or both. Sarah Higley's reading — that it is "a metaphor of its own making — a poem about the material 'spoils' of poetic composition" — makes the last question the primary one.

## What survives
One copy, in one manuscript, with no analogue and no second witness to check it against; the text is short and at points evidently corrupt. The NLW publishes colour images of Peniarth 2. The edition and translation to work from is Marged Haycock, *Legendary Poems from the Book of Taliesin* (2007); Higley's translation and study and John T. Koch's work supply the main alternatives; Roger Sherman Loomis's Grail-genealogy reading is the historically influential one. Ifor Williams excluded the poem from the authentic Taliesin canon. Linguistic argument places composition around AD 900, but the sources are explicit that there is no firm linguistic evidence putting it before the Gogynfeirdd period, so the date range runs from the late sixth century to the manuscript.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Loomis and the early comparativists read the poem as an ancestor of the Grail quest and as evidence for a Celtic Otherworld-raid archetype; the modern consensus rejects direct-ancestry claims while accepting that recurrent motifs are shared across Welsh and Irish tradition. The present scholarly position is that the poem is deliberately allusive rather than narrative — that its obscurity is a bardic technique aimed at an audience assumed to know the story and at monkish rivals assumed not to. That consensus explains the form without recovering the content. A newcomer should read Haycock's edition, then Higley's study for the self-referential reading, then Koch for the linguistic dating and the historical-Arthurian frame.

## What a solution would have to do
Recover the narrative the poem presupposes, and do it from evidence rather than reconstruction. Concretely: (1) identify Gweir's imprisonment story in an attested Welsh or Irish text — the triads, the *Mabinogi*, the Irish *echtrai* — and show the same configuration (a singing prisoner, a heavy chain, the spoils of the Otherworld) rather than a thematic resemblance; (2) settle the caer-question with a defensible count, and explain the epithets as either separate toponyms or successive kennings on internal grounds; (3) construe all sixty lines, marking emendations, and account for the Christian frame and the anti-monastic invective rather than dismissing them as accretion; (4) explain the "three shiploads / seven returned" arithmetic, which is the only quantitative datum the poem gives and which any narrative reconstruction must fit; (5) if the poem is read as being about poetry rather than about a raid, show that reading covers the concrete details — the ox, the pearl rim, the nine maidens — and not only the frame. With one corrupt copy and no analogue, the ceiling is a reading adopted by the next critical edition, plus, if one is claimed, a source text that a reader can go and check.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the central question is wide open, the poem is one of the two or three most discussed items in early Welsh literature, and it sits on the Arthurian and Grail questions, so a solution would matter well beyond Celtic philology.
- material 2: sixty lines, one unreliable copy, no analogue. The evidence caps what any method can do.
- solvable 3: serious opinion divides between a determinate lost narrative behind the allusions and a deliberately self-referential poem for which there is no narrative to recover.
- compute 2: the useful computational move is a systematic motif and collocation sweep of the Welsh and Irish corpora for the Gweir–prison–cauldron–nine-maidens complex; worth doing and mostly undone, but the target text may not survive.
- verifiable 2: absent a rediscovered source, competing readings would remain; if a source were found, the score would be 4.
- crowding 3: a substantial Arthurian and Celtic-studies literature with several serious editions, but far from exhausted, and the self-referential reading opened new ground recently.

## Unverified claims
- The identification of the caer-names (Caer Sidi, Caer Pedryfan, Caer Wydyr, Caer Rigor) and the count of four to seven caers is from memory of the poem and the criticism; the fetched source does not enumerate them, and the list should be checked against Haycock before being relied on.
- The reproach to unlearned monks and the poem's anti-monastic invective is from memory of the text; the fetched source mentions only the Christian opening and closing.
- The attribution of the "metaphor of its own making" phrase to Higley is via the Wikipedia summary, not her publication.
- Whether Ifor Williams excluded the poem from the authentic canon on stated grounds, or merely omitted it, was not verified.
