# The Publius Enigma (Pink Floyd, 1994)

## What it is
A series of anonymous Usenet posts to alt.music.pink-floyd beginning 11 June 1994, signed "Publius," asserting that Pink Floyd's album *The Division Bell* (released March 1994) contained a deliberate puzzle: "there is a central purpose and a designed solution," and "a unique prize has been secreted" for whoever solved it. Publius promised a demonstration that the claim had the band's sanction, and on 18 July 1994, at the Giants Stadium concert in East Rutherford, New Jersey, the stage lighting briefly spelled out "ENIGMA PUBLIUS." A further on-screen message is reported from the 1994 Earls Court shows and from a television broadcast of the tour. The posts came through the Penet anonymous remailer, which shut down in September 1996, ending them.

## What is unsolved
Whether there was ever a puzzle with a solution. The light-show demonstration establishes that the poster had access to the tour production, i.e. that the campaign was authorised by somebody inside the organisation — which is the only hard fact the case contains. It does not establish that a designed, solvable puzzle existed. Nick Mason said in 2005 that "that was a ploy done by EMI," created by an EMI employee who liked puzzles, and that the intended prize was philanthropic — something on the order of replanting a clear-cut area — rather than an object; David Gilmour in 2002 called it "some silly record company thing." The prize was never awarded and the puzzle was never declared solved. The entry is kept as a calibration case: an enigma with an authorial-seeming warrant, a spectacular verification event, and probably no determinate content.

## What survives
The Usenet posts, which are preserved in Google Groups and in fan mirrors and are the primary text; the album and its artwork, including the Storm Thorgerson metal-heads sleeve and the tour programme; concert video and photographs of the light-show message; and the later band statements in interviews. What does not survive — and may never have existed — is any statement of the puzzle's rules, its scope, or what would count as a solution. This is the material weakness of the case: the putative "clues" are whatever fans have chosen to treat as clues.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Fan communities spent years on the album's lyrics, artwork, running times and the Thorgerson sleeve, producing no accepted solution. The consensus, following Mason's and Gilmour's statements, is that the campaign was an EMI marketing exercise. Whether the EMI employee behind it had actually constructed something solvable is the open point, and it is answerable only by that person's testimony or by internal EMI records; neither has surfaced in thirty years. No serious scholarly treatment exists.

## What a solution would have to do
Before any claimed solution can be assessed, the puzzle's statement has to be recovered, and that is the real task: produce the complete set of authentic Publius posts with headers, from the Usenet archive, and treat only those as the clue set — everything else in circulation is fan accretion. A claimed solution would then have to: (a) derive from clues that appear in the authenticated posts, not from the album at large; (b) be checkable against the promised prize, since Publius specified that a prize existed and Mason described what it was, so a correct solution should connect to something an EMI employee could actually have arranged in 1994; (c) explain the two demonstrations (the Giants Stadium lights, the reported Earls Court or broadcast message) as parts of the design rather than as advertising; and (d) be confirmed by the campaign's author or by EMI documentation, because with a dead remailer, a defunct promotion and no rules, nothing internal to the album can settle it. The honest expected outcome is that the archive recovery succeeds and shows there was never a specified puzzle.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: a working consensus exists — a marketing campaign — with the minority position that a real puzzle was constructed inside it.
- material 3: the posts and the album survive, but the authentic clue set has not been isolated from fan accretion, and the tour footage documenting the light message is patchy.
- solvable 2: the best evidence, including two band members' statements and the never-awarded prize, favours a promotion without a determinate solution.
- compute 2: recovering and authenticating the Usenet thread from archives is a small data task worth doing; there is nothing to decipher.
- verifiable 2: only the campaign's author or EMI records could confirm anything, so any claimed solution rests on plausibility.
- crowding 3: a large fan literature and thirty years of amateur attempts, but no rigorous archival reconstruction and no scholarly treatment; the archive work is genuinely open.

## Unverified claims
- The task brief refers to a light-show message at Earls Court on 20 October 1994 and to an on-screen message during a 1994 US television broadcast. The secondary source consulted documents only the 18 July 1994 East Rutherford display. The Earls Court and broadcast events are therefore reported, not verified.
- The album's release date (March 1994) and the Thorgerson sleeve attribution are from general reference.
- The identity of the EMI employee has never been published; Mason's account is the only evidence that such a person existed.
- Whether the surviving Usenet record is complete, and whether all posts attributed to Publius came through the same remailer account, was not checked.
