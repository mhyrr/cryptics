# Carmen Arvale (the hymn of the Arval Brethren)

## What it is
A short ritual chant of the Fratres Arvales, the Roman priestly college of the fields, preserved because the college's *acta* for the year 218 CE were inscribed on stone and the chant was copied into them. The inscription is dated by its mention of the emperor Elagabalus and the consul Marcus Oclatinius Adventus. It was found in 1778 during construction work at the Vatican; its present location is uncertain, with Arthur E. Gordon suggesting tentatively that it may be the stone in the Sagrestia of St Peter's Basilica. The chant is a prayer for the aid of Mars and the Lares against plague and disaster in the fields. Its language is Old Latin, and the song itself is much older than the stone — possibly fourth century BCE or earlier.

## What is unsolved
Individual words and forms, in a text whose general sense is agreed. Philip Baldi's assessment is the fair one: the general meaning of the text is clear, but interpretations of its details "vary wildly." The disputed items are grammatical and lexical, and each changes what the prayer asks for: whether *sins* is a subjunctive or a future (Baldi 2018 against de Forest Allen 1880); whether *advocapit* is an imperative or a future (Weiss 2017, Allen 1880, Warmington 1940); the meaning of *berber*, which Baldi calls speculative; whether *semunis* is a genuine archaism or an Imperial-period corruption (de Forest Allen 1880). The last is the structural problem for the whole text: the chant reaches us through a third-century stonecutter copying a handwritten exemplar of a text nobody then understood, so any given oddity may be archaic Latin or may be a 218 CE mistake, and there is no second witness to decide.

## What survives
One inscription, containing the full chant, with the text complete rather than fragmentary — which is what distinguishes this entry from the Carmen Saliare. Gordon's observation that the stonecutter must have copied from a handwritten source, producing stylistic variation within the inscription, is the key physical datum: it identifies the class of error to expect. The text is printed in every collection of archaic Latin and in the *acta* of the Arvales. No open high-resolution imaging was found, and because the stone's present whereabouts are not certain, autopsy is not currently a route.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is agreement on purpose and addressees: Mars is invoked as guardian, the Lares are called on, and the prayer asks protection for the fields. Beyond that the readings diverge word by word, and the literature is a long series of proposals on individual forms rather than competing whole translations. Michael Weiss (2017) advanced the understanding of the *Semones*, the divine retinue, arguing that Mars summons them forth. Baldi (2018) is the current linguistic treatment. Zoa Alonso Fernández (2021) reads the inscription's occasion politically, as legitimizing Elagabalus' rule during instability — a claim about why the chant was inscribed in 218, not about what it means. The nineteenth-century treatments (de Forest Allen 1880) are still cited because the evidence has not changed.

## What a solution would have to do
The evidence is one complete but mediated text, so the whole problem is distinguishing archaism from transmission error, and a proposal must do it explicitly. (1) For every disputed form, state whether the reading assumes the stonecutter got it right, and if not, name the error type and show that the same stonecutter made errors of that type elsewhere in the inscription — Gordon's point about the handwritten exemplar makes this checkable within the stone. (2) Derive each form by a stated sound law from a reconstructible Proto-Italic or Old Latin shape, and count the forms the proposal cannot derive; a reading that requires three unattested developments is worse than one that requires none, and the comparison should be made in those terms. (3) Produce a syntax for the whole chant, not a gloss on the words: the disputes over *sins* and *advocapit* are disputes about mood and therefore about what kind of utterance the chant is, and a reading must commit. (4) Test against the comparative Italic ritual material — the Iguvine Tables above all, and the Carmen Saliare fragments — and show that the proposed formulae have parallels. (5) Say what would falsify it. Because there is no second witness, the achievable result is a ranked set of readings with their costs stated in derivations and assumed scribal errors, which is more useful than another translation.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: real and open at the level of the individual forms, and it matters to Latin historical linguistics and to archaic Roman religion; the overall sense is not in dispute, which caps it.
- material 3: a complete text, which is much better than the Carmen Saliare, but one witness, copied three centuries or more after composition by a scribe and a cutter who did not understand it, with no open imaging and an uncertain present location.
- solvable 4: certainly a determinate ritual utterance, publicly performed and deliberately recorded, with its general sense already recovered.
- compute 2: no corpus to sweep and nothing statistical at stake; at most systematic derivation-cost scoring of competing readings against reconstructed Old Latin morphology, which is bookkeeping for a humanistic judgement.
- verifiable 2: competing readings would remain; the internal error-type test gives some traction, which keeps it off 1.
- crowding 3: a modest but continuous literature from 1880 to 2021 (Allen, Warmington, Gordon, Weiss, Baldi, Alonso Fernández), with the obvious approaches tried.
