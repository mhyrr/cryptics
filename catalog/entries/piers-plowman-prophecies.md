# The prophecies in Piers Plowman

## What it is
*Piers Plowman* is a Middle English alliterative dream vision attributed to William Langland, written c. 1370–86 and surviving in three authorial versions — A (c. 1367–70, about 2,500 lines), B (c. 1377–79, about 7,300 lines) and C (1380s) — plus a disputed Z-text found in the 1980s, across 50 to 56 manuscripts, none in the author's hand. Embedded in the poem are several passages in the voice of prophecy, of which the clearest is the close of Passus 6 in the B version. The Piers Plowman Electronic Archive's critical Bx text reads:

> [Bx.6.328] ¶ Ac I warne ȝow werkemen · wynneth while ȝe mowe
> [Bx.6.329] For hunger hideward · hasteth hym faste
> [Bx.6.330] He shal awake with water · wastoures to chaste
> [Bx.6.331] Ar fyue ȝere be fulfilled · suche famyn shal aryse
> [Bx.6.332] Thorwgh flodes and foule wederes · frutes shul faille
> [Bx.6.333] And so sayde saturne · and sent ȝow to warne
> [Bx.6.334] Whan ȝe se þe sonne amys · and two monkes hedes
> [Bx.6.335] And a Mayde haue þe maistrie · and multiplied bi eight
> [Bx.6.336] Þanne shal deth withdrawe · and derthe be iustice
> [Bx.6.337] And dawe þe dyker · deye for hunger
> [Bx.6.338] But if god of his goodnesse · graunt vs a trewe

Lines 334–336 are the crux. Related riddling-prophetic passages occur in Passus 3 (Conscience on the coming of *kynde loue* and the reform of law) and Passus 10 and 15 (the "Dowel" prophecy and the prediction of a king who will discipline the religious orders).

## What is unsolved
What, if anything, lines Bx.6.334–336 designate. "The sun amiss," "two monks' heads," "a maid having the mastery and multiplied by eight" have been read as: heraldic or astrological signs of datable events; a numerological date arrived at by multiplying some value by eight; an allusion to the political prophecy tradition (the Bridlington prophecies, the *Prophetia Merlini* tradition, the sign-lists of the Fifteen Signs before Doomsday); a reference to specific persons; and — the reading this entry judges most likely — a pastiche of the political-prophecy idiom, constructed to sound like a decodable prophecy while being designed not to resolve. The same question attaches to the Passus 3 and Passus 10/15 passages.

The brief for this entry asked for a decision: **is this a real puzzle or open by design?** The decision here is *open by design, with an empirical residue*. The grounds: (1) the passage is spoken by Hunger inside a dream vision, with Saturn as the authority, which places it inside a genre Langland elsewhere satirises; (2) Langland revised heavily between B and C, and a poet with a determinate coded referent has the opportunity to clarify and does not; (3) the political-prophecy genre of the period is itself characterised by studied non-specificity, so imitating it well means producing something undecodable; (4) against all that, the passage does contain numeric and sign-language material ("multiplied bi eight," "two monkes hedes") that reads as though it were meant to be counted, and that residue is what keeps the question open. Hence `status = "contested"` rather than an EXCLUDED row.

## What survives
An unusually strong evidential base: 50–56 manuscripts across three or four versions, with the variation between them itself evidence about what scribes and early readers thought the prophecies meant. Editions of record: the Athlone editions (Kane for A; Kane–Donaldson for B; Russell–Kane for C); A. V. C. Schmidt's parallel-text edition of A, B, C and Z; Derek Pearsall's C-text. The Piers Plowman Electronic Archive (SEENET, lead editor Hoyt N. Duggan; the Bx critical text edited by John Burrow and Thorlac Turville-Petre, SEENET A.12) publishes the critical Bx text and diplomatic editions of B manuscripts F, G, Hm, L, M, O, R and W online and open access, which makes the passage and its variants machine-readable.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The prophecies are discussed within a large critical literature on Langland's apocalypticism rather than as a standalone decipherment problem — which is itself a datum about how the field reads them. The essential frame is Kathryn Kerby-Fulton's work on reformist and Joachite apocalypticism in the poem, which locates Langland's prophetic voice in a tradition of reformist expectation rather than of coded political reference; Morton Bloomfield's *Piers Plowman as a Fourteenth-Century Apocalypse*, Robert Adams on Langland's eschatology, and the commentaries in Schmidt and Pearsall supply the standard notes on the individual lines. No decoding of Bx.6.334–336 has been accepted. The working scholarly position is that the passage participates in the contemporary political-prophecy idiom without being a cipher — i.e. close to the "open by design" verdict above — but the notes in the standard editions still record proposed referents, so the question is not formally closed.

## What a solution would have to do
A decoding claim has to clear a bar the material makes unusually explicit. (1) It must work on the Bx critical text, with the variants: if a reading depends on a word-form found in only some manuscripts, it must argue that form is authorial, and the PPEA apparatus makes that checkable. (2) It must survive the B-to-C revision: either the referent is present in C too, or the proposal must explain the removal. (3) It must cover all three lines, including "two monkes hedes," which most proposals skip, and must say what operation "multiplied bi eight" denotes, on what operand, yielding what. (4) It must be tested against the contemporary political-prophecy corpus — the Bridlington prophecies, the Merlin prophecies, the *Prophetiae* of the Galfridian tradition, the sign-lists — to show that the specific signs Langland uses are *not* stock, because if they are stock the decoding collapses into genre imitation. This is a concrete corpus task and the decisive one: the Middle English and Anglo-Latin prophecy corpora are largely digitized, and a motif census would establish how much of Bx.6.334–336 is formulaic. (5) A claim of numerological dating must specify the calendar, the epoch and the arithmetic, and must not be able to generate a plausible date for arbitrary inputs.

Conversely, the "open by design" verdict is also falsifiable, and by the same census: if the signs turn out to be non-stock and to cluster on one datable configuration, it fails.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: a working consensus exists — the prophecies are read as participating in a genre rather than encoding a referent — with minority dissent recorded in the standard notes. The passages are not a famous open puzzle in the way the Dante prophecies are.
- material 5: 50–56 manuscripts, four versions, three critical editions, an open-access electronic archive with critical and diplomatic texts. The evidence is as good as it gets for a Middle English poem.
- solvable 2: the best evidence favours deliberate openness — genre imitation inside a dream vision, unrevised across a heavy revision — which is the rubric's definition of 2.
- compute 3: the motif census against the digitized prophecy corpora is a real, tractable and mostly undone contribution that would settle the stock-versus-specific question; it cannot decide what Langland meant.
- verifiable 2: only by plausibility, unless the census comes back with a sharp negative; competing readings would otherwise remain.
- crowding 2: a very large Langland literature, but little of it treats these lines as a cipher, so the specific question is less worked over than the poem's overall bibliography suggests. Not 3 because the standard commentaries have all had a go at the lines.
