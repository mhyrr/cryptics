# Solomon and Saturn: the rune-letter Pater Noster and the Vasa Mortis

## What it is
An umbrella title for four Old English texts of wisdom dialogue between Solomon and a figure called Saturn: the Prose *Solomon and Saturn* in the Nowell Codex, *Solomon and Saturn I*, *Solomon and Saturn II*, and the Pater Noster poem. The poetic texts survive in two Cambridge manuscripts, Corpus Christi College MSS 41 and 422; *Solomon and Saturn I* appears in both. Dating is contested: Patrick O'Neill argues for a connection with the circle of Alfred the Great (871–899), while Daniel Anlezark places the work at Dunstan's Glastonbury in the mid-tenth century, and the texts are agreed to be very hard to date. The poems are routinely described as among the most enigmatic and difficult in the Old English corpus.

## What is unsolved
Two distinct problems, plus a general one. (1) **The rune-letter Pater Noster.** *Solomon and Saturn I* and the related Pater Noster poem treat the letters of the Latin *Pater noster* as individual agents with physical and martial powers, and the manuscripts write the letters as a mixture of roman capitals and Anglo-Saxon runes. What governs the choice between roman letter and rune, and what determines the powers ascribed to each letter, is not explained: the ascriptions do not follow the rune-names in any straightforward way, nor Latin letter-name traditions, nor any known alphabet-mysticism source that has been identified. (2) **The Vasa Mortis.** *Solomon and Saturn II* describes a mysterious bound bird or creature called the *Vasa Mortis*, imprisoned by Solomon until Doomsday. Robert Menner identified it with the demon Asmodeus from Jewish legend; Cilluffo argues instead for Virgilian and Anglo-Saxon monster analogues; there is no consensus. (3) **The riddles generally.** The dialogues contain further riddling exchanges that the editions describe as still lacking definitive explanation.

## What survives
CCCC 41 and CCCC 422 (the latter the "Red Book of Darley"), plus the Nowell Codex for the prose text. The manuscript witnesses matter here more than usual, because the rune-and-letter forms are the evidence: any argument about the Pater Noster poem's letter-agents depends on what the scribes actually wrote, and CCCC 41 preserves *Solomon and Saturn I* as marginalia, which affects how much weight its forms can bear. Editions: Robert J. Menner, *The Poetical Dialogues of Solomon and Saturn* (1941), long the standard; Daniel Anlezark, *The Old English Dialogues of Solomon and Saturn*, Anglo-Saxon Texts (D. S. Brewer, 2009), now the edition of record with translation. Patrick O'Neill has published on the sources and the Alfredian connection. No open digital facsimile of either Corpus manuscript was located, though the Parker Library's digitization programme makes one likely to exist.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The texts moved from curiosity to serious study through Menner, and Anlezark's 2009 edition put them on a modern footing. Consensus: the dialogues belong to a European tradition of Solomonic wisdom dialogue with Eastern and Jewish-legendary components; the letter-magic of the Pater Noster poem is genuinely magical or quasi-liturgical in intent rather than decorative; and neither the letter-power scheme nor the *Vasa Mortis* has an accepted source. The dating dispute between an Alfredian and a Glastonbury milieu is live and matters because it changes which sources the poet could have known.

## What a solution would have to do
The letter-power scheme is the tractable target and the one this entry is built around. (1) **Tabulate the evidence from the manuscripts, not from an edition's normalisation:** for each letter of the *Pater noster* as it appears in each witness, record the graphic form (roman or runic, and which rune), the property ascribed to it, and the order of presentation. Differences between CCCC 41 and CCCC 422 are data, not noise. (2) **Test the obvious hypotheses against that table, each with a chance baseline:** that the roman/runic choice tracks the rune-names' semantics; that it tracks which Latin letters have no runic equivalent; that the ascribed powers derive from rune-names, from the *Old English Rune Poem*, from Latin letter-name lists, from Hebrew letter-mysticism as transmitted through Jerome or Isidore, or from the numerical values of the letters. With twenty-odd letters, a proposed correspondence will fit some letters by accident, so the requirement is that a proposal explain a clear majority and that the fit be measured. (3) **Find the source or demonstrate there isn't one.** The Latin, Greek and Hebrew alphabet-mysticism traditions available in tenth-century England are finite and largely digitized; a systematic sweep of them for letter-power ascriptions is a concrete, checkable piece of work that would either identify the source or establish that the scheme is an English invention — either result is publishable and neither has been achieved. (4) For the *Vasa Mortis*, a source claim must match more than the motif of a bound demon: it must account for the name, the bird form, the binding until Doomsday and the geographical detail the poem gives. (5) Any reading must handle the dating question, because a source available at Glastonbury in 950 may not have been available at Alfred's court in 890, and vice versa.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: real, unresolved and acknowledged in the standard editions, but confined to Old English studies and concerning a scheme inside the poems rather than the poems' overall sense.
- material 3: two manuscript witnesses for the poetry plus a prose relative, a modern critical edition with translation, and a text of usable length — but the poems are damaged and partly marginal, and no open facsimile was located.
- solvable 4: a poet who assigns a specific power to each specific letter and chooses runic forms for some of them was following a scheme; schemes have sources or rules. Not 5 because the rule may be the poet's own and only partly systematic.
- compute 3: the correspondence testing and the source sweep across digitized Latin and Greek alphabet-mysticism corpora are tractable and undone; but a twenty-letter table is small, so results will be suggestive rather than decisive.
- verifiable 3: identifying a source text with matching letter-ascriptions would be recognised at once; absent that, consensus would form slowly around the best-fitting scheme.
- crowding 4: two critical editions and a handful of source studies (Menner, O'Neill, Anlezark, Cilluffo) in ninety years; the letter-by-letter work described above appears not to exist.
