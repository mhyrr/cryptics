# Bach, The Art of Fugue BWV 1080 — the unfinished Contrapunctus XIV

## What it is
A cycle of fourteen fugues and four canons in D minor, all derived from one subject, assembled by Bach from about 1740 and worked on intensively from 1747 until his death in July 1750. The earliest source is the autograph Mus. ms. autogr. P 200 in the Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin; the first print was issued by C. P. E. Bach in May 1751, with a second issue in 1752 carrying added prefatory matter. The last piece, headed *Fuga a 3 Soggetti* and conventionally numbered Contrapunctus XIV, breaks off at bar 239 in the middle of a phrase. C. P. E. Bach wrote on the manuscript that "upon this fugue, where the name BACH… was placed in the countersubject, the composer died."

## What is unsolved
Two linked questions. First, textual: was the fugue left incomplete by Bach's death, or did it once have an ending now lost — a distinct hypothesis, since the surviving leaves are a fair copy and the 1751 print's editorial history is messy. Second, compositional: what was the plan? The three subjects exposed by bar 239 do not include the cycle's own principal theme, and Gustav Nottebohm showed in 1881 that that theme can be combined contrapuntally with all three, which is the basis of the near-universal view that Bach intended a quadruple fugue crowning the whole cycle. What the quadruple combination was, and how the movement was to be laid out and closed, is not known. A third, softer question is the intended order of the pieces, which differs between the autograph and the print.

## What survives
The autograph P 200 (dated by scholars to roughly 1740–1746 for its core, with the late fugue's leaves from 1748–1749) and the 1751/1752 print both survive and are reproduced in facsimile; the Neue Bach-Ausgabe provides the critical text, and public-domain scores including the fragment are on IMSLP in machine-readable form. Because the material is a fully notated, fully digitized score, the problem is not access but inference: everything that exists is known, and the missing object is a continuation that was either never written or has been lost.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Nottebohm (1881) established the contrapuntal compatibility of the four subjects. Donald Tovey's completion (1931) is the classic musicological attempt; there are well over eighty published completions, among them Helmut Walcha's, Davitt Moroney's (1989), Zoltán Göncz's (from 1992), Thomas Daniel's (2010), Kalevi Aho's (2011) and Daniil Trifonov's (2021). Göncz's is the most methodologically distinctive: he argues the quadruple combination is constrained by a permutation matrix implicit in Bach's practice, which narrows the possible voice orderings rather than inventing them. Christoph Wolff's argument that a lost final section once existed — that Bach worked out the quadruple combination on a separate sheet now missing — is the leading alternative to the "died mid-bar" story, and Gregory Butler's work on the print's engraving history bears on it. The field's position is that the quadruple-fugue plan is very probably right, that the specific realization is unrecoverable, and that C. P. E. Bach's romantic note is not reliable evidence about how the manuscript came to stop.

## What a solution would have to do
Distinguish the two claims and test them separately. (1) For the codicological claim, a demonstration would need paper, watermark, rastrology, gathering-structure and ink evidence from P 200 and from the 1751 engraved plates showing either that the surviving leaves are a complete unit (supporting "unfinished") or that a leaf or gathering is missing (supporting "lost ending"), together with an account of the 1751 print's editorial decisions. This claim is decidable in principle from the physical objects. (2) For the compositional claim, a proposed reconstruction must (a) state the exact quadruple combination — subject order, intervals of entry, key — and show it is contrapuntally correct without alteration of any subject; (b) show the combination is forced or near-forced by Bach's own constraints, in the manner of Göncz's matrix, rather than merely possible, since "possible" admits many solutions; (c) match measurable features of Bach's late style (voice-leading statistics, cadence types, proportions of the other Contrapuncti); and (d) account for the fragment's own trajectory at bar 239, which already commits to a harmonic and registral position. A completion that sounds convincing is not a result; a demonstration that only a small number of combinations are admissible, and that the fragment points to one of them, would be.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: resolved in outline — nearly everyone accepts the quadruple-fugue plan — with a large and genuine residue about the realization and about how the manuscript stops.
- material 4: autograph and first print both survive, are edited and digitized; not 5 because the object that would answer the question is precisely what is absent.
- solvable 4: Nottebohm's demonstration plus the cycle's evident architectural logic are strong internal evidence of a determinate plan; not 5 because Bach never stated it.
- compute 4: the admissible-combination question is a constraint-satisfaction problem over a small, exactly specified space, and stylistic scoring against Bach's own corpus is a well-posed machine task; not 5 because the answer cannot be checked against Bach.
- verifiable 3: the codicological sub-question could be settled and a consensus could form around a forced combination; no completion can ever be verified as Bach's.
- crowding 1: eighty-plus completions, a dedicated musicological literature, and every obvious approach attempted.
