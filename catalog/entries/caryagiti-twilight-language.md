# Caryāgīti / Charyapada — the twilight language (sandhyābhāṣā)

## What it is
A collection of tantric songs of realization from the Vajrayāna Buddhist milieu of northeastern India, composed by a group of *siddha* poets — Kāṇha, Lui, Bhusuku, Sarahapā, Tantripā and others — and usually dated between roughly the eighth and the twelfth century. They are the earliest substantial verse in any eastern Indo-Aryan vernacular. Haraprasad Shastri found the palm-leaf manuscript in the royal library in Nepal and published it in 1916 as *Hājār bacharer purāno bāṅgālā bhāṣāy bauddhagān o dohā*. The songs are sung in what Shastri, following the Sanskrit commentator Munidatta, called *sandhyā-bhāṣā*, "twilight language," also *ālo-āndhāri*, half-revealed and half-concealed: a register in which boatmen, crocodiles, elephants, weddings, wine, low-caste women and the mechanics of rivers and huts stand for something else.

## What is unsolved
Whether the coded register has a determinate key, and if so whose. Munidatta's Sanskrit commentary, the *Caryāgītikoṣavṛtti*, supplies a systematic decoding of the imagery in yogic and doctrinal terms. The question is whether that decoding recovers what the *siddha* poets meant or imposes a later scholastic grid on songs whose polyvalence was the point. Underneath it sits a second unresolved layer: the surface text itself, since the language is a transitional Indo-Aryan vernacular whose grammar is reconstructed rather than known, and many individual words remain contested.

## What survives
The Nepalese palm-leaf manuscript, incomplete and damaged, containing 47 verses with Munidatta's commentary; it is held in the National Archives of Nepal. The Tibetan canon preserves a translation of 50 *padas*, attributed to the twelfth-century Vairocanavajra, including three absent from the Sanskrit manuscript — among them *pada* 25 by Tantripāda, recovered from the Tibetan. Munidatta's commentary was itself translated into Tibetan (attributed to Kīrticandra or Candrakīrti). Editions and studies: Shastri's 1916 *editio princeps*; Prabodh Chandra Bagchi's edition incorporating the Tibetan; Sukumar Sen, *Caryāgīti-padāvalī* (1995); Muhammad Shahidullah's linguistic work; Shashibhusan Dasgupta, *Obscure Religious Cults*, for the religious context; and Per Kværne, *An Anthology of Buddhist Tantric Songs: A Study of the Caryāgīti*, the standard critical study in English. No open digitization of the manuscript was located.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The literature splits along two seams. The first is the language question, which is a nationalist as much as a philological dispute: Bengali scholars read the songs as Old Bengali, Assamese scholars find Assamese features, Odia scholars early Odia, and K. P. Jayaswal argued for early Maithili; the sober position is that the language is late Apabhraṃśa or an Abahaṭṭha dialect from the period before the eastern languages had separated. Dating is likewise split — Shahidullah argued 7th–11th century, Rahul Sankrityayan 8th–11th, with 1000–1200 the commonly cited compilation range. The second seam is the interpretive one: Dasgupta and the Indian tradition broadly follow Munidatta's decoding; Kværne's study treats the songs' language as functioning at several levels at once and is correspondingly cautious about a single key; Vidhushekhara Shastri preferred to call the register "intentional language," which shifts the emphasis from concealment to deliberate multivalence. No consensus exists on whether a determinate decoding is recoverable. A newcomer reads Kværne, then Dasgupta, then Shahidullah on the language.

## What a solution would have to do
This entry is admitted because a determinate decoding is at stake — Munidatta claims one and the tradition transmits it — but it is admitted with the warning that the answer may be "the key is real but not the poets'." A contribution would have to: (1) build a concordance of the coded lexicon across all 50 *padas* and test whether each image is used consistently, since a code with a fixed key is internally consistent and an idiom is not; (2) extend that test across the wider corpus of tantric coded vocabulary — the *Hevajra Tantra*'s *chomā* lists, the *Dohākoṣa*, the *Caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa* material — and ask whether the Caryāgīti's usage matches the technical registers documented there; (3) use the Tibetan translation as an independent witness, since the translators had to commit to a reading and their choices reveal what a twelfth-century reader thought the words meant; (4) show where Munidatta's decodings are forced, by finding songs whose surface grammar resists his reading; and (5) separate the two layers cleanly, because a claim about the hidden meaning that rests on a contested reading of the surface text is worth nothing. The wall is nameable: if the internal consistency test fails, the honest conclusion is that the register is intentionally polyvalent and the entry should move toward `open-by-design`.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the surface text is edited and translated and the religious context is well described; the live dispute is whether the allegorical key is original, which is central but narrow.
- material 3: one damaged manuscript with 47 of 50 songs, plus a Tibetan translation and a commentary — substantial, but with no open digitization and no modern critical edition that supersedes the others.
- solvable 3: serious positions include both a systematic code with a recoverable key (Munidatta, Dasgupta) and deliberate multivalence that no single decoding captures (Kværne, Vidhushekhara Shastri).
- compute 2: the consistency test described above is genuinely computational, but the corpora — Apabhraṃśa verse, Sanskrit tantric commentary, Tibetan translations — are poorly digitized and unaligned, so computation is a minor aid at present. If the texts were digitized this would be a 4.
- verifiable 2: competing readings would remain; Munidatta and the Tibetan translators are checks but not arbiters, and both are later than the songs.
- crowding 3: a real but small literature, most of it in Bengali and most of it a century old, with Kværne the only dedicated Western monograph; the language dispute has absorbed far more effort than the decoding question.
