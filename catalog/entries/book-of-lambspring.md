# Book of Lambspring (De lapide philosophico)

## What it is
A short German alchemical poem in fifteen emblematic figures, each a woodcut or engraving with a didactic verse, preceded by a preface of some fifty-two lines. The figures run through a bestiary of the work: two fishes in the sea, a salamander in fire, a unicorn and a stag in a forest, two lions, two birds, a wolf and a dog, a dragon devouring itself, a king and his son, and a final ascent. It circulated under the name "Abraham Lambspring," about whom nothing whatever is known. The manuscript version dates from 1556; the first printing was in Nicolas Barnaud's *Triga chemica: de lapide philosophico tractatus tres* (1599), without illustrations, and the engraved version that everyone reproduces first appeared in Lucas Jennis's *Musaeum Hermeticum* material of 1625, cut by Matthäus Merian the Elder.

## What is unsolved
Who wrote it, and what the fifteen figures denote. The name "Abraham Lambspring" is unattached to any documented person; it has been read as a reference to the Benedictine house at Lamspringe and as a pun on a leaping lamb. Because Lamspringe was at one period a women's house, a female author has been proposed. The figures themselves are the harder problem: the verses are readable and their surface sense — coagulation, dissolution, conjunction, death and revival of the king — is agreed in outline, but which substances and operations the animals stand for is not, and the figure order differs in its relation to the verses across witnesses and printings.

## What survives
A 1556 illustrated German manuscript is the earliest witness; handwritten copies survive at Nuremberg, Zurich and Leiden. Printings: Barnaud's *Triga chemica*, 1599 (text only); the Jennis/Merian illustrated edition, 1625; a 1678 reissue; and Arthur Edward Waite's English translation in *The Hermetic Museum* (1893), which is the version most readers use and is freely available. There is no critical edition, no collation of the manuscript witnesses against the 1599 text, and no scholarly translation. Katja Lehnert's 2023 Frankfurt dissertation is reported to examine the manuscript-to-print transition, which would be the first modern textual work on the poem.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The literature is thin. Joachim Telle's articles in the standard German literary reference works (*Verfasserlexikon*) give the transmission and are the scholarly baseline. Lehnert (2023) is the only recent dedicated study located. Otherwise the poem lives in the reprint and esoteric traditions descending from Waite, in Adam McLean's commentaries, and in Jungian usage of the individual figures. The field has no position on authorship beyond "unknown," and no consensus reading of the figures; the outline allegory is taken as read and the specifics are treated as unrecoverable.

## What a solution would have to do
For the text: collate the 1556 manuscript, the Nuremberg, Zurich and Leiden copies, and the 1599 print, and establish which verses and which figure order are original — since the engraved figures everyone reads are from 1625, sixty-nine years after the manuscript, any iconographic reading must first show it is reading the 1556 figures and not Merian's. For authorship: a documentary link, or a dialectal and source analysis that locates the poem's composition; "Lamspringe" is a guess from the name and is not evidence. For the figures: identify the source of each animal motif in the earlier German and Latin alchemical corpora — most of this bestiary is inherited, and a reading that treats the salamander as Lambspring's invention has skipped the transmission. A chemical reading must name substances, be consistent with mid-sixteenth-century German practice, and cover all fifteen figures plus the preface.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: resolved in outline as a conventional death-and-rebirth allegory, with a real residue about authorship, the figures' referents, and the manuscript-to-print relation; the stakes are small.
- material 4: a short complete text with an early manuscript, three manuscript copies, three early printings and a free English translation; held from 5 by the absence of any critical edition or collation.
- solvable 3: the verses are determinate German; whether the fifteen figures encode a specific operation is genuinely open, and the inherited character of the imagery cuts against it.
- compute 3: the corpus is tiny, so the computational payoff is modest — a collation of four or five witnesses and a motif-source sweep are feasible and worth doing, but neither is a large undertaking or a decisive one.
- verifiable 2: authorship is probably unrecoverable, and an emblem reading would settle only by plausibility; a collation result is checkable but answers a narrow question.
- crowding 4: essentially two scholarly treatments (Telle's lexicon articles, Lehnert 2023); no monograph, and the collation is untouched.

## Unverified claims
- The date 1556 for the manuscript and the 52-line preface come from German encyclopedic report and were not checked against a witness.
- The shelfmarks of the Nuremberg, Zurich and Leiden copies were not obtained.
- Lehnert's 2023 dissertation is reported encyclopedically; its title, institution and findings were not verified.
- Whether any 1556-state illustrations survive, or whether the Merian engravings are the earliest images — the German account implies figures existed in the manuscript but this was not confirmed.
- The suggestion of a female author is reported as speculation arising from the Lamspringe convent, not as an argument anyone has made in print.
