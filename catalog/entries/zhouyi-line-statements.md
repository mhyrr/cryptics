# Zhouyi line statements — the original pre-commentary meaning

## What it is
The base text of the *Yijing*, stripped of the *Ten Wings* commentaries that were appended to it by the Han and that have governed how it was read ever since. It consists of 64 hexagrams, each with a name (*guàmíng*), a short hexagram statement (*tuàn* / *guàcí*), and six line statements (*yáocí*) — 384 line statements in all. The line statements are the hard part: terse, unglossed fragments in the omen idiom of Western Zhou divination. "The wild goose approaches the shore; the lesser son is in danger; there is small talk; no misfortune." "A wineskin is used for the ritual; the tortoise does not oppose." Some are rhymed, some are formulaic, some appear to be fragments of proverb or song, and some are unparseable without a guess.

## What is unsolved
What the line statements meant to the diviners who used them, before the *Ten Wings* turned the book into a cosmology of yin and yang. The historicist school holds that there is a recoverable Western Zhou sense: omen language with real referents (weather, warfare, sacrifice, captives, marriage, hunting), organized by rhyme and by formula, which the later tradition overwrote. The counter-position is that the text is a heterogeneous accretion — divination records, proverbs, fragments of verse, mnemonic tags — with no single authorial intent to recover, so that "the original meaning" is partly an ill-posed question. Where any given line falls between those is exactly what is disputed.

## What survives
The received text complete, freely machine-readable, plus three pre-Han manuscript witnesses that are the reason this is a live problem rather than a dead one: the Shanghai Museum bamboo-slip *Zhou yi* (c. 300 BCE), which shows the text already in essentially its received form and in the King Wen sequence; the Mawangdui silk manuscript (168 BCE), with a different hexagram arrangement and substantial variant readings; and the Fuyang fragments. Every variant character in these is evidence about the base layer. Surrounding it is one of the best comparative corpora for any ancient text: the *Shijing* for Old Chinese rhyme, the Western Zhou bronze inscriptions, and the oracle-bone divination formulae, all with published transcriptions. Richard Kunst's 1985 Berkeley dissertation supplied a concordance and a philological reading of the original text; Richard Rutt, *Zhouyi: The Book of Changes* (Curzon, 1996) is the accessible historicist translation; Edward Shaughnessy's *I Ching: The Classic of Changes* (1996) and *Unearthing the Changes* (Columbia, 2014) are the standard treatments of the manuscripts.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The modernist reading began in China — Li Jingchi, Gao Heng, Wen Yiduo — and entered Western sinology through Arthur Waley's 1933 paper and then Kunst, Rutt, Shaughnessy, Whincup and Field. Its method is to read the line statements as omen texts, using rhyme to recover phrasing, the *Shijing* and bronze inscriptions for vocabulary, and the excavated manuscripts to control the received characters. Its results are uneven: for some lines the historicist reading is now standard even in non-specialist translations (the "captive" and "arrowhead" readings, the military and sacrificial vocabulary), for others it produces as many candidate senses as there are scholars. The field agrees on date range (10th–4th century BCE for assembly, with Shaughnessy narrowing composition to the last quarter of the 9th century), on the priority of the base text over the *Wings*, and on the relevance of the manuscripts. It does not agree that a coherent original reading exists to be had. For a newcomer: Rutt for the translation, Shaughnessy's *Unearthing the Changes* for the manuscripts, Kunst for the concordance method.

## What a solution would have to do
The question this entry poses — is the original meaning of the line statements a determinate puzzle? — is itself the thing a solution must settle, and it can be settled empirically rather than by argument. A serious effort would: (1) test whether the 384 line statements share a formulaic grammar, by extracting recurrent formulae and their slots across the whole set and measuring how much of the corpus they cover, against a null model built from comparable omen corpora; (2) reconstruct the rhyme scheme line by line using Old Chinese rhyme categories from the *Shijing*, and report what fraction of statements rhyme, since a rhymed corpus is a composed one and an unrhymed heap is not; (3) collate every variant in the Shanghai Museum, Mawangdui and Fuyang witnesses and show which disputed readings the manuscripts actually decide; (4) for each proposed sense of a crux term, produce every attestation of that graph in the oracle-bone, bronze and *Shijing* corpora and show the proposed sense fits all of them; and (5) state, at the end, the fraction of the 384 for which a Western Zhou sense is supported, the fraction that is formulaic filler, and the fraction that is irrecoverable — a number, not a posture. A translation that reads well and cites nothing is not a contribution; there are a hundred of those.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: resolved in outline — the base text is pre-commentarial divination material of Western Zhou date — with a central, genuine residue about whether its line-level sense is recoverable; a convincing answer would matter to sinology and to the history of divination, not much beyond.
- material 5: the strongest material case among the non-Western entries: a complete text, three pre-Han manuscript witnesses with published transcriptions, full digitization, and a large digitized comparative corpus of contemporary language.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both "there is a recoverable Western Zhou meaning" (Kunst, Rutt, Shaughnessy) and "the text is an accretion with no single intent," which makes the question partly ill-posed rather than merely hard.
- compute 4: formula extraction, rhyme reconstruction, and cross-corpus collocation against the *Shijing*, bronzes and oracle bones are computational to their core and the corpora are digitized; not 5 because no single computation decides the interpretive question, only the structural ones underneath it.
- verifiable 3: consensus does form on individual readings when the comparative evidence is strong, and the excavated manuscripts provide an external check that has already settled some disputes.
- crowding 2: the overall *Yijing* literature is among the largest attached to any book, and capable people have failed at exactly this question; the specifically corpus-computational corner is close to empty, which is why `compute` is high and `crowding` is not.
