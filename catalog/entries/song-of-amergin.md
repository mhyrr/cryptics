# The Song of Amergin

## What it is
A short incantatory poem attributed to Amergin Glúingel, the poet-judge of the Milesians, embedded in *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, the eleventh-century Irish pseudo-historical compilation of Ireland's settlements. In the narrative Amergin chants it as the Milesians land, to still a druidic storm raised against them and bring the ships ashore. The poem is a sequence of first-person identifications — in Lady Gregory's 1904 rendering, "I am the wind on the sea," and so on through natural and martial images — closing in most versions with a series of questions about who knows the secrets of the land. Two or three further short poems in the same voice occur nearby in the text.

## What is unsolved
What the poem says. The verse is in archaic, highly compressed Irish, transmitted through five recensions of *Lebor Gabála* across more than a dozen manuscripts, and the recensions do not agree with each other. Neither the word-division nor the sense of several lines is settled; the standard English versions in circulation, including Gregory's and Macalister's, differ so widely that they do not describe the same utterance, and the most familiar version — Robert Graves's in *The White Goddess* — is a reconstruction on a calendrical-alphabetic theory rather than a translation. Open questions: (1) the correct text, i.e. which recension's readings are primary and which are scribal repair of an already unintelligible archaism; (2) the literal sense of the identification lines and of the closing interrogatives; (3) whether the identifications constitute a cosmological claim, a poet's authority-formula, a riddle-sequence with answers, or a shape-shifting boast of the kind found in the Taliesin poems, with which the *Lebor Gabála* poems have long been compared; (4) the poem's date, which must be argued from its language and not from the narrative it sits in.

## What survives
The text exists only inside *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, which survives in more than a dozen medieval manuscripts across five recensions: R¹ (Book of Leinster, c. 1150; Book of Fermoy, 1373), R² (seven texts including the Great Book of Lecan, 1418), R³ (Book of Ballymote, 1391; Great Book of Lecan), the Míniugud group, and O'Clery's redaction of 1631. The language of the compilation is Middle Irish; the embedded verse is older in places. The standard edition and translation is R. A. Stewart Macalister, *Lebor Gabála Érenn*, 5 vols, Irish Texts Society (1938–56), which supplies the critical apparatus and remains the edition of record — though Macalister's judgement on the work ("not a single element of genuine historical detail") indicates how he approached it, and his translations of the archaic verse are treated with caution. No open critical digitization of the poem specifically was located.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is no scholarly consensus, and — importantly for scoring — there is very little scholarly attention to the poem as a textual problem, as opposed to a great deal of popular attention. The serious framework is: the *Lebor Gabála* verse is edited in Macalister; the poem belongs with the *roscad*/*rosc* tradition of archaic Irish rhetorical verse whose interpretation is notoriously unsettled; and the comparison with the first-person boast poems attributed to Taliesin in the Book of Taliesin is a recognised parallel rather than a demonstrated relationship. Graves's reading — that the poem is a calendar poem keyed to an alphabet of trees, the same apparatus he applied to *Cad Goddeu* — has no standing in Celtic studies and should be treated as a claimant, not a source. The many versions on the internet descend from Gregory, Macalister or Graves, usually without saying which.

## What a solution would have to do
Begin with the text, because everything published in English skips this step. (1) Collate the poem across the five recensions and publish a stemma and an apparatus for the poem itself, stating for each line which reading is primary and why; this has to be a philological result, not a preference. (2) Date the language of the verse on its own evidence — verbal morphology, the state of the neutral vowels, the presence or absence of Middle Irish features — independently of the eleventh-century frame. (3) Construe every line, marking emendations, and give the syntax of the identification formula, which is where the translations diverge most. (4) Decide the genre on formal grounds: if the closing lines are interrogatives expecting answers, the poem is a riddle-sequence and the answers must be proposed; if they are rhetorical, that must be shown from the syntax. (5) Any claim of an encoded alphabet or calendar must work on the manuscript line order in Irish, cover every line, and be shown to occur above chance against a null model — not demonstrated on a rearranged English paraphrase, which is what Graves did. Verification is by adoption in a new critical edition of the poem; there is no external control.
