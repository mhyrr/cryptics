# Ripley Scrolls

## What it is
A family of large illustrated alchemical rolls — several metres long — carrying a vertical sequence of emblematic figures (a bearded adept holding a flask of homunculi, a toad, a dragon, a tree, nested vessels, a sun-and-moon bird) interleaved with Middle English verses. Roughly 23 copies are known, all descendants of a lost fifteenth-century original, produced between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. They are named for the English canon George Ripley (c. 1415–1490) because some copies carry verse associated with him, but there is no evidence Ripley designed them.

## What is unsolved
Three distinct questions, often conflated. (1) The archetype: who composed the original roll, when, and what it contained, given that no two surviving copies agree. (2) The referents of the figures: whether the emblem sequence encodes a specific laboratory sequence, a cosmological scheme, or a devotional narrative. (3) The relation of image to verse: the verses are readable English and their sense is largely recoverable, but which verse belongs to which figure varies by copy, so the pairing itself is part of the puzzle rather than a given.

## What survives
Copies include British Library Add. MS 5025 (four rolls, copied at Lübeck in 1588); two sixteenth-century rolls at the Wellcome Collection; several rolls in the Bodleian; an eighteenth-century version at the Science Museum, London; a sixteenth-century copy at the Fitzwilliam, Cambridge; and a copy at Yale, which is fully digitized on Wikimedia Commons. Many are photographed; the Wellcome rolls are openly digitized. There is no critical edition of the corpus, no published collation of the 23 witnesses, and no single machine-readable transcription of the verses across copies. Adam McLean has published a commercial facsimile study; the verse texts circulate in Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum (Ashmole, 1652) and in Waite-era reprints.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Jennifer Rampling's work is the necessary starting point: "Establishing the Canon: George Ripley and his Alchemical Sources" (*Ambix* 55, 2008), "Transmission and Transmutation" (*Early Science and Medicine* 17, 2012), and above all "Depicting the Medieval Alchemical Cosmos: George Ripley's Wheel of Inferior Astronomy" (*Early Science and Medicine* 18, 2013), which shows how a Ripleian diagram can be read against the textual corpus that generated it — the method the scrolls need. Anke Timmermann's *Verse and Transmutation* (Brill, 2013) treats the Middle English alchemical verse corpus and its manuscript transmission. The field's current position is that the scrolls are a transmission problem before they are an iconographic one: until the witnesses are collated and the archetype reconstructed, iconographic readings are being applied to a text that is partly the accident of a copyist.

## What a solution would have to do
Produce a stemma. Collate all 23 witnesses figure by figure and verse by verse from images, establish the archetype's figure order and verse assignment, and date the innovations; a reading that draws on a detail present in only one eighteenth-century copy has read the copyist, not the scroll. Then, for the iconography, identify the textual source of each figure in the Ripleian and pseudo-Lullian corpus at the density Rampling reached for the Wheel, and show the identified sources account for the whole sequence in its archetypal order. A claimed laboratory reading would additionally have to name the substances, be consistent with fifteenth-century English practice, and survive the test of predicting a figure held back from the fitting.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: genuinely open, and a stemma would reshape the study of English alchemical imagery, but the stakes are inside the history of alchemy.
- material 4: about 23 witnesses, several openly digitized at high resolution, verses legible; held back from 5 by the absence of any critical edition or cross-witness transcription.
- solvable 3: the verses certainly mean something determinate; whether the figure sequence encodes one intended operation is exactly where serious scholars divide.
- compute 4: collating 23 digitized pictorial witnesses — image registration, figure matching, verse alignment — is a well-posed computational job with a concrete output (a stemma) and nobody has done it.
- verifiable 3: a stemma is judged by philological criteria and could win consensus; the iconographic referents would not become mechanically checkable.
- crowding 4: a handful of serious treatments, effectively one specialist; the collation is untouched.
