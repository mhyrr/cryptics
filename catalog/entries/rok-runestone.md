# Rök runestone (Ög 136)

## What it is
The longest known runic inscription: a granite slab 2.4 m high and about 5.1 tonnes, standing under a shelter beside the church at Rök in Östergötland, carved around AD 800 and bearing roughly 760 characters on five faces. Rundata signum Ög 136. The inscription names Varinn as the commemorator and Vámóðr as the commemorated, and then, instead of the expected memorial formula, sets out a sequence of numbered statements introduced by phrases translated as "I say/tell the twelfth" and the like, with passages referring to a ruler who "ruled over the shore of the Hreiðsea" — long read as Theodoric the Great — and to twenty kings on Sjælland. Several sections are deliberately obscured: parts are written in the older Elder Futhark, which was already antiquarian by 800, and parts in cipher runes using a displacement (Caesar-type) system.

## What is unsolved
Not the letters — the transliteration is largely settled and the ciphers were broken long ago — but what the inscription is saying and why. The dispute is over sense and genre: whether the numbered statements are riddles, and if so what their answers are; whether the "Theodoric" stanza is a reference to the Ostrogothic king, to a local ruler, or to something else; whether the text is heroic-legendary, ritual, apocalyptic, or self-referential; and whether the composition is one coherent utterance or a sequence of separately posed questions. The carver's use of three scripts and two cipher systems is agreed to be purposeful, but what the purpose was is not.

## What survives
The complete stone, in good condition, documented photographically and in 3-D; the transliteration is published in Samnordisk runtextdatabas / Rundata as Ög 136 and is machine-readable. A long editorial tradition supplies competing normalisations and translations. The principal recent treatment, with a full text and translation, is Per Holmberg, Bo Gräslund, Olof Sundqvist and Henrik Williams, "The Rök Runestone and the End of the World," *Futhark: International Journal of Runic Studies* 9–10 (2018–19, published 2020), 7–38, DOI 10.33063/diva-401040, which is open access. Holmberg's earlier "Svaren på Rökstenens gåtor: En socialsemiotisk analys av meningsskapande och rumslighet," *Futhark* 6 (2015), 65–106, is the companion study.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The classical readings — Sophus Bugge, Otto von Friesen, later Otto Höfler, Lars Lönnroth, Gun Widmark and Joseph Harris — treat the inscription as a father's memorial to a dead son framed by heroic-legendary allusion, with the Theodoric stanza as a genuine reference to the Gothic king, and differ over whether the frame is vengeance, initiation, or genealogical boast. Holmberg (2015) broke with this by reading the numbered statements as riddles whose answers refer to the monument and the act of inscribing it rather than to heroic narrative. Holmberg, Gräslund, Sundqvist and Williams (2020) then argued that the riddles concern the sun, light and cosmic crisis, and that the inscription responds to a remembered climate catastrophe — the extreme weather of 535–536 — read together with the 774–775 radiocarbon spike and a solar eclipse of 810, i.e. that the stone is about fear of another Fimbulwinter rather than about Theodoric. The 2020 reading was widely reported in the press. Its scholarly reception is the load-bearing thing this entry cannot yet report: no published rebuttal or endorsement was located in the sources consulted (see Unverified claims). The field's position is therefore best described as split between the traditional heroic-memorial reading and the Holmberg school, with the transliteration itself not in serious dispute.

## What a solution would have to do
Read all five faces as one text under one hypothesis about genre, and in doing so: (1) account for every numbered statement, including the ones prior readings skip, with a stated answer if the statements are riddles; (2) explain the script switching functionally — why exactly these passages are in Elder Futhark and these in cipher, and what the displacement values are for each — rather than treating it as generic mystification; (3) derive the reading from the syntax and word-forms of early Old East Norse without emendation, and say where it emends; (4) explain the spatial arrangement, since the order in which the faces are to be read is itself part of the evidence and the two schools order them differently; (5) show that the reading is consistent with the commemorative frame (Varinn for Vámóðr) that opens the inscription, which any purely cosmological reading must still accommodate. A claim that a passage alludes to a legend or to a historical event must point to an attested version of that legend or a dated proxy for that event, and must explain how a carver around 800 had access to it. Verification, realistically, is adoption by the runological community and by the next Rundata revision — not a mechanical check.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the flagship object of runology, with a century-old reading challenged in 2020 and nothing settled since; not 5 because the text is read at the level of letters and the dispute is interpretive.
- material 4: complete, undamaged in the main, fully documented, transcription published and machine-readable. Not 5 because some rune sequences remain contested at the level of segmentation.
- solvable 4: the ciphers and the numbered structure are proof of deliberate design with a determinate content; not 5 because the content may be allusive by intent.
- compute 3: the cryptographic part is already solved, so computation cannot decide the question; it can quantify — formula and collocation comparison against the eddic and skaldic corpora, and statistical testing of the proposed riddle-answer patterns. Worth doing, not decisive.
- verifiable 3: recognition would be by scholarly consensus, which could plausibly form within years given how concentrated the community is.
- crowding 2: a very large dedicated literature and many failed readings by first-rank scholars, but the 2020 intervention shows the field is not exhausted.

## Unverified claims
- **The reception of the 2020 reinterpretation.** No published response, rebuttal or endorsement was located. The entry's "contested" status reflects the existence of two incompatible readings, not a documented exchange. This is the single most important gap and the first thing a next pass should fix: check *Futhark* 11–15, *Arkiv för nordisk filologi*, *Scripta Islandica* and *Maal og Minne* for responses.
- The exact displacement values of the cipher runes and which passages use which system were not verified; the sources consulted state only that displacement ciphering, cipher runes, Elder and Younger Futhark are all used.
- Whether the "twenty kings on Sjælland" and "Theodoric" passages are still read that way by the Holmberg school, or reinterpreted, was not established from the sources fetched.
- The weight of 5.1 tonnes and height of 2.4 m are from the Wikipedia article.
- The 774–775 radiocarbon spike and the 810 solar eclipse are cited by the 2020 authors as context; the entry reports their use of them, not their relevance.
