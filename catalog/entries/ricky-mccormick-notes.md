# Ricky McCormick's encrypted notes (1999)

## What it is
Two handwritten sheets found in the trouser pockets of Ricky McCormick (b. 14 June 1958), whose body was discovered on 30 June 1999 in a cornfield near West Alton, St. Charles County, Missouri; he is thought to have died between roughly 25 and 28 June. The sheets carry lines of capital letters and numbers, sometimes grouped inside parentheses, in no recognized notation. On 29 March 2011 the FBI's Cryptanalysis and Racketeering Records Unit published images of both notes and asked the public for help — an unusual step, taken because the unit had exhausted its own approaches over twelve years.

## What is unsolved
Whether the notes encode anything. The notes are the catalog's clearest case where the *prior* question dominates: McCormick's family insist he was functionally illiterate — his mother: "The only thing he could write was his name. He didn't write in no code"; a cousin said he could not "spell anything, just scribble." If that is accurate, the notes are not a cipher in any sense a cryptanalyst can attack; they are an idiosyncratic private notation, or a copy of something, or mimicry of writing. Against that, the notes have visible internal structure — recurring letter groups, consistent use of parentheses, line organization — and the FBI, which had the originals and the case file, treated them as a code worth publishing. Both positions are held by people with access to relevant evidence, which is why `solvable` is 2 rather than 1, and why this entry sits at the boundary of the rubric's admission test.

## What survives
Two sheets, in FBI custody, with images published by the Bureau in 2011 at moderate resolution. Those images are the entire corpus. There is no transcription that I could verify as authoritative — which matters enormously, because with handwriting this irregular the transcription *is* the analysis, and disagreements about whether a character is `E` or `F`, `1` or `I`, propagate into every statistic. The case file presumably contains context (what else was in his pockets, his known associations, his employment history) that would constrain interpretation; it is not public.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The FBI's CRRU worked it from 1999, did not crack it, and in 2011 declared it one of its top unsolved cases; the public appeal produced an overwhelming volume of submissions and no accepted solution. The American Cryptogram Association has also worked it without result. No peer-reviewed treatment exists. The most commonly advanced reading in the amateur literature is that the notes are a personal mnemonic system — addresses, names, or drug-trade bookkeeping written in a private abbreviation scheme with parenthesized qualifiers — which, if true, means the notes are decipherable only with biographical knowledge, not cryptanalytically. The counter-position, that they are meaningless, is held by the family. Current status: open, with no consensus even on what kind of object it is.

## What a solution would have to do
Two very different bars, depending on the hypothesis, and a claimant must pick one in advance.
If the notes are a *code* (signs for words), a solution must produce and publish a full transcription first, then a sign-to-referent mapping derived from documented facts about McCormick's life — addresses he lived at, people he knew, places he worked, dates in the case chronology — such that the mapping is fixed by the biography and *then* reads the notes, rather than being fitted to make the notes read. The verification is external and archival: the decoded content must name things checkable in the 1999 record.
If the notes are a *cipher*, the claimant must state the system, decode both sheets under one key, and show that the recurring groups and the parentheses fall out of the system rather than being explained away.
If the claim is that the notes are *not* writing, the demonstration is statistical and is the most tractable of the three: publish a careful transcription, then show that its character distribution, repetition structure and positional constraints match a corpus of known non-linguistic scribbling and copied-shapes baselines better than they match any English-derived encoding. Nobody appears to have done this, and it is the single most valuable unexecuted piece of work on the problem — a rigorous "this is not a cipher" would be a full success by this repository's standards.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: an open question in one homicide case; a solution would matter to a family and to the FBI unit, not to a field.
- material 2: two sheets, moderate-resolution images, no verified transcription, irregular hand.
- solvable 2: the best-informed people closest to the writer say there is nothing there. Not 1, because the FBI had the file and disagreed, and because visible structure is real.
- compute 2: with only two short sheets and no transcription, computation has little purchase; its one real contribution is the negative statistical test above, and that is limited by corpus size.
- verifiable 1: this is the decisive score. Absent external corroboration from the case record, no reading of a short private notation can be recognized as correct, and any reading would be as good as another.
- crowding 3: a great deal of amateur attention in 2011 and a modest ongoing interest, but the obvious approaches are exhausted and nothing systematic has been published.
