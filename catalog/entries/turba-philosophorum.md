# Turba Philosophorum (Muṣḥaf al-jamāʿa)

## What it is
The oldest Latin alchemical text translated from Arabic, and the foundational work of the Latin tradition. It stages a conference — a *turba*, a crowd — of nine pre-Socratic philosophers (Pythagoras presides; Anaximander, Anaximenes, Anaxagoras, Empedocles, Archelaus, Leucippus, Ecnostus and Xenophanes speak) who deliver sermons on matter, the elements and the work, followed by a second part of practical instruction. Xenophanes closes with three theses that affirm monotheism, one universal matter, and the elemental composition of bodies. The Arabic original is the *Muṣḥaf al-jamāʿa*, the "Book of the Assembly."

## What is unsolved
(1) The Arabic original: it survives only partially and its author and date are inferred rather than documented; the dating to around 900 rests on Ibn Umayl's use of it by the mid-tenth century and on the appearance of the poison-maiden motif in Arabic literature in the first half of the ninth. (2) The pseudo-Greek frame: which, if any, genuine Greek doxographical or alchemical material lies behind the speeches, and by what route — the philosophers' names are historical but the doctrines are not theirs, and the mechanism of the attribution is unexplained. (3) The Latin translation: who made it, when, and from which Arabic state; the Latin is the version the West read and used for four centuries. (4) The operation: what the practical second part instructs.

## What survives
The Latin text in manuscript and then in print: first printed in *Artis auriferae quam chemiam vocant* (Basel, 1572) and again in Lazarus Zetzner's *Theatrum Chemicum* (1659–61). Julius Ruska's *Turba Philosophorum: Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der Alchemie* (Springer, 1931) is the critical edition and study, printing the Latin with the Arabic evidence; it is print-only and remains the standard. Martin Plessner's *Vorsokratische Philosophie und griechische Alchemie in arabisch-lateinischer Überlieferung* (Steiner, 1975) is the second indispensable study. Arthur Edward Waite's English translation (1896) is freely available and is what most readers use; it is not based on a critical text. There is no open machine-readable critical Latin text and no full edition of the Arabic.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Ruska (1931) established that the *Turba* is a translation from Arabic and not a Greek text, which was the decisive result and is not in dispute. Plessner (1975) worked out how the compiler used pre-Socratic doxography — his verdict, quoted in the literature, is that the work is "a well planned and, from a literary point of view, a most remarkable attempt" to join Greek alchemy to Arabic science — and identified the intellectual project behind the pseudo-Greek frame. Since Plessner there has been comparatively little. The consensus: Arabic origin around 900, a deliberate and learned pseudo-Greek construction, a Latin translation of uncertain authorship that founded Western alchemy. What is unresolved is the source layer beneath the speeches and the state of the Arabic text.

## What a solution would have to do
The tractable question is source identification, and it has a hard criterion. A solution must align the speeches against the surviving Greek doxographical tradition (the Aëtius/pseudo-Plutarch *Placita*, Hippolytus, Diogenes Laertius) and the Greek alchemical corpus (the Marcianus collection, Zosimos, pseudo-Democritus) in their Arabic transmissions, and report which sentences are traceable and which are the compiler's — a match at the level of phrasing across two translations is checkable, and a claim about the compiler's project that does not distinguish quotation from composition is unsupported. Independently: edit the Arabic. A full edition of the *Muṣḥaf al-jamāʿa* from all located witnesses, collated against Ruska's Latin, would fix the translation's base text and date the Latin from below. For the practical part, a chemical reading must name substances consistent with ninth- or tenth-century Arabic practice and account for the sequence, not selected sentences.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the central historical question (Greek or Arabic) is settled and settled firmly; the residue — the source layer, the Arabic text, the Latin translator — is real but narrower.
- material 3: substantial text, but the best edition is from 1931 and print-only, the Arabic original survives only in part and is not fully edited, and the freely available English translation is pre-critical.
- solvable 3: the speeches certainly assert determinate doctrines and the practical part certainly intends operations, but the referents of the operations may not be recoverable and the field has not tried hard.
- compute 3: a cross-language parallel sweep against the Greek doxographical and alchemical corpora is the right move and the Greek corpora are digitized — but the *Turba*'s own critical text is not machine-readable and the Arabic is unedited, so the data is not ready.
- verifiable 4: a source identification is checkable phrase by phrase, and an Arabic edition would settle the textual questions by ordinary philological criteria.
- crowding 4: two serious treatments, both old, and almost nothing since 1975; the source alignment and the Arabic edition are both open.
