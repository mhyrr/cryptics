# Tamam Shud / Somerton Man code

## What it is
A short sequence of capital letters, pencilled in indented handwriting on the back of a copy of Edward FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám* (a 1941 New Zealand edition), found in a car in Adelaide in 1949 after police appealed for the book from which a slip reading "Tamám Shud" ("it is finished") had been torn. The slip was in the trouser pocket of an unidentified man found dead on 1 December 1948 against the seawall at Somerton Park beach. The lines are usually given as: `WRGOABABD / MLIAOI / WTBIMPANETP / MLIABOAIAQC / ITTMTSAMSTGAB`, with one line struck out. The code is roughly fifty letters; no line forms a word in any language.

## What is unsolved
What the letters stand for. The near-universal reading of the *form* is that these are initials of words — the letter frequencies and the absence of vowel-consonant alternation fit an English initialism, not a substitution cipher — which makes the problem a one-way function: initials throw away almost all the information, and nothing in fifty initials can select among the sentences that produce them. The 2022 identification of the dead man does not change this. Derek Abbott and the genealogist Colleen Fitzpatrick announced on 26 July 2022, from DNA genealogy, that the man was Carl "Charles" Webb, born 16 November 1905 in Footscray, Melbourne, an electrical engineer with a history of depression; South Australia Police have not confirmed the identification, describing themselves as cautiously optimistic. An identification supplies a biography that a correct reading of the code would have to be consistent with, which is a constraint — but it supplies no key.

## What survives
Almost nothing of the object. **The *Rubáiyát* itself was lost in the 1950s**, which removes the one artefact that might have carried the key: the actual page, the pencil pressure, any further indentation, and the edition's own text as a possible keytext. What survives is a police photograph of the letters, of variable legibility, and disagreement about several characters — most notoriously whether the second line begins `M L I A O I` or `M L I A B O I`, and whether the first line's final letters are `A B D` or something else. With fifty letters total, a one-character transcription dispute is a two-percent error rate on the whole corpus. The man's body was exhumed in 2021 and yielded the DNA used in the 2022 identification; the Somerton Man's other effects are catalogued in the SA Police file.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Australian naval and military cryptographers examined it in 1949 and in 1978; the 1978 analysis concluded there were insufficient symbols to provide a pattern and that no definitive interpretation was possible — that assessment has never been overturned and remains the field's position. Derek Abbott (University of Adelaide) ran a long-running research programme on the case, including statistical work on the letter frequencies comparing them against English initialisms; the conclusion was consistency with initials of English words, which narrows the type without identifying the text. Candidate readings offered over the years — a code for horse-race bets, a private memorandum, Masonic or espionage signalling, and the possibility that the letters are simply a distressed man's doodle — have no discriminating evidence between them. Consensus: unsolved, widely believed unsolvable from the surviving evidence.

## What a solution would have to do
The only credible route is external, and it now has a name attached to it. A solution must (1) settle the transcription from the best available photograph, publicly and character by character, with the disputed characters flagged; (2) propose the underlying sentences; and (3) corroborate them from documents in Carl Webb's life — his correspondence, his employment records, his known associations in Melbourne, the divorce and family records that the 2022 identification opened up — such that the expansion is fixed by a document rather than invented to fit. The test of a good candidate is that it explains features the fitting did not use: why one line was struck through, why the `MLIA…` pattern recurs at the start of two lines, and why the letters were written in *that book*, on *that page*. If the underlying text is a keytext or a quotation, the check is mechanical and total — initials of a specific passage either match or do not. Absent a document, this stays at `verifiable 1` permanently, and this repository should say so rather than encouraging attempts: the honest deliverable here is a precisely named wall.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the code is genuinely unread and sits inside a famous case whose central puzzle — who the man was — has probably just been answered, which redirects interest to the code rather than resolving it. Not higher, because the field's view is that it is unresolvable.
- material 1: about fifty pencilled letters, the original book lost, known only from a police photograph with disputed characters. The weakest material position in the catalog.
- solvable 2: someone wrote it deliberately, but if it is an initialism the referent is lost by construction, and "doodle by a suicidal man" remains a live reading among serious commentators.
- compute 1: fifty letters. Enumerating English sentences whose initials match is trivial and produces nothing; the useful statistical work (Abbott's frequency comparison) has been done and cannot decide. There is nothing left to compute.
- verifiable 1: without an external document, any expansion is as good as another. This is the score that should stop a would-be solver.
- crowding 1: seventy-five years of attempts, an enormous popular literature, official cryptanalysis in 1949 and 1978, and a dedicated academic research programme.

## Unverified claims
- The exact letter sequence. Different sources print different versions and one Wikipedia rendering includes a bracketed placeholder line; the sequence above should be treated as approximate and the disputed characters (notably a possible `B` in line 2 and the line-1 ending) are unresolved here. Any serious work must start from the police photograph.
- Whether South Australia Police have confirmed the Carl Webb identification since the sources consulted were written. As of the material checked, they had not.
- Colleen Fitzpatrick's and Derek Abbott's method and the confidence level of the genealogical identification were not examined.
- Whether the *Rubáiyát* is definitively lost or merely untraced, and the circumstances of its loss in the 1950s, were not verified beyond the encyclopedic statement.
- Abbott's frequency-analysis publication venue was not verified; the characterization of his conclusion ("consistent with initials of English words") is from general accounts of his work.
- Whether the 1978 assessment was by the Australian Department of Defence or another body, and whether it is published, was not established.
- The relevance of the "Tamám Shud" phrase's position in FitzGerald's text, and of the 1941 New Zealand edition's specific pagination, to any keytext hypothesis was not investigated.
