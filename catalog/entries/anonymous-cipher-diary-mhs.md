# Anonymous cipher diary, 1776–1845 (Massachusetts Historical Society, Ms. Sbd-133)

## What it is
A small bound volume at the Massachusetts Historical Society, catalogued as "Anonymous cipher diary, 1776-1845" (call number Ms. Sbd-133), containing diary entries kept in cipher or shorthand, arranged by year, month and day, with longer entries on one side of the leaves and shorter entries on the other. Dates are written in Arabic numerals and the month notations are evident, so the chronology can be followed even though the text cannot. The WorldCat record gives the bulk dates as 1776–1795; the "1845" in the title comes from a provenance note by Judge John Davis of Boston dated 27 March 1845, not from the diarist. The volume was found in the library of Chief Justice Theophilus Parsons (d. 1813), passed to Davis in 1841 by Theophilus Parsons Jr., and was given by Davis to the Society.

## What is unsolved
What the diary says, and who wrote it. Davis guessed Rev. Moses Parsons of Byfield (1716–1783), father of Theophilus, but ruled him out himself because the entries run past Moses's death (the MHS catalogue note says the entries extend to 1799). The first question is the system: a published eighteenth-century English shorthand (several were in use by New England clergy and lawyers), a personal modification of one, or a true cipher. Klaus Schmeh's reading of the two published page images is that it looks like "notes with many abbreviations and shorthand symbols" with many numerals.

## What survives
The complete volume, at the MHS. Only two page images are public (on the MHS Beehive blog, 2009, reproduced by Schmeh in 2022). No transcription exists. Page count is not stated in any source consulted. The diary is dated throughout, which is the property that matters most: every entry has an external anchor (weather, deaths, sermons, public events of the Revolutionary and early-Federal period in Essex County).

## Prior attempts and current consensus
In 1842 Davis consulted Benjamin Lynde Oliver Jr. (1788–1843), a legal writer with an interest in stenography; no progress is recorded. Jeremy Dibbell's 2009 Beehive post asked readers to identify the system and promised a follow-up on "a possible (and potentially really fascinating) contender" among Parsons-family clergy; that follow-up was not found. Schmeh featured it in 2022 as unsolved. No published decipherment, and no serious attempt beyond these, was found. Essentially untouched.

## What a solution would have to do
Identify the system (a named published shorthand, or a stated key) and show that it reads the whole volume continuously as English, not selected entries. Read entries must agree with their dates: weather against surviving Massachusetts weather records, named deaths and ordinations against church and town records, public events against the chronology of 1776–1799. The reading should narrow the author to a person whose movements match the diary, and explain the diary's route into Theophilus Parsons's library. A reading that uses the dates only as labels, without being checked against them, has not used the diary's best control.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: wide open, but the stake is a private Revolutionary-era diary, valuable to New England and ecclesiastical history rather than to the wider world.
- material 3: the whole book survives in a public institution, but only two pages are imaged and nothing is transcribed. Imaging would raise this to 5 at once.
- solvable 4: dated entries by year, month and day show a diary with real content; the risk is an idiosyncratic personal shorthand whose arbitrary signs cannot all be recovered.
- compute 3: once imaged, the core step can be enumerated: match sign inventories against the finite set of shorthand systems printed before 1776 (Shelton, Rich, Mason, Weston, Byrom and others), then fit the residue with dated cribs. Scored 3, not 4, because the data is not ready.
- verifiable 4: the dates make it strongly checkable: a correct reading will say things about the weather and local deaths that archives confirm.
- crowding 5: one 1842 consultation, one 2009 blog post, one 2022 blog post.
