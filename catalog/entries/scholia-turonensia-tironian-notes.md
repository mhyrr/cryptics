# Tironian-note glosses of the Vergilius Turonensis (Bern, Burgerbibliothek, Cod. 165)

## What it is
A 219-folio parchment Virgil (Bucolics, Georgics, Aeneid), 32.5 × 24.5 cm, copied at Saint-Martin, Tours, in the second quarter of the ninth century. The Levite Berno gave it to the abbey, and Jakob Graviseth gave it to Bern in 1632. The scribes left room between the lines and in the margins for commentary. Carolingian readers filled it with scholia drawn from Servius, Donatus and other late-antique commentators, and compiled at Tours (hence *Scholia Turonensia*). The glosses were written in a mixture of ordinary script and Tironian notes: the Roman shorthand, taught in Carolingian schools from the late-antique sign dictionary *Commentarii Notarum Tironianarum* (CNT), with some 13,000–14,000 word-signs.

## What is unsolved
What the shorthand glosses say. This is not a cipher in the adversarial sense. The system is documented and the sign dictionary survives. But very few living scholars read Tironian notes fluently, and German Wikipedia's account says editions of these glosses "scarcely exist": the ten pages Hermann Hagen printed in 1867 are still the fullest publication from the manuscript. So what is probably the largest single witness to Carolingian classroom reading of Virgil is mostly unread. That is the reason it is here: a long, digitized text in a period practitioner's coded system, containing a teacher's own notes, and nearly untouched.

## What survives
The manuscript, complete and fully digitized in colour on e-codices (the Swiss virtual manuscript library), and on Europeana and Wikimedia Commons. The CNT survives in several Carolingian copies. The standard modern tools for reading the notes are the nineteenth-century sign lexica (Kopp's *Lexicon Tironianum*, Schmitz's *Commentarii*) and online indexes such as the *index tironianorum*; their machine-readability was not checked. How many glosses are in shorthand, as against alphabetic script, is not stated in any source consulted.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Hagen's 1867 partial edition. In 2023 the Volkswagen Foundation funded an 18-month project of Heidelberg, Erlangen and Mainz, "Deciphering Stenographic Notes in Historical Documents" (Tino Licht among its leads, about €35,000 reported), to build a pattern-recognition tool that transcribes Tironian notes, tested on this very manuscript. No results from it were found. This is a live, funded, small effort, not a crowd.

## What a solution would have to do
A full transcription of the shorthand layer. Each note resolved to a CNT sign or a documented variant, with the Latin reading, and the unresolved signs listed rather than guessed. The readings must parse as Latin that glosses the line they sit beside. Where a gloss derives from Servius or Donatus, the reading must match the known commentary, which makes such glosses a built-in check. Once the Servian material is subtracted, whatever is left is the new content: the Tours masters' own remarks. A tool-based transcription should report its accuracy on a held-out set of notes read by a human specialist.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the question is open and the answer would matter to Carolingian studies and the reception of Virgil, not beyond them.
- material 4: complete, one manuscript, fully digitized; there is no transcription, and the shorthand layer's extent is unmeasured.
- solvable 5: the system is known, the dictionary survives, and the glosses comment on a known text. There is certainly a determinate meaning.
- compute 4: it is a handwriting-recognition-plus-lexicon problem with strong language and source constraints: sign spotting, CNT lookup, Latin parsing, Servius alignment. Scored 4, not 5, because symbol spotting on Carolingian shorthand is not a solved vision task.
- verifiable 4: readings are checked against the CNT values, Latin grammar and the Servius/Donatus parallels; the original remarks can be checked only by the first two.
- crowding 4: Hagen 1867 and one small current project.
- compute mode: IMAGE (HTR and sign spotting) then SWEEP (Servius/Donatus alignment) · verifier STRONG · space ENUMERABLE (a closed sign dictionary) · signal YES · fit MEDIUM: the images and dictionary are public, but a labelled Tironian training set must be built first.
