# Oera Linda Book

## What it is
A paper manuscript in an invented script and an imitation of Old Frisian, presenting itself as a family chronicle of the Over de Linden family covering history from 2194 BCE to 803 CE. It claims that Frisian civilization possessed writing before the Greeks and Phoenicians, that a Frisian mother-goddess figure founded European culture, and that Atlantis-like catastrophes shaped the North Sea coast. It surfaced in 1867 when Cornelis Over de Linden, a shipyard foreman at Den Helder, brought it to the provincial librarian Eelco Verwijs in Leeuwarden. The manuscript is held in Friesland (Tresoar / the Fries Genootschap collection, Leeuwarden).

## What is unsolved
Not authenticity. The manuscript is written on nineteenth-century paper, the language is a pastiche that no Frisian philologist accepts, and by 1879 the scholarly verdict was settled: it is a modern fabrication. The catalog keeps it as a calibration case for the `solvable` axis — a text that reads as an unsolved ancient document and scores low because the best evidence is of fabrication. The genuinely open question is attribution: who wrote it, and to what end. Three candidates are in play — Cornelis Over de Linden, who produced it; Eelco Verwijs, who had the philological competence and the access; and François Haverschmidt, the Protestant minister and poet who wrote as "Piet Paaltjens." Goffe Jensma's monograph argues for Haverschmidt as principal author, with the others involved, and reads the book as an intentionally temporary hoax aimed at Frisian nationalists and orthodox Christians, designed to be exposed.

## What survives
The manuscript itself, complete and in good condition, with its full text transcribed, translated repeatedly and digitized; Jan Gerhardus Ottema's 1872 Dutch edition and translation, which defended authenticity; nineteenth-century Dutch press and learned-society debate from 1867 to 1879; and the personal papers and published writings of all three candidate authors, which is what makes the attribution question tractable. For an attribution problem this is an unusually complete evidence base: a fixed target text, three named suspects, and large comparison corpora in Dutch and in Haverschmidt's case in literary Dutch and Frisian.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Ottema's defence collapsed under philological attack within a decade; the paper dating and the impossible linguistic forms closed the authenticity question. The twentieth century gave the book a second life outside scholarship: Herman Wirth's 1933 German translation brought it to the attention of Heinrich Himmler, and it circulated in Nazi-era and later esoteric and earth-mysteries literature. Goffe Jensma's *De gemaskerde God* (2004) is the standard modern treatment and the source of the Haverschmidt thesis; it is a genuine piece of literary-historical detective work rather than a debunking, since the authenticity question was already closed. Fringe defenders of authenticity continue to publish; the field does not engage.

## What a solution would have to do
The attribution claim is the live one and it has clean bars. (a) Stylometric: a computational authorship attribution of the Oera Linda text against control corpora of Over de Linden's, Verwijs's and Haverschmidt's known Dutch writings, with the obvious complication that the target is in a constructed language — which means the test has to be run on translation-invariant features (function-word-equivalents in the constructed grammar, sentence-length distributions, rhythmic and syntactic habits) and must be validated on a held-out set first, or it proves nothing. (b) Codicological: paper, watermark and ink analysis dating the manuscript precisely and, if possible, tying the paper stock to a purchaser. (c) Documentary: correspondence, diaries or publishers' records placing the three men in contact around 1865–67. (d) Motive and design: Jensma's "intentional temporary hoax" reading predicts that the text contains deliberate internal signals of its own fictionality; a strong version of the thesis would enumerate them in advance and show they are not artifacts of hindsight. A claim of authenticity would have to explain nineteenth-century machine-made paper, which it cannot.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: authenticity is settled and a working consensus on authorship exists after Jensma, with real minority dissent about who held the pen.
- material 5: a complete manuscript, fully transcribed, translated and digitized, with large comparison corpora for all three candidate authors.
- solvable 2: the best evidence favours fabrication; there is a determinate author to find, but no hidden ancient referent.
- compute 4: authorship attribution is exactly the kind of sub-question computation can settle, and the data is ready; not 5 because the constructed language makes standard stylometric features unusable without careful adaptation and validation.
- verifiable 4: a well-validated attribution plus codicological evidence would convince by strong criteria, and the field has shown it will accept such an argument.
- crowding 3: a modest Dutch scholarly literature and one standard monograph; the obvious approaches have been tried but the stylometric attack is, as far as this entry can tell, open.
