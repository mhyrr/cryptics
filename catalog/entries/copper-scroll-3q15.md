# Copper Scroll (3Q15) — the treasure list and the Greek letter groups

## What it is
A document engraved on two sheets of copper (about 1 % tin), originally one roll roughly 2.4 m long, found on 14 March 1952 in Cave 3 at Qumran — the only Dead Sea Scroll recovered by archaeologists rather than Bedouin, and the only one on metal. Oxidation had made it unrollable; H. Wright Baker of Manchester cut it into 23 strips in 1955–56. Its text is a bare inventory of 64 entries: 63 give a hiding place and a quantity of gold or silver, the rest list tithing vessels, other vessels, scrolls and priestly vestments. One entry alone specifies 900 talents of silver. The last entry points to a duplicate document with fuller particulars, never found. It has been displayed at the Jordan Museum in Amman since 2013.

## What is unsolved
Where the places are. The scroll names them in the idiom of someone who assumed his reader knew the countryside — "in the cistern which is below the rampart, on the east side" — and almost none of the 64 has been identified to a spot on the ground that anyone has excavated successfully. Two subsidiary puzzles ride on top of it: (a) seven of the entries close with a short group of Greek letters, whose function is unexplained; (b) whether the hoard was ever real, or whether the whole document is folklore, a literary fiction, or a memory of the Second Temple treasury.

## What survives
The object itself, complete but cut into strips and corroded. Editions: J. T. Milik's official edition in DJD III (1962); John Marco Allegro's independent transcription and translation (1960), made while he supervised the opening; Émile Puech's edition, which had the benefit of the 1994–96 conservation campaign by Électricité de France (X-rays, new photography, a metal facsimile); the scroll was rephotographed at higher precision in 1988. Judah K. Lefkovits, *The Copper Scroll 3Q15: A Reevaluation* (Brill, 1999) is the standard column-by-column commentary; Brooke and Davies, *Copper Scroll Studies* (2002) collects the conference that followed the EDF work. Readings at exactly the load-bearing points — place names, numerals, the Greek letters — still differ between editors. There is no openly published high-resolution image set; a record page for "3Q Copper Scroll" exists in the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library but its holdings could not be read in this pass.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Allegro dug at candidate sites in 1962 and found nothing. Milik read the text as folklore — a Jewish legend of hidden temple wealth — and much of the field followed him for a generation; the trend since the 1990s has been to take it as a real inventory, on the grounds that no folktale reads like a quartermaster's ledger. Dating estimates cluster in the first century CE: Cross 25–75 CE, Albright 70–135 CE, McCarter, Wolters, Wilmot and Lefkovits near 70 CE, Milik c. 100 CE. Proposed owners: the Qumran community's own wealth, the Second Temple treasury evacuated before 70 CE, or post-70 funds gathered for rebuilding. Nobody has produced a published identification of a location that yielded anything. A newcomer should read Lefkovits for the text, *Copper Scroll Studies* for the state of the debate, and Wolters on the Greek letters.

## What a solution would have to do
Solve the itinerary, not one entry. The scroll is formulaic — locality, sublocality, direction, depth in cubits, contents, amount — and any serious identification must (1) rest on a transcription that is defended against Milik/Allegro/Puech/Lefkovits at the disputed characters, (2) place all or nearly all 64 entries in a geography that is internally consistent in order and travel distance, since consecutive entries appear to cluster, (3) account for the recurrence of the same localities across non-adjacent entries, (4) explain the seven Greek letter groups by a rule that applies to all seven and not to the other 57 entries, and (5) explain the total, which on the scroll's own figures is implausibly large for the Qumran community. An excavation that produces metal at a predicted spot is self-verifying; an excavation that produces nothing proves only that the spot was emptied, which is why negative results have never settled anything here. A reading that identifies a picturesque handful of sites and is silent on the rest is not a reading.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the central question is untouched, and a real hoard or a firm Second Temple provenance would be a significant find; not 5 because the text's genre and rough date are now reasonably agreed.
- material 3: the whole text survives and is published, but access is partial — the object is in Amman, high-resolution imaging is not openly available, and editors still disagree at the characters that matter most.
- solvable 4: the document is plainly a purposeful inventory, so an intended meaning certainly existed; not 5 because Milik's folklore thesis, still not refuted, would make the referents fictional.
- compute 3: a systematic gazetteer sweep — Mishnaic, Josephan and Greek toponymy against the scroll's localities, with the entry order as an ordering constraint and modern GIS as the search space — is a real and largely undone computation; it stays at 3 because disputed readings and the absence of open imagery mean the input data is not clean.
- verifiable 3: a hit would be mechanical, but only a hit; short of that, identifications are adjudicated by scholarly plausibility.
- crowding 2: a large scholarly literature, a 600-page commentary, a conference volume, plus a vigorous amateur ecosystem of treasure hunters.

## Unverified claims
- The exact Greek letter groups. The seven groups are commonly transcribed as strings of two or three letters (KEN, XAG, HN, ΘE, ΔI, TP, ΣK circulate in the secondary literature); Wikipedia reports only that seven location names carry Greek letters "indicating numerical values, likely representing isopsephy." Neither the individual strings nor the isopsephy explanation was confirmed against an edition in this pass, and Al Wolters' specific proposal was not located.
- What the Leon Levy library record actually contains for 3Q15, and whether any images of the scroll are openly available anywhere at research resolution.
- Whether the announced Facsimile Editions (London) facsimile, reported in production in 2014, was published.
- Whether any 3D surface scan of the 23 strips exists; none was found.
