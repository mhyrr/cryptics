# Hypnerotomachia Poliphili

## What it is
An anonymous illustrated romance printed by Aldus Manutius at Venice in December 1499, usually called the most beautiful book of the Italian Renaissance. Poliphilo dreams his way through ruins, triumphs, gardens and rites in pursuit of Polia. The prose is a constructed macaronic idiom that welds Latin and Greek roots onto Italian syntax and coins new words where existing vocabulary failed; Hebrew and Arabic appear in the illustrations. It carries 172 woodcuts, several of which present invented "hieroglyphic" inscriptions with Latin glosses.

## What is unsolved
Not the authorship signature: the acrostic formed by the decorated chapter initials, POLIAM FRATER FRANCISCVS COLVMNA PERAMAVIT, is established and undisputed as a feature of the book. What is unresolved is (a) *which* Francesco Colonna — the Venetian Dominican of SS. Giovanni e Paolo, the lord of Palestrina, or neither, with Alberti and others also proposed; (b) the referents of the pseudo-hieroglyphic inscriptions and whether they encode more than the Latin glosses supplied beside them; (c) whether the architectural, ritual and botanical program is a single coherent doctrine or an accumulation of learned display; and (d) the identity of the designer of the woodcuts.

## What survives
The 1499 Aldine edition survives in many copies and is fully digitized at high resolution (Library of Congress; Getty; MIT). The text is available in the critical edition of Marco Ariani and Mino Gabriele (Adelphi, 1998, with commentary), and completely in English for the first time in Joscelyn Godwin's 1999 translation (Thames & Hudson). Earlier witnesses: a French adaptation (1546) and a partial English version of 1592, *The Strife of Love in a Dreame*, attributed to R.D. (Robert Dallington). No manuscript survives. The macaronic text has been transcribed and is machine-readable in several editions; the woodcuts are digitized as images, and the hieroglyphic plates are individually legible.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Three positions dominate. Giovanni Pozzi and Lucia Ciapponi's 1964 edition and Ariani–Gabriele's 1998 commentary treat the book as an immensely learned humanist compilation whose sources can be identified passage by passage; on this reading the "program" is the sum of its quotations, not a cipher. Liane Lefaivre (*Leon Battista Alberti's Hypnerotomachia Poliphili*, MIT Press, 1997) argued for Alberti's authorship on architectural and stylistic grounds; this has not carried the field. Maurizio Calvesi argued for Francesco Colonna of Palestrina. Gombrich and Karl Giehlow situated the hieroglyphs in the Horapollo tradition — the pseudo-*Hieroglyphica* recovered in 1419 — which makes them learned invention rather than Egyptian. The consensus a newcomer should absorb: Ariani–Gabriele for the text, Godwin for access, Lefaivre for the strongest dissenting attribution.

## What a solution would have to do
For the authorship: produce archival evidence (a Dominican record, a dedication, a bookseller's note, an autograph) tying a named Francesco Colonna to the Aldine print shop or to the text's specific learning, not stylistic resemblance. A stylometric argument would have to be run against a control set of comparable macaronic and humanist Latin, and would have to explain why the acrostic names a friar. For the hieroglyphs: show, for the whole set and not one plate, that the pictorial signs generate the Latin glosses by a stated rule, and that the rule predicts the reading of a plate withheld from the fitting. For the program: identify the sources of the architecture, the rites and the botany at the density Ariani–Gabriele reached for the prose, and then show that the identified sources compose into one argument — a reading that explains the triumphs and abandons the Polyandrion is not a reading.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the attribution and the program are both genuinely open and the book is central to Renaissance studies; not 5 because the acrostic already fixes a name.
- material 5: complete printed text, many copies, high-resolution digitization, a critical edition and a complete translation.
- solvable 3: the acrostic proves the author built at least one hidden layer, but serious scholars hold that the imagery is erudite display rather than a decodable system.
- compute 4: the obvious undone move is a mass source-parallel sweep of the macaronic lexicon and the visual motifs against the digitized humanist Latin, Greek and emblem corpora — Ariani–Gabriele did this by hand for the prose only.
- verifiable 3: a source identification is checkable word for word, but a "program" reading would settle only by consensus.
- crowding 2: a large literature and famous failed attributions; not 1 because the hieroglyph and source questions are far from exhausted.

## Unverified claims
- The count of 172 woodcuts is the figure usually given; it was not confirmed against a bibliographical authority in this pass.
- The claimed date of composition (1467) rests on a colophon-like statement inside the book and is not independently documented.
- The attribution of the woodcuts to Benedetto Montagna or to Botticelli is reported in encyclopedic sources as disputed; no scholarly adjudication was located.
- Pozzi–Ciapponi (1964) and Calvesi's Palestrina thesis are cited here from secondary report; neither edition was consulted directly.
