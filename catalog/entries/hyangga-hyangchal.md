# Hyangga — the hyangchal readings of the 25 surviving Old Korean poems

## What it is
The only substantial body of Old Korean verse: twenty-five poems written with Chinese characters used to encode Korean, in the system called *hyangchal*. Fourteen survive in the *Samguk yusa*, a late-thirteenth-century compilation, and are dated from the late sixth to the ninth century; eleven are by the monk Kyunyeo (Gyunyeo) and survive in his eleventh-century biography. Eighteen of the twenty-five are Buddhist in theme, and eulogy and mourning dominate — laments for monks, warriors, a dead sister. A ninth-century anthology, the *Samdaemok*, which would have preserved far more, is lost.

## What is unsolved
How to read them. *Hyangchal*, like Japanese *man'yōgana*, mixes characters used for their meaning (read as the Korean word) with characters used for their sound, and the mixture is not marked. Unlike *man'yōgana*, there is almost no corpus to learn the conventions from: twenty-five poems, no contemporary manuscripts, and transmission through sources three to seven centuries later than composition. Consequently every hyangga has multiple competing reconstructions, differing not only in phonology but in syntax and sometimes in sense. The only surviving near-contemporary description of the system's structure is itself ambiguous, and the classification derived from it has been challenged in Korean scholarship since the 1980s, with competing three-form and two-form analyses in play. Because Old Korean is known almost entirely from these poems, the readings and the grammar have to be reconstructed together — which is circular, and known to be.

## What survives
The twenty-five poems in two late witnesses (*Samguk yusa*, compiled by Iryeon, late 13th century; *Gyunyeo-jeon*, 11th century), plus fragmentary *hyangchal*-adjacent material outside them. The supporting evidence that has grown in recent decades is the *idu*/*gugyeol* corpus: sixth- to eighth-century wooden tablets (*mokkan*) from Silla sites, stone inscriptions, and interlinear gloss materials in Buddhist manuscripts, which document the same orthographic strategies in non-poetic contexts and are the nearest thing to independent control data. The standard reconstructions are Ogura Shinpei's (1929), Yang Chu-dong's (1942), and the later Korean tradition through Kim Wan-jin and Nam Pung-hyun; Alexander Vovin has published readings in English. No open digital edition of the *hyangga* with variant readings was located.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Ogura and Yang produced the two foundational reconstruction systems, and essentially all later Korean work is a dialogue with them. The modern Korean school, above all Nam Pung-hyun, has reframed the problem by bringing the *gugyeol* and *mokkan* evidence to bear, on the argument that the poems cannot be read in isolation from the wider Old Korean writing practice they belong to. Vovin's readings apply Old Japanese comparative method and reach conclusions that diverge from the Korean consensus at many points. What is agreed: the general sense of most poems, the identity of the authors where given, and the fact that *hyangchal* mixes logographic and phonographic usage. What is not agreed: the phonographic value of most graphs, the morphological analysis of nearly every line, and whether the system admits a consistent description at all. No reading of any single hyangga is settled in the sense that the field has stopped proposing alternatives.

## What a solution would have to do
A contribution here is a system, not a poem. It would have to: (1) state a graph-by-graph inventory of proposed values, distinguishing those attested in *mokkan*, inscriptions and *gugyeol* material from those posited only to make a poem work, and report the ratio, since a reconstruction that invents values for a quarter of its graphs is a paraphrase; (2) apply the same inventory to all twenty-five poems without local exceptions, and publish the failures; (3) produce Old Korean morphology that is consistent across poems — the same particle, spelled the same way, doing the same work — rather than a per-poem grammar; (4) be tested on the *gugyeol* and *mokkan* material as held-out data, which is the only genuinely independent check available; and (5) account for transmission, since the witnesses are centuries late and a reading that requires no emendation anywhere is suspicious while one that emends freely proves nothing. The wall should be named in advance: with twenty-five poems there may simply not be enough data to fix the system, in which case the honest result is a stated bound on what is recoverable, and the remaining hope is archaeological — more *mokkan*.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the gist of most poems is agreed and the corpus is small, so this is "resolved in outline with a real residue"; against that, Old Korean as a language is known almost only from here, so the residue is the whole of a field. A defensible case exists for 4.
- material 2: twenty-five poems, no contemporary manuscripts, two late witnesses, and the anthology that would have supplied the rest is lost. The surviving portion is the binding constraint on any method.
- solvable 4: these were written to be read by contemporaries and encode determinate Korean utterances; not 5 because seven centuries of transmission through Chinese-literate copyists may have destroyed part of what was encoded.
- compute 3: structurally the same problem as *man'yōgana* — learn graph values from distribution, then search readings under grammatical and metrical constraints — but with roughly one two-hundredth of the corpus, so there is too little data to learn from. The *gugyeol*/*mokkan* material is the variable that could raise this; digitizing and aligning it is the enabling move.
- verifiable 2: competing readings have coexisted for a century and no criterion currently selects between them; a system validated on held-out *gugyeol* data would deserve 3.
- crowding 3: a real literature but a small and largely regional one — two foundational systems, a modern Korean school, one Western scholar working on it — and essentially untouched in English or computationally.
