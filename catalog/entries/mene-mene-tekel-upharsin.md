# Mene mene tekel upharsin (Daniel 5:25) — the writing on the wall

## What it is
Four Aramaic words that a disembodied hand writes on the wall of Belshazzar's banqueting hall in Daniel 5, which the king's own wise men cannot read and which Daniel then interprets. The consonantal text at Daniel 5:25 reads מְנֵא מְנֵא תְּקֵל וּפַרְסִין — *mənē mənē təqēl ûp̄arsîn*. The Book of Daniel in its final form is dated by critical scholarship to around 165 BCE, in the Maccabean crisis, and the Belshazzar story is treated as a legend of the Persian or early Hellenistic period rather than a report of 539 BCE.

## What is unsolved
Almost nothing, which is why the entry is here: it is a calibration case. The chapter decodes itself. Verses 26–28 read the same consonants twice, once as nouns and once as verbs: *mənē* as a mina and as "God has numbered your kingdom and brought it to an end"; *təqēl* as a shekel and as "you have been weighed in the balance and found wanting"; and then — the detail that shows the mechanism — the text switches from the plural *ûp̄arsîn* of verse 25 to the singular *pərēs* in verse 28, read as "your kingdom has been divided and given to the Medes and the Persians," *pārās*. The residual scholarly questions are narrow: why *mənē* is written twice, whether *parsîn* is a dual, a plural, or a half-mina (*pərēs*), and whether the Old Greek and Theodotion versions preserve a different or shorter original text.

## What survives
The Masoretic text, complete and canonically fixed, with vowel pointing that already encodes the interpretive tradition; the Aramaic consonants are what matter and they are stable. Two independent Greek witnesses, the Old Greek and Theodotion, transmit the scene with their own transliterations and are the evidence for any claim about an earlier form of the inscription. Qumran preserves Daniel fragments (1Q71–72, 4Q112–116), relevant to the book's textual history. The passage is available in machine-readable form with translation from Sefaria's API, quoted above; the usual critical editions are BHS/BHQ and Ziegler-Munnich for the Greek.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Pre-critical exegesis treated the words as miraculous and their difficulty as the point. The modern reading — that the four words are units of weight, and that Daniel's interpretation works by re-vocalizing the same consonants as passive verbs — is standard in the commentaries and is credited in the handbooks to Clermont-Ganneau's 1886 note; the *parsîn*/*pārās* pun on Persia is likewise standard. The observation that mina + mina + shekel + half-mina can be summed, and that the sum has been read as a coded sequence of Babylonian kings or as the number 62 matching Darius the Mede's age at Daniel 5:31, remains speculative and has no consensus. What is agreed: the words are weights, the interpretation is paronomasia on the consonantal text, and the author of Daniel 5 constructed both the cryptogram and its solution.

## What a solution would have to do
For the residue, not the whole: a proposal about the doubled *mənē* would have to explain it without special pleading — dittography in transmission, a deliberate doubling for emphasis, or a fourth weight — and show that the chosen explanation is consistent with what the Greek versions transliterate, since a scribal error and an authorial device predict different things about the Greek. A proposal about *parsîn* would have to account for both forms in the text (plural in 5:25, singular in 5:28) and for the Persia pun at once. A proposal that the weights encode a king-list would have to name the kings from an independent source and show the arithmetic works without adjusting the weights to fit. The general lesson the entry records: this is a cryptogram whose author supplied the key inside the text, and even so its last twenty per cent has been argued over for 140 years. That is the realistic ceiling for humanistic puzzles of this class.

## Unverified claims
- The attribution of the weights reading to Charles Clermont-Ganneau (1886, *Journal asiatique*) is reported from the standard handbook tradition; the original note was not consulted and the secondary source does not name him.
- The composition date of c. 165 BCE for Daniel's final form is the critical consensus and is asserted here from general familiarity; the secondary source says only that the story is "a folktale composed in the Persian or early Hellenistic periods."
- The specific Qumran sigla (1Q71–72, 4Q112–116) and their coverage of Daniel 5 were not verified.
- What the Old Greek and Theodotion actually transliterate at Daniel 5:25, and whether either omits one *mənē*. This is the load-bearing fact for the dittography question and it was not checked.
- The "62" arithmetic and the king-list readings are reported as circulating proposals without consensus; no specific proponent was identified.
