# Chaldean Oracles

## What it is
A body of Greek hexameter verse, treated as revealed scripture by Neoplatonist philosophers from the third to the sixth century CE and attributed in antiquity to Julian the Theurgist or his father Julian the Chaldean, working in the second century CE. Julian the Theurgist is said to have served in Marcus Aurelius' campaign against the Quadi and to have uttered the oracles in verse while in trance. Nothing survives as a book. What survives is quotation: Porphyry, Iamblichus and Proclus wrote extensive commentaries, all lost; Damascius quotes the text heavily; and the longest surviving commentary is by the eleventh-century Christian philosopher Michael Psellus.

## What is unsolved
Whether there was a single poem and, if so, what it said in what order. The oracles reach us as isolated lines and half-lines embedded in philosophical argument, each quoted for the point it proves, with the quoting author's paraphrase often indistinguishable from the verse. The open questions are therefore: the extent and sequence of the original composition; whether it was one mystery-poem, a compilation, or a genre of oracular verse with no single archetype; whether a coherent doctrinal system (the transcendent Father and his Power producing Intellect, Hecate as the boundary between the intelligible and the material, the World-Soul, and the descending Empyrean, Ethereal and Elemental worlds) belongs to the oracles themselves or to the Neoplatonists who systematized them; and which unattributed hexameters scattered in late antique prose are oracles at all.

## What survives
- No continuous text. The standard formulation is that "the original poem has not come down to us in any connected form, and is known through quotations in the works of the neoplatonists, especially Damascius."
- Wilhelm Kroll, *De oraculis Chaldaicis* (1894), arranged all then-known fragments by subject with a Latin translation; scanned and freely readable (see `digitized`).
- Hans Lewy (1956); Édouard des Places, Greek–French edition (1971), whose numbering is the one normally cited; Ruth Majercik (1989), Greek with English translation, described as the current standard edition although not a critical one.
- Psellus' commentary, which preserves both text and an interpretation of it, and is the main route into the excerpts.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The consensus is that the collection is a product of Hellenistic and Roman-era syncretism rather than anything genuinely Chaldean, that the second-century Julian attribution is the best available, and that the fragments cannot be reassembled into the original poem. Attempts to reconstruct a continuous poem from Psellus' summaries exist and "are not generally regarded as having scholarly value" — a rare case where the field has explicitly ruled a whole class of attempt out. Modern work has moved instead to the reception: how Proclus and Damascius used the oracles, and what theurgy meant in practice. A newcomer reads Majercik 1989 for the text and translation, des Places 1971 for the numbering, and Kroll 1894 for the history of the question.

## What a solution would have to do
Given that the order is lost by construction, a defensible result is not a restored poem but a defensible boundary: a fragment-by-fragment account of which quoted lines are verse, which are the quoting author's paraphrase, and which are the quoting author's own composition, each judged on metre, dialect, formula and the syntax of the citation, and each reproducible from the digitized Greek corpora. A claim to have recovered the poem's sequence would have to (i) explain why each Neoplatonist quotes the lines he does in the order he does, (ii) predict the content of lines quoted only in paraphrase, and (iii) survive the discovery of a new witness. A claim about doctrine would have to distinguish, line by line, what the verse says from what Proclus needs it to say. A new fragment identification is checkable and is the only kind of claim here that can be decisively right.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: genuinely open, and it matters for late Platonism and the history of theurgy; but it is a specialist question and the outline of the doctrine is not in doubt.
- material 2: no continuous text at all, and the fragments are mostly one and two lines, many transmitted only inside a paraphrase.
- solvable 3: there was certainly a determinate poem, but its order and extent may be unrecoverable in principle, which is exactly what the field's rejection of reconstruction attempts records.
- compute 3: the digitized Greek corpora make a systematic metrical and formulaic sweep for unidentified oracular hexameters in late antique prose a real and mostly undone job; it can add fragments and eliminate false ones, but cannot restore an order.
- verifiable 2: a reconstruction cannot be checked against anything; only a new witness or a metrical test can adjudicate.
- crowding 3: a modest, serious literature (Kroll, Lewy, des Places, Majercik) and the obvious approaches have been tried.
