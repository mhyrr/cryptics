# D'Estaing's coded letter to Gérard, Martinique, 30 April 1779

## What it is
Admiral the comte d'Estaing wrote this letter aboard the *Languedoc* in the roads of Fort Royal, Martinique, on 30 April 1779. It is addressed to Conrad-Alexandre Gérard, the first French minister to the United States, in Philadelphia. The letter alternates code and clear French:
- about seventeen lines of numerical code;
- a clear sentence hoping for orders to carry out "the plan sent in the cipher of the letter I had the honour of writing to you on the 9th of March";
- more code;
- clear news that the Americans seem able to repel the British from Georgia without allied help.

The British intercepted it. Sir Henry Clinton forwarded it to London in July 1779. It survives in the Henry Clinton Papers at the William L. Clements Library, University of Michigan, "vol. 64:14." The next item, 64:15, is a clear letter of the same date from d'Estaing to Jean Holker, in two copies, one in John André's hand.

## What is unsolved
What the two coded passages say. They plausibly concern d'Estaing's plans for the 1779 campaign: the "plan" of 9 March, and what became the Franco-American siege of Savannah in September–October. The code is a numerical codebook. Its highest group is 597, which suggests a smaller code than the four French diplomatic codes of 1780–82 that Tomokiyo reconstructed from CO 5/40. None of those four decodes it.

## What survives
The original intercept at the Clements Library. Tomokiyo's full transcription is printed on the Cryptiana list; Bourdeau counts it at 217 tokens. Whether page images are online was not confirmed. On the French side, Gérard's received copy and any decipherment of it, and d'Estaing's minute, would be in the French naval (Marine B4) or foreign-affairs (Correspondance politique, États-Unis) archives. Their survival was not checked here.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
- Tomokiyo tested his four reconstructed French codes of the period; none fits.
- He also notes a decoded but unidentified code of Luzerne, Gérard's successor, as a lead.
- Daniel Bourdeau (15 Sept 2026) logged it as "217 tokens of a 600-code with no key material; needs the archive copy," and did not attempt cryptanalysis.

No other published attempt was found. Consensus: unsolved, and not attackable from this one letter alone.

## What a solution would have to do
- Read both coded passages as continuous French in one code table of at most about 600 entries, consistent with French naval and diplomatic code design of the 1770s.
- Fit the clear sentences on either side: the "plan" of 9 March and the Georgia news.
- Be confirmed outside the letter, by a decoded French copy, the key itself, or a second message in the same code (for example, the 9 March letter). A table inferred from 217 groups alone would not convince anyone.

## Why the scores
- **mystery 2:** a single operational letter. Historians of the 1779 campaign would read it with interest, but the French side of the correspondence may already record its content.
- **material 3:** complete, and transcribed, but a single short witness; images not confirmed online.
- **solvable 5:** a state-issued code, used as designed.
- **compute 2:** this is the "large codebook, very few messages" case at its sharpest: about 217 groups in a code of about 600. Ciphertext-only recovery is underdetermined. Computation can only test candidate codes from the archives, cheaply, once they are found.
- **verifiable 5:** a found key gives French that either reads or does not, and the French archive copy would be a direct check.
- **crowding 4:** Tomokiyo's check against four codes and Bourdeau's note are the only treatments found.

## Unverified claims
- The item number "vol. 64:14" and Clinton's forwarding in July 1779 come from Tomokiyo. They were not checked in the Clements finding aid.
- Whether the Clements Library has digitized this item.
- Whether Gérard's decoded copy or d'Estaing's minute survives in French archives. If it does, the "unsolved" status is only an artefact of the British intercept.
- The token count (217) is Bourdeau's and was not recounted here.
- The connection of the "plan" to the Savannah campaign is an inference from the date and the Georgia sentence, not a sourced claim.
