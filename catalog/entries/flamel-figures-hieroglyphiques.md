# Livre des figures hiéroglyphiques and the Book of Abraham the Jew (attributed to Nicolas Flamel)

## What it is
A French alchemical text first printed at Paris in 1612 as *Livre des figures hiéroglyphiques*, and in London in 1624 as *Exposition of the Hieroglyphicall Figures*, presented as Nicolas Flamel's own account of how he acquired a gilded twenty-one-leaf book by "Abraham the Jew" for two florins in 1357, spent decades failing to read it, was helped by a converted Jewish sage met on the road back from Spain, achieved the stone, and then had its secret carved into a tympanum he paid for at the charnel of the Cimetière des Innocents in Paris. The 1612 publisher is named P. Arnauld, sieur de la Chevalerie. This entry is kept as a calibration case: the popular mystery is enormous and the scholarly question is close to settled.

## What is unsolved
Not the attribution. Nicolas Flamel (c. 1330 – 22 March 1418) was a real Parisian *écrivain public* — a scrivener who drafted contracts — whose wealth came from property and who left a documented 1416 will showing means that are substantial but not legendary; there is no contemporary evidence connecting him to alchemy. Étienne Villain argued in 1761 that the 1612 publisher was the actual author, and the field's synthesis is that Flamel's reputation as an adept is a seventeenth-century invention. What *is* unresolved: who actually wrote the 1612 text, whether "P. Arnauld de la Chevalerie" is a real person or a mask, whether any earlier manuscript of the *Figures* exists, and what, if anything, the surviving Innocents arch sculpture actually depicted.

## What survives
The 1612 Paris printing and the 1624 London translation, both digitized. The genuine Flamel record: his 1416 will, notarial documents, the house he built in 1407 at 51 rue de Montmorency (Paris's oldest surviving stone house, with its inscribed façade), and his 1410 tombstone carved with Christ, Peter and Paul, now at the Musée de Cluny. The Innocents charnel arch was demolished before 1612 — the text's central physical claim was unverifiable at the moment of publication. Fragments attributed to the Innocents arch are reported in Paris collections. Nothing resembling the "Book of Abraham the Jew" has ever been produced, and no medieval witness to it is known.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Villain's *Histoire critique de Nicolas Flamel et de Pernelle sa femme* (1761) is the founding debunking and identified the publisher as author. Robert Halleux's *Les textes alchimiques* (Brepols, Typologie des sources du Moyen Âge occidental, 1979) is the standard scholarly treatment of alchemical pseudepigraphy and places the Flamel corpus among the seventeenth-century fabrications. Nigel Wilkins's *Nicolas Flamel: Des livres et de l'or* (Imago, 1993) reconstructs the documentary Flamel from Parisian archives. The consensus, in the encyclopedic formulation: Flamel was real and may have dabbled in alchemy, but his standing as an author and immortal adept is an invention of the seventeenth century.

## What a solution would have to do
Two questions, of very different difficulty. For the surviving question — who wrote the 1612 text — a solution must identify the author documentarily (a privilege, a censor's record, a bookseller's account, a manuscript in a known hand) or by a stylometric attribution against the corpus of Paris alchemical prints of 1590–1620 with proper controls, reporting a held-out test. For the *Figures* to be rehabilitated as medieval, a claimant would need a pre-1600 manuscript witness, and would have to explain the 1416 will, the absence of any alchemical reference in Flamel's documented life, and the convenient prior demolition of the arch. Any reading of the "hieroglyphical figures" themselves must first state which object it reads — the 1612 woodcuts, or the destroyed arch — since these are not the same evidence and the text asserts the identity it needs to prove.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: a working consensus exists (seventeenth-century fabrication) with minority dissent, and the residual popular mystery is not a scholarly one; not 1 because the real authorship of the 1612 text is genuinely unidentified.
- material 4: both early printings digitized, plus an unusually rich documentary record for the historical Flamel and two surviving carved objects; the one thing missing is the only thing that would matter, a pre-1600 witness.
- solvable 2: the best evidence favours fabrication, so there is no fourteenth-century intended meaning to recover; the determinate question that remains is a 1612 attribution.
- compute 3: stylometric attribution across the digitized French alchemical print corpus is a real and undone move, and Gallica's holdings make it feasible; it cannot decide alone.
- verifiable 3: a documented attribution would be accepted by scholarly consensus within years; a stylometric result alone would not close it.
- crowding 3: a modest scholarly literature (Villain, Halleux, Wilkins) against an enormous popular one; the 1612 attribution question has had little serious attention.
