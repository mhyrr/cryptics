# The Sola Busca tarot and its iconographic programme (c. 1491)

## What it is
The earliest completely surviving 78-card tarot deck, engraved on metal in the late fifteenth century — usually dated about 1491 and placed in Ferrara or Venice — and the first deck with fully illustrated pip cards rather than plain suit symbols. Trumps carry Roman numerals; suit cards carry Arabic numerals. Almost uniquely among early tarots, the figures are *named*: the trumps and several pip cards bear legends identifying classical, Roman and biblical persons and some names not obviously identifiable. The complete hand-coloured deck was bought by the Italian Ministry of Cultural Heritage in 2009 for €800,000 and is in the Pinacoteca di Brera, Milan; thirty-five uncoloured impressions of individual cards survive elsewhere, in the Albertina in Vienna, the British Museum, and collections in Hamburg and Paris. The engraver is unidentified; an attribution to Nicola di maestro Antonio d'Ascoli has been proposed.

## What is unsolved
Why these figures, in this order, with these attributes. The naming is the crux: a deck that labels its trumps with specific historical persons is asserting a programme, and no source has been identified that accounts for the selection, the sequence or the iconographic details. Subsidiary open questions: who commissioned it and for whom, which city produced it, who engraved it, and whether the names are keys to an allegorical scheme (dynastic, moral, military, alchemical) or an antiquarian display of learning with no systematic content.

## What survives
Excellent objects, thin scholarship. The complete coloured deck at Brera, digitized by the museum; the thirty-five uncoloured sheets in four other collections, which matter because they show the engraved state before colouring; and photographs commissioned by the British Museum in 1907, which are themselves historically important because they are widely believed to have influenced Pamela Colman Smith's illustrated pips for the Rider–Waite deck. There is no critical catalogue of the deck in English by an academic art historian known to this entry; the fullest treatment is a facsimile with commentary.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Sofia Di Vincenzo's facsimile and commentary proposed that the imagery encodes European alchemical and hermetic doctrine, reading figures such as Alexander and Olympias as alchemical operations. Peter Mark Adams's *The Game of Saturn* (2017) argues for a deliberate, transgressive Graeco-Egyptian and Saturnine cult programme; it is a claimant work, argued from iconographic resemblance, and has not been taken up by art historians. Art-historical work has concentrated on the engraver's identity, the deck's place in Ferrarese and Venetian printmaking, and its influence on later card design rather than on the programme. There is no accepted reading. The honest state of the question is that a deliberately named, sequenced set of figures exists and nobody has matched it to a source.

## What a solution would have to do
This is a source-identification problem with an unusually crisp success criterion, which is why it is worth listing despite the thin literature. A solution would have to: (a) produce a fifteenth-century text or image cycle — a chronicle, a *Uomini famosi* cycle, a Valerius Maximus or Plutarch compilation, a genealogical or military treatise, a court entertainment — containing the same persons, ideally in the same order; (b) account for *all* the named figures, including the ones that do not correspond to well-known classical persons, since the easy names (Alexander, Nero, Cato) can be matched by any number of sources and the hard ones are diagnostic; (c) explain the attributes, not just the names — what each figure holds and does, which is where a real source match will show itself; (d) be consistent with the numbering, since the trump order is the structural claim the deck makes; and (e) fit the material evidence on date, place and patron, so that the proposed source was actually available at that court. Iconographic resemblance to a doctrine, without a text, is what the existing claimant readings offer and is not sufficient. The concrete computational move, which does not appear to have been attempted: assemble the full list of card legends as a named-entity set and search it against digitized fifteenth-century Italian and Latin corpora and catalogues of *Uomini famosi* cycles for a co-occurring set, scoring matches against a null model of arbitrary classical name-lists.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the programme is genuinely unexplained and a sourced reading would matter to Renaissance print and card history and to the historiography of tarot; not 5 because the stakes are contained.
- material 4: a complete deck plus uncoloured impressions in four collections, digitized; short of 5 because there is no critical catalogue and no verified open facsimile URL was pinned here.
- solvable 4: naming specific persons and numbering them is strong internal evidence of a determinate programme; not 5 because the selection could be antiquarian display rather than a scheme.
- compute 3: a named-entity sweep against digitized fifteenth-century corpora is a real and apparently untried contribution; the judgement about what a match means stays humanistic.
- verifiable 3: a source text listing the same figures in the same order would very likely convince the field; nothing weaker would.
- crowding 4: only a handful of serious treatments, no academic monograph on the programme known to this entry, and the main published readings are claimant work — this is among the emptier fields in the catalog.
