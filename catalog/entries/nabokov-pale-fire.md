# Nabokov, Pale Fire — the authorship question inside the novel

## What it is
A novel published in 1962 by G. P. Putnam's Sons, written 1960–61 in Nice and Montreux. It consists of a foreword, a 999-line poem in heroic couplets called "Pale Fire" attributed to the (fictional) American poet John Shade, a commentary many times the length of the poem, and an index — the last three by Shade's neighbour and self-appointed editor Charles Kinbote, an émigré academic who believes himself the exiled king of a country called Zembla and reads the poem as a coded account of his own life. The poem is 999 lines, not 1,000; Kinbote says the missing line would have repeated line 1. The index cross-references create a navigable structure that can be read non-linearly.

## What is unsolved
Who, inside the fiction, wrote what. Three positions have been held in print: that Kinbote is Shade's invention, that Shade and his poem are Kinbote's invention, and that both are personae of a third character, the Russian émigré scholar V. Botkin, who appears only in the index. The question is whether Nabokov built a determinate answer into the text — a set of clues that resolve it — or whether the oscillation is the design. The catalog admits the case because there is documentary evidence of a determinate authorial answer and a designed clue structure, not merely a critical taste for ambiguity; it is retained partly as a calibration case for "open by design" verdicts.

## What survives
The printed novel, stable across editions, plus Nabokov's working notes and index cards, his correspondence, the Véra Nabokov papers and the Berg Collection materials at the New York Public Library, and Nabokov's own interviews and *Strong Opinions*. The text is in copyright, which constrains open digitization but not scholarly access; machine-readable copies exist for research. Brian Boyd's archival work in the biography and in *Nabokov's Pale Fire: The Magic of Artistic Discovery* (1999) reports the relevant notes.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Mary McCarthy's 1962 review set the terms by treating the book as a puzzle with recoverable structure. Andrew Field and Page Stegner advanced the Kinbote-as-sole-author reading; Boyd argued at length first for Shade as the author of everything, then revised to a position involving Shade's ghost and Hazel Shade's spirit influencing Kinbote — a public change of mind that is itself evidence about how tractable the question is. Pekka Tammi's narratological work, Michael Wood's *The Magician's Doubts* and Priscilla Meyer's *Find What the Sailor Has Hidden* represent the main alternatives. The Botkin identification has documentary support: Nabokov is reported to have said in 1962 that the commentator "is Professor Botkin, or Botkine, a Russian and a madman," and the index plants the name. Most scholars nonetheless treat the global question as designed to remain in oscillation, with the local Botkin identification accepted and the "who invented whom" frame regarded as a category error.

## What a solution would have to do
Distinguish three claims. (1) *Botkin is Kinbote*: this is close to settled and rests on the index entry plus Nabokov's reported statement; confirming it needs only a verified citation of that statement in the Nabokov papers. (2) *One character invented the other*: a claim here would have to point to textual evidence that discriminates, not evidence consistent with both — for instance a fact in the commentary that the commentator could not know unless he wrote the poem, or a demonstrable difference or identity of idiolect between poem and commentary that survives the objection that Nabokov wrote both. A stylometric comparison of the poem against the commentary against Shade's quoted prose is a concrete, runnable test that would at least show whether Nabokov differentiated the voices measurably; it decides nothing about invention but would constrain claims that rest on "the same voice." (3) *The question has a determinate answer at all*: this requires external evidence — a note, a plan, a letter — from the working papers. Absent (3), a reading is a reading. Any claim must also account for the index, which is the most obviously engineered part of the book: the cross-reference graph is a designed object and its structure (cycles, dead ends, the entries for Botkin, Zembla and Kinbote) is computable and has not been exhaustively analysed as a graph.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: resolved in outline — Botkin — with a real and persistent residue about the global frame; a solution would matter inside Nabokov studies and to narratology, not beyond.
- material 4: the text is complete and stable and the working papers survive and have been used by Boyd; short of 5 because the novel is in copyright and the archive is not openly digitized.
- solvable 3: Nabokov's reported statement and the index's engineering are real evidence of a determinate design, but the field's majority reads the oscillation as intended; both positions are serious.
- compute 3: idiolect comparison of poem versus commentary and graph analysis of the index are well-posed, cheap and apparently incomplete; they cannot settle an authorial-intent question.
- verifiable 2: "correct" here means a critical consensus about a fiction; competing readings will survive any computation, and only an archival note could raise this.
- crowding 1: sixty years of a large, sophisticated Nabokov industry, with the leading scholar having published two incompatible answers.
