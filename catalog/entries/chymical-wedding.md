# Chymische Hochzeit Christiani Rosencreutz (The Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosenkreutz)

## What it is
A German allegorical romance published anonymously at Strasbourg in 1616, the third of the Rosicrucian manifestos after the *Fama Fraternitatis* (Kassel, 1614) and the *Confessio Fraternitatis* (Frankfurt, 1615). It narrates, in the first person and over seven days, Christian Rosenkreutz's summons to a royal wedding at a castle: the weighing of the guests, the beheading of the royal pair, the voyage to the Tower of Olympus, the distillation of the bodies, the revival of the king and queen, and the narrator's investiture as a Knight of the Golden Stone and his apparent punishment at the end. The action is dated 1459. Johann Valentin Andreae (1586–1654), later an orthodox Lutheran theologian, acknowledged the work in his autobiography.

## What is unsolved
What it is for. Andreae called the *Chymische Hochzeit* — and Rosicrucianism generally — a *ludibrium*, a word that can mean a joke, a lampoon, a stage-play or a plaything, and in *Menippus* (1617) said he had written it in his youth, around 1605; whether that is a disavowal under pressure from his clerical position, a genuine description of a satire, or a deliberately ambiguous cover is the central dispute and has been for a century. Downstream: whether the seven days encode a specific alchemical operation, a Lutheran spiritual itinerary, a satire of alchemists, or a political-utopian program; what the embedded devices (the weighing, the inscriptions, the riddle of the invitation, the counted guests) are doing; and whether the fraternity described existed in any form.

## What survives
The 1616 Strasbourg first edition, complete, digitized and in many copies; Ezechiel Foxcroft's English translation of 1690, which is itself a historical document of English reception; Richard van Dülmen's modern German critical edition; and Joscelyn Godwin's modern English translation with commentary by Adam McLean. The *Fama* and *Confessio* are equally available, and Andreae's very large acknowledged corpus of Latin and German works — utopian, satirical and theological — is largely in print and increasingly digitized, which matters because it is the control set for any claim about his intent. This is the best-documented text in this category after *Atalanta fugiens*.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Frances Yates's *The Rosicrucian Enlightenment* (Routledge, 1972) made the political-Palatinate reading famous and is still the entry point, though its central thesis is heavily qualified by later work. John Warwick Montgomery's *Cross and Crucible* (Nijhoff, 1973) is the major study of Andreae as a Lutheran, and reads the work within that frame. Carlos Gilly's bibliographical work (Bibliotheca Philosophica Hermetica, especially *Cimelia Rhodostaurotica*, 1995) reconstructed the manuscript circulation of the manifestos before print and is the documentary foundation. Roland Edighoffer's *Rose-Croix et société idéale selon Johann Valentin Andreae* (2 vols., 1982–87) is the standard monograph on Andreae's thought. Consensus: Andreae wrote it; the *ludibrium* statement is real and its force is contested; the Tübingen circle around Christoph Besold and Tobias Hess is the likely milieu of the manifestos; no fraternity has ever been documented.

## What a solution would have to do
Resolve the *ludibrium* question from evidence rather than preference. Concretely: date the composition. The 1605 claim can be tested — Andreae's dated works from 1605 to 1616 are extant, and a stylometric and lexical comparison against them, with controls, would say whether the 1616 text is a youthful composition lightly revised or a 1616 work, which is the single fact that most constrains the interpretive options. Second: establish which alchemical literature the seven days draw on, by aligning the text against the digitized German and Latin alchemical corpora of 1550–1615; a reading that claims the work encodes an operation must name the source of the operation. Third, for any interpretive claim: account for the ending, in which the narrator is apparently condemned to be doorkeeper, and for the satirical episodes at the weighing — a reading that produces a coherent initiation and discards the comedy has read half the book. Any claim about the fraternity requires a document, not an inference from the manifestos to their own subject.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the author's own characterisation of his work is the disputed evidence, which is an unusually sharp and genuinely open question with reach into Reformation history, the history of utopia and the history of alchemy.
- material 5: complete printed text, digitized, modern critical edition and translations, plus the author's large acknowledged corpus as a control set.
- solvable 3: Andreae certainly intended something determinate, but "a joke" and "a coded program" are both live scholarly positions and the author's own statement is what makes them both live.
- compute 3: stylometric dating against Andreae's own corpus and a source sweep against the alchemical corpus are both real, well-posed and largely undone; but the corpus is early-modern German and Latin with uneven digitization, and the interpretive question would survive either result.
- verifiable 2: a dating result would constrain the debate without ending it; "what it means" would remain contested by plausibility, which is the honest score.
- crowding 2: a large and distinguished literature — Yates, Montgomery, Gilly, Edighoffer, Brecht — plus a very large esoteric ecosystem; not 1 because the stylometric dating and the source alignment have not been done.
