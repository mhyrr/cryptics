# Carmen Saliare (the hymn of the Salian priests)

## What it is
The chant of the Salii, the "leaping priests" of Rome, sung in their processions for Mars and Quirinus while they danced in archaic armour. The college was held to predate the Republic and to go back to the reign of Numa Pompilius, and the words they sang were already archaic and largely unintelligible to Roman antiquarians. It survives only in quotation: thirty-five fragments, of which the three longest are preserved by Varro in *De Lingua Latina* in the first century BCE, with further material in Terentius Scaurus' *De orthographia* (second century CE) and smaller pieces in Festus and Macrobius.

## What is unsolved
What the hymn said. This is a case of a text that was already opaque to the people transmitting it: the ancient authors who quote it "may have misunderstood and erroneously recorded passages of the song," so the transmitted letters are not reliably the words, and the words are in a stage of Latin for which there is almost no comparative material. The standing assessment is that the original text "may have become so diluted that it is irrecoverable." What is open, then, is not a single crux but the whole: the segmentation of the quoted strings into words, the morphology of those words, the identity of the divine names in them, and whether the fragments belong to one hymn or several.

## What survives
Thirty-five fragments, most of them a few words long, embedded in grammatical and antiquarian prose whose own manuscript transmission has had four more centuries to corrupt them. B. Maurenbrecher's *Carminum Saliarium reliquiae* (1894) established the numbering by which fragments are cited. George Hempl provided linguistic analysis in 1899–1900. The most recent substantive advance is methodological rather than interpretive: Sarullo and Taylor (2013) went back to digital scans of the manuscripts of the quoting authors to re-establish what the transmitted letters actually are. That is the right move for this text, and it points at where the remaining evidence is — in the codices of Varro and Scaurus, not in the hymn.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The consensus is that the text is not recoverable as a text, and the field has largely stopped trying to produce continuous translations. Work has shifted to the individual words that can be tied to something — divine epithets, the vocabulary of the *Semones*, apparent imperatives — and to the manuscript question of what the quoting authors wrote. Maurenbrecher 1894 remains the reference collection; Sarullo and Taylor 2013 is the current state of the transmitted text. Comparison with the Carmen Arvale, the one archaic Roman carmen that survives as a complete inscription, is the only internal control the Latin tradition offers.

## What a solution would have to do
Given that the wall here is the transmission rather than the language, a defensible result has to work backwards through it. (1) Establish the transmitted letters first: a reading must state which manuscripts of Varro, Scaurus, Festus or Macrobius it rests on and what those manuscripts have, which is the job Sarullo and Taylor began and which should be completed for all thirty-five fragments before any interpretation is offered. (2) Segment explicitly: because the quoting authors sometimes misdivided the words, a proposal must present its segmentation as a hypothesis with alternatives, not as the text. (3) Derive every word form from a reconstructible Proto-Italic or archaic Latin shape by a stated sound law, and count the forms it cannot derive. (4) Be consistent with the ritual context reported for the Salii — a procession hymn to Mars and Quirinus — and say what the proposed content would have been for. (5) Accept a ceiling: for this text, a rigorous demonstration that a given fragment cannot be recovered from the transmitted letters is a result of the same standing as a reading, and should be published as one. The catalog's honest expectation is that the Carmen Saliare ends in a precisely named wall, and that the wall is in the manuscripts of the grammarians.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: it would matter substantially to Latin historical linguistics and to the reconstruction of archaic Roman religion, and it is wide open; not higher because almost nobody expects it to be answered.
- material 2: thirty-five short fragments at second hand, corrupted in transmission by authors who did not understand them. It sits near the rubric's material floor, and would be a 1 if not for the three longer Varronian passages.
- solvable 3: there was certainly a determinate hymn, but the best evidence favours the position that its wording is unrecoverable from the surviving witnesses, which is the rubric's "lost referent" territory. A 4 would overstate what the evidence supports and a 2 would deny that a real text stood behind it.
- compute 2: nothing statistical inside the question and no corpus to sweep; the useful computational contribution is image processing and collation of the grammarians' manuscripts, which is a philological aid rather than a solution.
- verifiable 2: readings cannot be checked against anything; a manuscript collation result can be, which is why the score is not 1.
- crowding 3: a modest literature going back to Maurenbrecher, with the obvious approaches long since tried and the field's attention elsewhere.
