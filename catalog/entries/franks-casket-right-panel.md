# Franks Casket, right-hand panel: the Hos/Ertae inscription and its encrypted vowels

## What it is
A small whalebone box, 22.9 × 19 × 10.9 cm, carved in Northumbria in the first half of the eighth century, with five carved faces each bearing figural scenes and runic or Latin inscriptions. It surfaced in the nineteenth century at Auzon in the Haute-Loire; the casket is British Museum 1867,0120.1, and the right end panel — separated from the rest and only identified as belonging to it in 1890 — is in the Museo Nazionale del Bargello, Florence, with a cast in London. The other panels carry identifiable subjects: Romulus and Remus, the sack of Jerusalem by Titus, the Adoration of the Magi, and on the lid an episode of the Germanic archer Egil. The right panel does not.

## What is unsolved
Two linked problems. First, the scene: three groups — a horse or horse-headed creature and a seated figure beside a mound, a beast, and three figures at right — match no story anyone has been able to fix. Second, the inscription, which is the only part of the casket written in a substitution cipher. R. I. Page's transliteration reads *herhos(?) sitæþ on hærmberge / agl? drigiþ / swa hiri ertae gisgraf / særden sorgæ / and sefa tornæ*, translated by him as "Here Hos sits on the sorrow-mound; she suffers distress as Ertae had imposed it upon her, a wretched den (?wood) of sorrows and of torments of mind." *Herhos* may be one name (Hos) or two words (*herh* "sacred grove/temple" + *os* "divinity"); *Ertae* is otherwise unattested; *agl* and *særden* are uncertain. Page's own verdict on the panel is "What the scenes represent I do not know."

## What survives
The object itself, complete in five faces and photographed to museum standard; the bottom is lost. The inscription is intact and legible as letters — the difficulty is not wear but the cipher and the vocabulary. In the cipher, three vowels are represented consistently by three invented symbols, and two further invented symbols stand for *a* and *æ*, which, in Page's words, leaves it "not clear which is which or even if the carver distinguished competently between the two." The substitution is confined to this panel; the other inscriptions use ordinary Anglo-Saxon runes and, on the Magi panel, Roman letters. The editions of record are Page's *An Introduction to English Runes* (2nd edn 1999) and Leslie Webster's *The Franks Casket* (British Museum Objects in Focus, 2012).

## Prior attempts and current consensus
At least eight substantive published readings of the right panel exist, and the consensus is that none is accepted. Wadstein (1900) and Eleanor Clark (1930) proposed Sigurd and the horse Grani with Guthrun; Arthur Napier said in 1901 that he remained "entirely unconvinced," and that has remained the field's attitude to every candidate since. Bouman (1965) and d'Ardenne (1966) proposed Hengist and Horsa with Renwein. Krause (1959) and Alfred Becker (1973, 2002, 2023) read *herh* + *os* and construct a pagan scene of a slain warrior, a valkyrie and Sleipnir, with the two trefoils between the horse's legs as *valknútr*. Peeters (1996) proposed the madness of Nebuchadnezzar; Howlett (1997) the death of Balder from Saxo; Schwab (2008), after Eichner (1991), the penance of Rhiannon from the Welsh *Mabinogi*; Simmons (2010) Satan and the Nativity. Webster and the British Museum accept the lid as Egil but do not adopt a reading of the right panel. A newcomer should read Page on the runes, Webster on the object and its programme, and Becker as the fullest sustained attempt.

## What a solution would have to do
It must do four things at once, which is why nothing has succeeded. (1) Fix the cipher: state which invented sign stands for which vowel and show that the assignment produces good Northumbrian Old English throughout, not only in the words the reading depends on. (2) Explain *Ertae* as a name, a title or a common noun with an attested cognate, and *herhos* as either one word or two, consistently with (1). (3) Identify the scene from a narrative attested before or near 800 in which the same configuration occurs — a mounded burial, a seated mourner, a horse or horse-headed being, a beast, and a group of three — and account for the beast and the trefoils, which most readings pass over. (4) Explain why this panel and no other is enciphered, in terms of the casket's overall programme: the other four faces pair a Roman, a Jewish, a Christian and a Germanic subject, so a reading that leaves the fifth thematically unrelated has not finished. A proposal that satisfies (1)–(3) but not (4) would still be a real advance; a proposal that satisfies none but produces a striking story is what the field already has eight of.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: a famous, genuinely open crux at the centre of the best-known object of Anglo-Saxon art; a solution would matter to Old English studies, runology and art history together.
- material 2: the object is superb but the text is about twenty words and the scene is one image; there is no second witness and no analogue.
- solvable 4: the carver plainly encoded something determinate — a cipher and a named agent presuppose a referent — so the question is recoverability, not existence.
- compute 2: the cipher space is trivially small and does not need a computer; an iconographic corpus sweep across Insular and Germanic art is conceivable but the decisive parallel may not survive.
- verifiable 2: with twenty words of text there is nothing to predict; competing readings would remain unless an exact narrative parallel turned up.
- crowding 2: eight or more named published readings across 125 years plus a substantial art-historical literature; not 1 because the object still attracts serious new work.

## Unverified claims
- Which three vowels receive dedicated invented symbols, and what the symbols look like or derive from, is not stated in the source consulted; only the fact that three vowels are so treated, plus two further signs for *a*/*æ*, is verified.
- Whether Page or Webster offers any explanation of *why* the carver enciphered this panel was not established; the source consulted gives none.
- The date of the casket ("first half of the 8th century") and the Northumbrian attribution are the museum's and Wikipedia's; no independent dating study was consulted.
- The interpretation of the two trefoils as *valknútr* is Becker's claim, reported not endorsed.
- That the bottom of the casket is lost is from memory of the object's description and was not confirmed in the sources fetched.
