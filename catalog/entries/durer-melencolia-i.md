# Dürer, Melencolia I (1514)

## What it is
A copperplate engraving by Albrecht Dürer, 24 × 18.8 cm, dated 1514 and existing in two states — the first with a reversed 9 in the magic square, corrected later. A winged female figure sits among scattered instruments of geometry and carpentry: compasses, a sphere, a millstone, a plane, nails, a pair of scales, an hourglass, a bell, a crucible, a ladder, a truncated rhombohedron now called "Dürer's solid," a sleeping dog, a putto writing on a slate, a 4 × 4 magic square on the wall, a comet and a rainbow over a bay, and a bat carrying a banderole lettered MELENCOLIA I. The magic square is the first printed in Europe; every row, column and diagonal sums to 34, and the two central cells of the bottom row read 15 and 14, giving the date. It is one of Dürer's three *Meisterstiche* and survives in many impressions in the major print rooms.

## What is unsolved
What the assemblage means as a program — whether the objects, the square, the polyhedron and the "I" instantiate a specific doctrine that can be named and sourced, or whether the print is designed to resist that. The concrete sub-questions: what the "I" after MELENCOLIA denotes; why this particular set of attributes and not another; what the truncated rhombohedron is doing and by what construction it was derived; and whether the whole is a self-portrait of the artist's condition, a therapeutic talisman, an emblem of geometry's limits, or a compendium with no single key. Admission is on the strength of the magic square: a Jupiter square of demonstrably correct construction, adjusted so that the bottom row yields the date, is a purposeful, non-random, decodable element, which establishes that the print contains at least one determinate encoded item.

## What survives
Excellent material. Many impressions of both states are in public collections (Metropolitan Museum, British Museum, National Gallery of Art, Albertina, Kupferstichkabinett) with high-resolution images online; the Google Art Project scan is on Wikimedia Commons. Dürer's own writings — the *Vier Bücher von menschlicher Proportion*, the *Underweysung der Messung* (1525) with its polyhedral nets, the Netherlandish diary, the letters to Pirckheimer — survive and are published, as do the preparatory drawings associated with the print. The intellectual context (Ficino's *De vita triplici*, Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* in its 1510 manuscript form, the Nuremberg humanist circle around Pirckheimer) is also fully available and digitized.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The founding study is Erwin Panofsky and Fritz Saxl, *Dürers "Melencolia I": Eine quellen- und typengeschichtliche Untersuchung* (1923), expanded with Raymond Klibansky into *Saturn and Melancholy* (1964). Their reading derives the print from the Ficino–Agrippa revaluation of Saturnine melancholy as the temperament of genius, and takes the "I" to mark Agrippa's first of three kinds of melancholic inspiration, *melancholia imaginativa*, the one belonging to artists and craftsmen. That reading dominated the twentieth century and remains the starting point. It has been pressed on several fronts: the "I" has been read as the Roman numeral one of a projected series, as an imperative ("melancholy, begone!"), and as an abbreviation; the polyhedron has generated a small technical literature on which solid it is and how Dürer constructed it; and Joseph Leo Koerner has argued that the print "generates multiple and contradictory readings" by design, functioning as an occasion for the viewer's self-reflection rather than as a rebus with an answer. The honest summary is that the milieu and the general theme are established, the specific program is not, and a significant strand of current scholarship denies that a specific program exists.

## What a solution would have to do
A program claim would have to: (a) name a source text or texts, available in Nuremberg before 1514, that contain the attribute set — not the theme of melancholy in general but this list of objects — and show the correspondence item by item, including the items normally passed over (the millstone, the ladder's rung count, the enema syringe, the bell, the crucible); (b) explain the "I" from that source rather than by analogy; (c) account for the magic square's specific arrangement, since more than one 4 × 4 arrangement gives 34 with 15 and 14 in the bottom row, and the chosen one may carry further information; (d) derive the polyhedron by a construction Dürer himself uses in the *Underweysung*, so that the solid is explained rather than described; (e) be consistent with the two states and with the print's relation to the other two *Meisterstiche*; and (f) meet Koerner's objection head-on by predicting some feature not used in building the hypothesis. A concrete computational move: a systematic parallel sweep of the digitized Latin and German astrological, medical and *Kunstbuch* corpora before 1514 for co-occurrence of the specific attribute set, which is the kind of search that was infeasible when Panofsky worked and does not appear to have been done at scale.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: a central, much-discussed program question is open, and a sourced solution would be a notable art-historical result; not 5 because the general subject is understood and the dispute is about specificity.
- material 5: multiple impressions of both states, fully digitized at high resolution, with the artist's own theoretical writings and the relevant source literature all published.
- solvable 3: the magic square proves at least local encoding, and the print's density argues design; but serious scholars — Koerner above all — hold that the work is constructed to produce contradictory readings, so both positions are live.
- compute 3: corpus-scale parallel search and geometric reconstruction of the solid are real contributions; the decision between a sourced program and designed overdetermination is a humanistic judgement.
- verifiable 3: a single source text matching the attribute set would very plausibly convince the field within years; nothing weaker will.
- crowding 1: among the most written-about prints in existence, with a century of first-rate scholarship and a large esoteric fringe.
