# Nostradamus, Les Prophéties (1555–1568)

## What it is
A collection of quatrains in deliberately obscure Middle French by Michel de Nostredame, physician and almanac-writer of Salon-de-Provence. The first edition, printed by Macé Bonhomme at Lyon in 1555, contains a preface addressed to his son César and three complete "Centuries" plus 53 quatrains of a fourth. A 1557 edition adds further Centuries; the posthumous 1568 edition is the first to present all ten, prefaced additionally by the Letter to King Henry II. Century VII is short and has never been completed. About 942 quatrains are canonical. The language mixes French with Latinate and Greek coinages, Occitan forms, anagrams, astrological terms and syntactic inversion.

## What is unsolved
Not the popular question. Whether the quatrains predict future events is not an open scholarly question — the academic literature says they do not, and the appearance that they do is produced after the fact. What is open, and genuinely unresolved, is the *source* question: for any given quatrain, which text Nostradamus was working from. Scholars have identified sources for a substantial fraction — the *Mirabilis Liber* (1522), Richard Roussat's *Livre de l'estat et mutation des temps*, biblical prophecy, and classical history from Livy, Suetonius and Plutarch recycled as future events, apparently sometimes by a bibliomantic method of opening a book at random — but not for all of them. A second open question is textual: which quatrains are authentic, since spurious editions and forged quatrains circulated from the sixteenth century and no two early printings agree. The entry carries a low `solvable` score deliberately: there is a determinate referent behind many quatrains, but it is a *source text*, not a prophecy, and for a large number of quatrains the referent may be irrecoverable by construction.

## What survives
Many early editions, with serious bibliographic problems. The 1555 Bonhomme printing survives in a small number of copies (the Albi copy is the usual reference), as do the 1557 and 1568 printings, and there are numerous later, altered and outright forged editions. Facsimiles and digitized copies exist through Gallica and the Bibliothèque municipale de Lyon; the standard modern critical work is Pierre Brind'Amour's edition and commentary of the first Centuries, and Bernard Chevignard's edition of the almanacs and presages. Machine-readable transcriptions circulate widely on enthusiast sites and are unreliable; the critical text is the one to work from. The almanacs and presages, which are dated and specific, are the best control corpus for understanding the method.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The scholarly tradition — Pierre Brind'Amour, Bernard Chevignard, Peter Lemesurier, Elmar Gruber — has established the source-based account and demolished the standard popular translations, which are frequently shown to have "little or no knowledge of sixteenth-century French" and to have been adjusted to fit the events they claim to predict. James Randi's critique named the mechanism: "retroactive clairvoyance," fitting words to events after the fact. Lemesurier's point that no quatrain has ever been interpreted in specific terms *before* the event it is claimed to describe is the operative test and has not been met. Against this stands an enormous claimant literature, continuously productive and essentially unaffected by the scholarship. The field's position is therefore not "unsolved" but "solved in the wrong direction from what the public believes": the interesting residue is philological.

## What a solution would have to do
Two different programmes, with different bars. (1) *The source programme*, which is tractable: for each quatrain, produce a candidate source passage with verbatim or near-verbatim lexical overlap, from a text demonstrably available to Nostradamus, and report coverage honestly — what fraction of the 942 quatrains are sourced, at what confidence, with a stated similarity threshold and a null model showing that the matches are not what a random 16th-century corpus would yield. This is the obvious large-scale computational move: the relevant corpora (the *Mirabilis Liber*, Roussat, Livy, Suetonius, the Chronicle of Nuremberg, French chronicles) are digitized, and a systematic alignment sweep of the whole quatrain corpus against them does not appear to have been published at scale. It would also settle authenticity questions, since a forged quatrain should show a different source profile. (2) *The prophetic programme*: a claim that the quatrains encode future events would have to be stated as a prediction, in advance, in terms specific enough to be falsified, and registered before the event. Nothing else counts, and nothing else has ever been offered. Any reading published after the event it explains is scored here as zero evidence.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: a working consensus exists among scholars, with a live and incomplete source-identification project as the real residue and a very large popular dissent that the catalog does not weight.
- material 4: the early editions survive and are digitized with critical editions available; short of 5 because the textual tradition is corrupt and the authentic corpus is itself disputed.
- solvable 2: determinate referents exist for many quatrains but they are sources, not predictions; the "prophetic" meaning is unrecoverable by construction, which is the definition of this band.
- compute 4: a mass source-parallel sweep over digitized sixteenth-century corpora is exactly the kind of move the axis rewards, the data is largely ready, and it appears not to have been done comprehensively; not 5 because the critical text has to be prepared first and transcription quality is poor.
- verifiable 3: a verbatim source match is checkable and a consensus on sourcing could form; the prophetic question is not verifiable at all, so the axis is split and scored in the middle.
- crowding 1: five centuries of attempts, a permanent commercial industry, and every naive approach exhausted.
