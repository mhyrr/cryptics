# Berg, Lyric Suite — the secret programme (composed 1925–26, recovered 1977)

## What it is
A six-movement string quartet by Alban Berg, composed 1925–26, published with a dedication to Alexander Zemlinsky and for fifty years understood as abstract chamber music with an unusually charged expressive surface. It is in fact a concealed programme work: Berg wrote it about his affair with Hanna Fuchs-Robettin, encoded her initials and his own as pitches (A–B♭ and B♮–F in German note-naming, giving Alban Berg and Hanna Fuchs), built the movements around his personal numbers 23 and her 10, and wrote the finale over a suppressed vocal setting of Baudelaire's "De profundis clamavi" in Stefan George's translation. He gave Hanna Fuchs a copy of the first edition annotated throughout with the programme and told nobody else.

## What is unsolved
Nothing material. The entry is in the catalog with `status = "solved"` as a calibration case, and it is the most instructive one available for the `musical` kind. Before 1977 the Lyric Suite looked exactly like the Enigma Variations looks now: a work with a widely felt but unlocatable hidden meaning, about which capable analysts (H. F. Redlich among them) had written that there was a "concealed vocality" without being able to say what it was. The hidden programme was real, it was fully determinate, and it was recovered — not by analysis, but because George Perle found the annotated score in 1977 and Douglass M. Green then decoded Berg's cryptic marks to identify the Baudelaire setting. The lesson the catalog should draw is that for a hidden musical programme the decisive evidence was a document, and that even excellent analysts working on the notes alone had not got there in fifty years.

## What survives
The published score and Berg's manuscripts; the annotated first-edition copy given to Hanna Fuchs, which is the key document; Berg's correspondence, including the material relating to the Fuchs-Robettin family; and the reconstructed vocal version of the finale, which has been performed and recorded. The work is in copyright and there is no open digitization; the scholarly record is in Perle's publications and in the Berg literature. As evidence this is a complete chain: a concealment, a key written by the concealer, and an independent second decoding step.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Analysts before 1977 described the work's expressive trajectory and noted its vocal-sounding final movement without identifying a programme; Redlich's phrase about concealed vocality is the standard example. George Perle published the discovery of the annotated score in 1977, and Douglass M. Green identified the Baudelaire text from Berg's notations. The programme is now universally accepted and is standard in programme notes and recordings. No serious dissent exists, because the evidence is the composer's own annotation. Berg's use of letter-pitch ciphers and numerology elsewhere — the Chamber Concerto, the Violin Concerto, *Lulu* — is also well documented, which is why the discovery was immediately credible.

## What a solution would have to do
Stated retrospectively, as calibration: what convinced the field was (a) a document in the composer's hand, addressed to the person the programme concerns, stating the programme movement by movement; (b) a cipher that is arbitrary enough not to be reachable by guesswork (A–B♭/B♮–F for two specific names) but mechanical enough to be checked once stated, so that every occurrence of the motif can be verified in the score; (c) numbers (23, 10) that recur in measurable quantities — bar counts, metronome marks, structural divisions — and can therefore be counted rather than intuited; and (d) an independent second find, the Baudelaire text, which was predicted by the annotations and then confirmed, i.e. the programme made a successful prediction about something not used to build it. Any claim about a hidden programme in another work — Elgar's "larger theme" is the obvious comparison — should be measured against this standard: a document, a mechanically checkable cipher, countable quantities, and a confirmed prediction.
