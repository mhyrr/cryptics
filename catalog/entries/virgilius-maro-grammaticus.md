# Virgilius Maro Grammaticus, Epitomae and Epistolae

## What it is
Two Latin grammatical works — the *Epitomae* in twelve books (possibly fifteen originally) and the *Epistolae* in eight — written in the seventh century by an author who calls himself Virgilius Maro and whom the manuscripts and the later tradition call Virgil the Grammarian or Virgil of Toulouse. Computistical evidence in Irish texts places him before 658. The works are formally modelled on Donatus and the late-antique grammarians, but their content is unlike anything else in the tradition: Virgil describes twelve kinds of Latin, of which only one is in ordinary use; he cites dozens of authorities who either never existed or are misquoted, including several further Virgils and several Vulcans; he reports grammarians disputing for a fortnight over the vocative of *ego*, and works with titles such as *De laudibus indefunctorum*, "In praise of the undead"; and he describes and uses a procedure of *scinderatio fonorum*, the deliberate splitting and scrambling of words, presented as a technique for concealing meaning from the unworthy.

## What is unsolved
What the works are. The candidate answers in the literature are mutually exclusive: (1) serious, if eccentric, grammatical teaching from a milieu whose norms we have lost; (2) sustained parody or satire of the grammatical tradition; (3) a cryptographic or initiatory game, in which the *scinderatio* and the invented authorities are apparatus for hiding content; (4) a Jewish or Hebrew-influenced work, on the strength of the Hebrew traces some scholars find, possibly indicating a converted or crypto-Jewish author; (5) Vivien Law's reading, in which the oddity is neither ignorance nor simple parody but a considered position — "a veiled plea for diversity and variety" — about language and authority. Subsidiary but load-bearing: the author's origin (Ireland, Gaul, Spain, the Basque country), and whether the *scinderatio* passages actually conceal recoverable text or only describe a possibility.

## What survives
About twenty manuscripts from the eighth to the eleventh century, mostly from early-ninth-century France, with one written at Tours and the majority from German centres; the *Epistolae* are much more poorly represented than the *Epitomae*. Three critical editions: Johannes Huemer (1886), Giovanni Polara with an Italian translation (1979), and Bengt Löfstedt (2003), the most recent. Vivien Law's *Wisdom, Authority and Grammar in the Seventh Century: Decoding Virgilius Maro Grammaticus* (Cambridge, 1995) is the essential modern study; Michael Herren has written on the language and the Hiberno-Latin affiliations. No open digital critical text was located; Huemer (1886) is out of copyright and likely available in scanned form, but no verified link was obtained.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The tradition of reading Virgil has swung from treating him as a fool or a forger, through the nineteenth-century view that he was an incompetent, to the modern position that the strangeness is deliberate and systematic. There is no consensus on genre. Law's *Decoding Virgilius Maro Grammaticus* is the closest thing to a field-defining treatment and argues for a coherent intellectual project rather than a joke; the parody reading remains widely held; the cryptographic reading is a minority position that has never been tested against the whole corpus. The Irish-milieu hypothesis is supported by the early Irish computistical citations and by the works' Hisperic affinities; the Hebrew traces are reported and disputed. What is agreed: the works were read and taught — twenty manuscripts and citations by Aldhelm and later grammarians attest that — so whatever they are, a seventh- and eighth-century readership took them seriously enough to copy them.

## What a solution would have to do
The decisive, and so far untried, test is on the *scinderatio fonorum*. Virgil states rules for splitting and rearranging words; those rules can be implemented and run backwards over the whole corpus. A cryptographic reading has to show that applying the stated rules to the passages Virgil marks as scrambled yields coherent Latin, and that applying them to unmarked passages does not — i.e. the method must discriminate, and the discrimination must be measured against a null model, not asserted. If the rules do not yield text, the cryptographic hypothesis is dead and the field gains a real negative result.

Beyond that: (1) a genre claim must account for both works, including the *Epistolae*, and for the proportion of the text that is ordinary grammar, which any "it's all a joke" reading has to explain; (2) it must handle the invented authorities systematically — a census of the citations, sorted into genuine, distorted-genuine, and invented, with the distortions characterised, would show whether the fabrication is random, patterned, or targeted at specific real authorities; (3) an origin claim must rest on datable linguistic or citational evidence, not on the *Toulouse* of the later title, whose authority is itself uncertain; (4) the Hebrew-traces claim must identify the specific forms and rule out their transmission through Jerome, Isidore or the glossaries, which is where a seventh-century Latin author would ordinarily meet Hebrew.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the text is edited and largely intelligible; what is open is what kind of thing it is. That is a real and unresolved question with a substantial residue of dispute, but not a wide-open central mystery.
- material 4: about twenty manuscripts, three critical editions, a substantial and complete-enough text. Not 5 because the *Epistolae* transmission is thin and there is no open digital edition.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold "serious grammar," "parody," and "coded" positions, which is the rubric's definition of 3.
- compute 3: the *scinderatio* is genuinely computational and the citation census is a tractable corpus task; but the central question — what the author was doing — is a judgement about intent that no computation settles. If the *scinderatio* test came back positive, this would be a 5.
- verifiable 3: the *scinderatio* sub-question is mechanically decidable; the genre question would be settled, if at all, by scholarly consensus.
- crowding 4: a handful of serious treatments — Huemer, Polara, Löfstedt, Law, Herren — and no crowd; the corpus-level tests described above appear undone.
