# Newton Stone: the ogham and the unidentified six-line inscription

## What it is
A standing stone 203 cm high, 50 cm wide and 26 cm thick, found in 1803 at Pitmachie Farm near Old Rayne in Aberdeenshire by the 4th Earl of Aberdeen and later moved to the grounds of Newton House at Culsalmond, where it stands. It is conventionally dated to the sixth or seventh century. It carries two inscriptions: an **ogham** inscription of about twenty-five letters running along a natural ridge of the stone, and — on the face — a **six-line inscription of about forty-three characters in an alphabet nobody has identified**, in an insular-looking but non-runic, non-ogham, non-standard script.

## What is unsolved
Both inscriptions, though for different reasons. The ogham is legible as ogham letters but does not yield an agreed word-division or language: Skene (1865) read mixed Gaelic and Welsh forms; G. F. Browne (1921) read it as the memorial of "Aed, king or ruler of Fortren"; neither has become standard, and the Pictish ogham corpus generally resists linguistic interpretation. The six-line inscription is the harder problem: the script has been called Phoenician (Mill, 1863), Greek or Latin (Graves, 1885), Hebrew or Sanskrit (Moore, 1865), Old Gaelic (Bannerman, 1907, connecting it to St Moluag), and "debased Roman cursive," which is the position most scholars have settled for without producing a reading. Kelly A. Kilpatrick (2021) argued that it resembles late-antique informal writing, comparing it to wax tablets and lead curse tablets, and proposed a Pictish origin with the stone as a memorial. No reading of the six lines is accepted. A further open question is authenticity: some scholars have suspected the face inscription of being a modern addition, and Gordon (1956) defended it on the ground that the carving technique matches Pictish Class I symbol stones.

## What survives
The stone itself, in the open air, worn; no second witness and no companion inscription in the same script anywhere. The inscription has been drawn, rubbed and photographed repeatedly since the 1860s — and the history of those records is part of the problem, since nineteenth-century drawings differ from one another and each decipherment tends to depend on one of them. The Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Scotland (now Historic Environment Scotland / Canmore) holds the survey record. No open high-resolution or RTI dataset was located, and no machine-readable transcription of the six-line inscription exists in a form independent of a particular reading.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The Newton Stone has the longest record of failed decipherment of any object in this category: from the 1860s to the present, in a sequence — Phoenician, Hebrew, Sanskrit, Greek, Gaelic — that tracks the enthusiasms of each generation rather than any accumulating evidence. That pattern is itself the main diagnostic finding, and it is why this entry sits beside the Kensington Runestone as a calibration case: a short inscription in an unknown script with no parallel will generate a decipherment in whatever language the decipherer already knows. The current position is that the script is probably a debased or informal Roman cursive of late-antique type, the stone is probably genuine and Pictish, and no reading is accepted. Kilpatrick (2021) is the most recent comprehensive analysis and the necessary starting point.

## What a solution would have to do
(1) **Fix the letterforms first.** Publish an RTI and photogrammetric survey of both inscriptions and a diplomatic transcription made from it, with a per-character confidence, and reconcile it against the nineteenth-century drawings, stating where they were wrong. Every reading so far has been built on a drawing; until this step is done, no decipherment is testable and the field cannot even agree on the input.
(2) **Settle authenticity before meaning.** Compare the tooling of the six-line inscription with the ogham on the same stone and with dated Pictish Class I carving, by measurement, not by eye. If the face inscription is nineteenth-century, the problem dissolves; the entry's `solvable` score is set at 3 rather than 4 precisely because this has not been excluded.
(3) **Identify the script by inventory.** With forty-three characters there is enough for a sign inventory and a repetition profile. Compare that profile against Roman cursive of the fourth to seventh centuries, insular majuscule and minuscule, ogham, the *futhorc*, Greek and the invented and ornamental alphabets of insular manuscripts, using a stated similarity metric and a chance baseline. "Debased Roman cursive" should become a measured claim or be abandoned.
(4) **Respect the length limit.** Forty-three characters cannot support a long reading. Any proposal must be a short formula — a name, a patronymic, a *hic iacet* — and must show that its language's onomastics and formulae fit sixth- or seventh-century Aberdeenshire. A reading that produces a sentence of doctrine or a genealogy from forty-three characters is, on its face, over-fitted.
(5) **Use the ogham as a control.** If the two inscriptions are contemporary and commemorate the same person, the ogham constrains the face inscription's content. Test whether the ogham's length is compatible with the name a face-reading proposes, before proposing it.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: a genuinely unread inscription on a genuine early monument, which would matter to Pictish studies and to the history of writing in Britain; not higher because the recoverable content is at most a name and a formula.
- material 2: one worn stone, two short inscriptions, no parallel in the script, and a record polluted by inconsistent nineteenth-century drawings.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both that this is a genuine early inscription with determinate content and that the face text may be a modern addition; until the authenticity question is closed, 3 is the honest score.
- compute 3: the letterform-inventory comparison and the imaging are genuinely computational and undone; but forty-three characters is too little for statistical decipherment, so computation can constrain and eliminate, not decide.
- verifiable 2: with no parallel text and no bilingual, competing readings would remain; only the authenticity question and the script-identification question admit sharper answers.
- crowding 4: 160 years of amateur and antiquarian attempts but only a handful of serious scholarly treatments (Gordon 1956, Kilpatrick 2021) and no modern imaging programme. The field is thin where it matters.
