# The Thunder, Perfect Mind (Nag Hammadi Codex VI,2)

## What it is
A short revelation discourse, the second tractate of Nag Hammadi Codex VI, found in the 1945 Nag Hammadi discovery and preserved in Sahidic Coptic. Its Coptic title is ⲧⲉⲃⲣⲟⲛⲧⲏ: ⲛⲟⲩⲥ ⲛ̄ⲧⲉⲗⲉⲓⲟⲥ (*tebrontē: nous n̄teleios*). The text is a sustained first-person monologue of paradoxical self-predications — "I am shameless: I am ashamed. / I am strong: and I am afraid" — alternating with direct address to the hearer. The speaker presents as feminine. Scholars generally hold that it was composed in Greek, on the evidence of its metre and phrasing, with composition estimates ranging from the first or second century to about 350 CE.

## What is unsolved
Everything about the frame, while the words themselves are readable. Who is speaking and under what name; what the title means and how its grammar works, since the feminine *brontē* ("thunder") stands beside the masculine *nous teleios* ("perfect mind"); what genre the text belongs to and therefore how its antitheses are meant to be taken; and what tradition produced it. The serious possibilities on the table are an Isis aretalogy in the Egyptian tradition, a Jewish wisdom discourse in the line of Proverbs 8 and Sirach 24, a Gnostic revelation of a divine female figure, and a text constructed so that no single referent can be assigned — in which case the "unsolved" status is the answer rather than a gap.

## What survives
One complete-enough witness: the tractate as copied in Codex VI, with the codex dated to the middle of the fourth century. The Nag Hammadi corpus is fully published with photographic facsimiles and has standard critical editions and translations; for this tractate the reference points are George MacRae's edition and translation in the Coptic Gnostic Library and in Robinson's *Nag Hammadi Library in English* (1990 edition), Bentley Layton's translation in *The Gnostic Scriptures* (1987), Paul-Hubert Poirier's French edition and commentary in the Bibliothèque copte de Nag Hammadi, and Hal Taussig and collaborators, *The Thunder: Perfect Mind: A New Translation and Introduction* (2010). The 1973 Berkeley colloquy protocol on the tractate is also in print. The text is short, fully transcribed and widely available; the Coptic is machine-readable through the Coptic corpus projects.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is no consensus on classification, and the dissent is on record. George MacRae and Hal Taussig both argue against calling the text Gnostic. Patricia Cox Miller reads it as the self-revelation of a powerful goddess. The parallels to Egyptian Isis aretalogies and to Jewish wisdom literature are the two standing comparanda, and the current assessment is that both are "less compelling than they may initially seem" — which is the honest state of the question: the text resembles several genres and belongs securely to none. Taussig and his collaborators' 2010 volume is the fullest recent treatment and reads the paradoxes as doing social and rhetorical work rather than encoding a doctrine. Poirier's commentary is the most detailed philological treatment.

## What a solution would have to do
A genre or provenance claim here is testable in a way that most interpretive claims are not, because the comparanda are finite and digitized. It would have to: (i) exhibit a control corpus — the Greek and demotic Isis aretalogies, the Jewish wisdom poems, the "I am" revelation discourses in the Nag Hammadi and Hermetic corpora, the self-predications in the Greek magical papyri — and show that the Thunder's formal features (the antithesis rate, the syntax of the self-predication, the address formulae, the lexical field) fall inside one of those distributions and outside the others, with the measurement reported and reproducible; (ii) account for the whole text, including the passages that do not fit the favoured genre, rather than the quotable antitheses; (iii) explain the double title and its gender mismatch on the reading proposed; (iv) state what the reading predicts about the tractate's placement in Codex VI alongside its neighbours. A reading that asserts the text is deliberately aporetic must equally be held to (i) and (ii): it should show that the antitheses resist referential assignment systematically, not selectively, which is a measurable property.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: a real and unresolved interpretive question that matters to the study of ancient religion and of female divine figures; not 4 because nothing about the text's words is in doubt.
- material 4: a substantially complete tractate in a securely dated codex with facsimiles, multiple critical editions and machine-readable Coptic; not 5 because there is one witness, with lacunae, and the Greek original is lost.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both that the text belongs to a determinate tradition and that its paradox is the point; that split is exactly what a 3 means.
- compute 4: the decisive question is a mass source-parallel problem over corpora that are already digitized, and no quantitative comparison of the Thunder against its candidate genres appears to have been done. Not 5 because the final judgement about what a genre match means stays interpretive.
- verifiable 2: competing readings would survive any result; the field has not converged in fifty years.
- crowding 3: a modest but real literature (MacRae, Poirier, Miller, Taussig et al.) and the obvious comparative approaches have been tried by hand.
