# Lapis Niger cippus inscription (CIL I² 1)

## What it is
A tufa cippus carrying the oldest substantial Latin inscription from Rome, found by Giacomo Boni during his excavation of the Comitium in the Roman Forum between 1899 and 1905, beneath the black paving stones (the *lapis niger*) that the Romans themselves regarded as a sacred and inauspicious spot associated with Romulus. The inscription dates to about 570–550 BCE and is written boustrophedon in vertical columns. The stone is still on site in the Forum, protected under a modern concrete and marble cover since the early twentieth century.

## What is unsolved
What the inscription says, because most of it is gone. The block was cut down, probably when the Comitium was repaved: "the beginning and end are missing and only one third to one half of each line survives." What can be read are isolated words in an archaic Latin whose morphology is itself reconstructed: `sakros` ("sacred", or "accursed"), `esed`, `sord`, `recei` (a dative, "for the king" — one of the earliest Latin attestations of *rex* in Rome), `kalatorem` (a herald or summoner), `iouxmenta` (yoke-animals), `iter` (a way or procession), `loiuquiod`. From these the genre is inferred, not read. So the open questions are: what class of document this is (a *lex sacra*, a curse formula protecting a precinct, a regulation of a procession, a boundary text), what act it prohibits or prescribes, who the *rex* is (the king, or the later *rex sacrorum*), and what its relation is to the monument complex it stood in.

## What survives
One damaged witness, in situ, with roughly half of each of its lines lost and no duplicate, no ancient quotation, and no parallel text in the same formula from Rome. It is published as CIL I² 1 and appears in every handbook of archaic Latin; photographs, squeezes and drawings exist in print. No open high-resolution or 3D documentation of the stone was found in this pass, which matters more here than in most entries, because several disputed readings rest on whether particular strokes are letters at all.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The standing consensus, stated by Michael Grant among others, is that the text "clearly represents a piece of ritual law," with the opening words plausibly a warning that whoever damages, defiles or violates the place will be cursed — an inference from `sakros` in its archaic sense. Georges Dumézil connected the text to augural practice and the *iuges auspicium*, the omen taken from yoked animals. Robert E. A. Palmer read it as early law governing ritual violation and the fines for it. Beyond "archaic ritual law involving a *rex*, a herald, yoke-animals and a route," the field has not converged, and the supplements printed in the handbooks differ from one another at almost every point.

## What a solution would have to do
This is the catalog's clearest case of a text whose meaning is limited by the stone rather than by method, so the falsifiability bar has to be set against that. A supplement is not a reading unless it (i) fits the measured line lengths and the boustrophedon column layout, letter count by letter count, on the surviving stone; (ii) draws every supplied word from attested archaic Latin or from a reconstruction with an independent warrant, rather than from later legal Latin; (iii) matches a formula type attested elsewhere in Italic *leges sacrae* — the Iguvine Tables, the *lex* of the Luceria grove, the Spoletium *lex luci* — and says which; (iv) explains why each surviving word occupies the position it does. A claim that goes further and names the occasion, the *rex*, or the monument's dedicatee is by construction unverifiable from this stone and should be labelled a hypothesis about early Rome that uses the inscription, not a reading of it. The one thing that would genuinely breach the wall is physical: high-resolution RTI or photogrammetric imaging of the surviving surface, published openly, to settle which marks are letters before anyone argues about words.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the earliest Roman public document and a direct witness to regal-period institutions; open, and it would matter to Roman historians, but only a fraction of the text can ever be at stake.
- material 2: a single badly truncated witness with a third to a half of each line lost. This is the entry that sits closest to failing the rubric's material test; it is kept because a real fraction of the text is legible and the words that survive are institutionally informative, not because the whole is recoverable.
- solvable 4: it was certainly a determinate legal or ritual prescription, publicly inscribed to be obeyed.
- compute 2: there is no corpus to sweep and nothing statistical inside the question; at most, constrained supplement generation against Italic formula templates, which is a minor aid to a judgement that stays humanistic.
- verifiable 2: a supplement can be shown to fit or not fit the physical line lengths, which is more than nothing, but no supplement can be confirmed.
- crowding 2: a century of attention from capable people (Boni, Dumézil, Palmer, Coarelli) with every naive approach exhausted; not 1 because it has not attracted a crank ecosystem.

## Unverified claims
- The exact number of surviving lines and the letter-by-letter transcription, including whether `loiuquiod` or `loiquod` is correct; the handbooks differ and no photograph was consulted here.
- The identification of the *recei* as the regal-period king rather than the *rex sacrorum*.
- Filippo Coarelli's reconstruction of the Comitium complex and its bearing on the inscription; cited from memory and not verified.
- Whether any RTI, photogrammetric or 3D documentation of the cippus has been published; none was found, but the absence was not confirmed with the Parco archeologico del Colosseo.
