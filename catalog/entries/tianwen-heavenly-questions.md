# Tianwen (Heavenly Questions), Chu Ci — the unanswered questions

## What it is
A poem of 172 questions put to Heaven, preserved as the third piece in the *Chu Ci* (Songs of the South), the anthology of Chu-region poetry assembled in Han times around a core traditionally ascribed to Qu Yuan. It is written in four-character lines, unlike most of the anthology, and its vocabulary sits closer to wisdom literature such as the *Daodejing* than to the rest of the *Chu Ci* — evidence that the questions draw on older material, oral or otherwise, whatever hand gave them their final shape. It is the densest single source for pre-Han Chinese mythology: cosmogony, the shape of the sky, the nine heavens, the flood, Yu and Gun, Yi the archer, the ten suns, Nü Wa, the fates of the Xia, Shang and Zhou houses.

## What is unsolved
What most of the questions are asking about. Each is a one- or two-line allusion to a story the poet assumed his audience knew — "the nine heavens, who ranged them? / who first built them?" — and for a large fraction of them the story is gone. Wang Yi's Han commentary and the later commentarial tradition supply answers where parallel texts survive; where they do not, the commentators guess, and their guesses have been repeated as data for two thousand years. A second, compounding problem is textual: the sequence appears disordered in places, lines are suspected of transposition or corruption, and the poem's questions do not obviously organize into the topical blocks editors have imposed on them.

## What survives
The text, complete, in the *Chu Ci* transmission with Wang Yi's *Chu ci zhangju* commentary and Hong Xingzu's Song supplement; it is freely available in machine-readable Chinese at the Chinese Text Project. David Hawkes's *The Songs of the South* (Penguin, revised 1985) is the standard English version with notes on the allusions; Stephen Field's *Tian Wen: A Chinese Book of Origins* (New Directions, 1986) is a dedicated translation and commentary. Wen Yiduo's twentieth-century work on the *Chu Ci* and on Chinese mythology is the foundation of the modern Chinese scholarship. The comparative material — the *Shanhaijing*, *Huainanzi*, *Shujing*, *Zuozhuan*, the *Mu Tianzi zhuan*, and a growing body of excavated Warring States and Han manuscripts from Guodian, Mawangdui, Shuanggudui and elsewhere — is where the referents have to come from, and much of it is now digitized.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The tradition from Wang Yi onward treated the poem as Qu Yuan's, written in exile, and read the questions as rhetorical reproaches. Modern scholarship (Hawkes, Wen Yiduo, Field) treats the ascription as doubtful and the poem as a compilation over older sources, and reads the questions as genuine catechetical or ritual questions rather than rhetorical ones — possibly a mnemonic scaffold for a body of myth transmitted by specialists. The consensus is that many allusions are recoverable only by parallel-hunting, that new excavated manuscripts have added referents and will add more, and that a residue is simply lost. Nobody claims a complete reading. For a newcomer: Hawkes for the text and the frame, Field for the line-by-line, Wen Yiduo for the mythographic method.

## What a solution would have to do
The realistic unit of success is a question, not the poem, and this entry exists partly to record that the total solution may not be achievable in principle. A credible contribution would: (1) state, for each of the 172 questions, whether its referent is (a) attested in an independent pre-Han or Han text, (b) attested only in the commentarial tradition, which is not independent evidence, or (c) unattested — a triage that has never been published in that explicit form; (2) for newly proposed referents, cite the parallel text and show that the allusion's distinctive vocabulary, not just its topic, matches; (3) address the line order, since a proposed reading that depends on re-sequencing must justify the re-sequencing on metrical or rhyme grounds; (4) distinguish rigorously between Wang Yi's guesses and Warring States knowledge, which the received commentaries do not; and (5) not treat the *Shanhaijing* as a neutral answer key, since its own date and relation to the *Tianwen* are contested. The wall is nameable in advance: for questions whose myth left no other trace, only new excavated manuscripts can help, and no amount of analysis will substitute.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: partly resolved — a substantial fraction of the allusions are identified — with a large residue that is opaque because its mythology is lost, not because it is hard.
- material 4: the poem survives complete with an early commentary and is fully digitized; not 5 because textual disorder and suspected corruption are real and the comparative corpus is what is actually thin.
- solvable 2: the referents certainly existed and were determinate, but for many questions they are unrecoverable by construction — the rubric's "lost referent" case. This is the axis that keeps the entry honest.
- compute 3: a systematic allusion-matching sweep across the digitized pre-Han and Han corpus, including excavated manuscripts, is the only method that can add referents at scale, and it has not been run; it stays at 3 because the limiting resource is new text, not new analysis.
- verifiable 3: when a referent is found in an independent text the identification is checkable and consensus does form — this is how Wen Yiduo and Hawkes worked — so individual answers are genuinely adjudicable.
- crowding 3: two thousand years of Chinese commentary, but the modern mythographic program is a small field, the Anglophone literature is two books, and no systematic corpus sweep has been attempted.

## Unverified claims
- The question count. Wikipedia gives 172; other counts circulate (roughly 170–180 depending on how compound questions are divided), and no edition was consulted to fix it.
- Bibliographic details for David Hawkes, *The Songs of the South* (Penguin, rev. 1985), Stephen Field, *Tian Wen: A Chinese Book of Origins* (New Directions, 1986), and Wen Yiduo's *Chu Ci* studies are cited from memory and were not pinned to catalog records in this pass.
- The specific claim that lines are transposed or corrupt in identifiable places, and the existence of a scholarly consensus on which ones, was not verified; it is reported from the general characterization of the poem as "fragmentary."
- Whether any excavated manuscript has in fact resolved a previously opaque *Tianwen* question. This is plausible and often asserted but no specific case was verified here.
- Whether Wang Yi's commentary is complete for the *Tianwen* and how much of it is transmitted intact.
