# Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit

## What it is
The earliest substantial alchemical work in German and one of the earliest illustrated alchemical treatises anywhere: an early fifteenth-century treatise that fuses the alchemical work with Christian mysticism, reading the passion, death and resurrection of Christ and the Trinity as the pattern of the transmutation. It is attributed to a Frater Ulmannus, a German Franciscan. Its drawings — crucifixions with alchemical attributes, coupled royal figures, shields and coats of arms, the Trinity — are the ancestors of much later alchemical iconography: several reappear in the twenty woodcuts of the printed *Rosarium philosophorum* of 1550, more than a century later. It circulated among German princes precisely because it made alchemy religiously respectable.

## What is unsolved
Who Ulmannus was; whether he is the author or a redactor; the precise date and the occasion, which has been connected with the Council of Constance (1414–18) and with a Hohenzollern or Nuremberg patron; and whether the work's Christ-as-lapis argument is a theological claim, an allegorical code for a metallurgical process, or a deliberate identification of the two. The illustrations carry their own problem: the Berlin archetype's drawings are not identical across witnesses, and the direction and mechanism of their transmission into the later German picture cycles — the *Rosarium*, and beyond it *Splendor Solis* and the *Rosarium*'s descendants — has been asserted more often than demonstrated figure by figure.

## What survives
Four fifteenth-century manuscripts of the original version: Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, Cod. 78 A 11 (the archetype, 1410–1419, with the drawings); Heidelberg, Cpg 843 fasc. 3; Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cgm 598 (after 1467); and St Gallen, Kantonsbibliothek, VadSlg Ms. 428 (1488). A 1433 revision made for Johannes von Bayreuth survives in Dresden N 110 (1492) and Gotha, Landesbibliothek, Ch. B. 254 (seventeenth century), with further copies. The Heidelberg and Munich manuscripts are digitized by their libraries; the Berlin archetype's imaging status should be checked. There is no complete critical edition — one was reported in preparation as of 2009 — and no machine-readable transcription. Uwe Junker's 1986 study is the principal modern treatment of the text and its transmission.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Wilhelm Ganzenmüller's 1956 essay opened the modern study; Herwig Buntz's 1972 work and Uwe Junker's *Das "Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit" in seiner zweiten, alchemistischen Fassung* (1986) are the standard treatments of the text and its two versions. Barbara Obrist's *Les débuts de l'imagerie alchimique* (1982) is the indispensable study of the illustrations and of the emergence of alchemical imagery in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; it is Obrist who situates this manuscript as the origin point of the tradition. The consensus: an early fifteenth-century German Franciscan compilation, genuinely the first of its kind, of great importance for the iconography, with authorship and occasion unresolved and no full edition to argue from.

## What a solution would have to do
Edit it. A critical edition collating all six or more witnesses, distinguishing the original from the 1433 revision, would be the first-order result and would be judged by ordinary philological criteria. Alongside it, an image-lineage study: register the drawings across the witnesses, then align them against the later cycles — the 1550 *Rosarium* woodcuts above all — and demonstrate the descent figure by figure, with the intermediate witnesses named. This is checkable image to image, and it would settle a claim that the whole field currently repeats on the strength of a handful of resemblances. For authorship and occasion: documentary evidence tying Ulmannus to a house, a patron or the Council; a dialect analysis locating the text; and a source identification against the earlier Latin alchemical corpus, which would date the compilation from below. For the register question, a reading must account for the Christological argument as an argument, not translate it away — and must explain why a Franciscan wrote it at all.

- mystery 3: genuinely open on authorship, date and register, and consequential for the whole history of alchemical imagery; the stakes are within medieval German studies and the history of alchemy.
