# Dante's prophecies: the Veltro (Inf. I) and the DXV (Purg. XXXIII)

## What it is
Two deliberately cryptic prophecies in the *Commedia*, universally treated as a pair. In *Inferno* I 100–111 Virgil tells the pilgrim that the she-wolf who blocks his way will mate with many beasts until a *veltro* — a greyhound — comes to kill her: he will feed not on land or money but on wisdom, love and virtue, "e sua nazion sarà tra feltro e feltro," and he will drive avarice back to Hell. In *Purgatorio* XXXIII 37–45 Beatrice prophesies that "un cinquecento diece e cinque, / messo di Dio" — five hundred, ten and five, i.e. DXV — will slay the whore and the giant. Dante marks both passages as riddles: Beatrice explicitly tells the pilgrim her words are dark and will be understood in time.

## What is unsolved
Who, if anyone, the two figures are. For the *veltro*, the identifications proposed across seven centuries include: an astrological configuration or a new age (Iacopo della Lana, Iacopo Alighieri); a just sovereign, emperor or pope (Bambaglioli, Guido da Pisa); Christ, or Christ at the Last Judgement; an emperor who restores peace, possibly identical with the DXV (Pietro Alighieri); Cangrande della Scala (Vellutello); Uguccione della Faggiuola (Troya, 1826); Henry VII of Luxembourg; Pope Benedict XI; and Dante himself (Ruggiero della Torre, 1887, judged implausible in the standard reference on the ground that Beatrice states no one governs on earth). The subsidiary crux *tra feltro e feltro* has been read as "between heaven and heaven," as the two places Feltre and Montefeltro, and as cloth — humble birth between two pieces of felt; Boccaccio admitted he did not know what it meant. For the DXV, Iacopo della Lana first read the numeral as an anagram of *DVX*, "leader"; the dominant modern line since Vellutello and Daniello, given its authoritative form by E. G. Parodi, is Henry VII, argued from the parallel with Revelation 13:18 (where 666 is a cipher for a ruler), from a 1312 dating, and from Henry's sacred imperial mission; Lombardi, Tommaseo and Zingarelli preferred Cangrande; Davidsohn read 515 as the year 1315 and so as Louis of Bavaria; Chistoni and Tripepi read it christologically; Kaske and Sarolli, independently, read DXV as a Christomimetic monogram formed from the V and D of *Vere dignum* in the canon of the Mass joined by a cross.

Also unresolved: whether the two prophecies point to the same figure, and whether the *Epistola* to Cangrande and the *Monarchia* constrain the answer.

## What survives
Everything. The *Commedia* is transmitted in some 800 manuscripts, has a modern critical edition (Giorgio Petrocchi, 1966–67; more recently Federico Sanguineti and the Lachmannian debate around it), is fully digitized and machine-readable, and carries a continuous commentary tradition from the 1320s to the present — Iacopo Alighieri, Iacopo della Lana, the Ottimo, Guido da Pisa, Pietro Alighieri, Boccaccio, Benvenuto da Imola, and on. The commentaries themselves are digitized and searchable in the Dartmouth Dante Project; the Princeton Dante Project hosts the Petrocchi text with translation and notes. The *Enciclopedia Dantesca* (Treccani) entries *veltro* and *cinquecento diece e cinque* are the standard surveys of the debate and are online.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
This is the rare case where the evidential situation is perfect and the question is still open — which makes it a useful control on the whole rubric. On the *veltro*, the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* reports that scholarship of recent decades has largely stopped naming a person and sorted itself into three camps: an emperor or imperial vicar (Pascoli, Nardi, Barbi, Singleton, Sarolli), a reforming pope (D'Ancona, Torraca, Bonaiuti), and an unidentified virtuous reformer (Pietrobono, Vallone, Sapegno). Its own verdict is that the identification is unresolved but the function is clear: an eschatological figure "identifiable, plausibly, with Christ of the final judgement and with his great imperial precursor," and that what matters is his office — to purge the world of avarice — rather than his name. It stresses that Dante himself may never have fixed on a person, citing his uncertainty about timing at *Purgatorio* XX 13–15. On the DXV, the same reference work reports continuing debate while treating Parodi's Henry VII as the most authoritative reading. The prophecies also carry a warning for this catalog: the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* lists a century of propagandists who applied the *veltro* to figures Dante could not have known — Charles IV, Luther, Garibaldi, Victor Emmanuel II, Wilhelm I, Hitler, Mussolini. A puzzle with perfect evidence and no verification criterion attracts exactly this.

## What a solution would have to do
Any identification must satisfy constraints that are, unusually, well specified by the text itself. (1) It must fit the chronology of composition: *Inferno* I was written before Henry VII's expedition and death (1313), *Purgatorio* XXXIII around or after it, so a single figure common to both prophecies must be available at both dates, and a proposal must state whether Dante revised. (2) It must construe *tra feltro e feltro* — a reading that identifies the *veltro* and leaves the line "obscure" has not finished the job, and this is where almost every candidate fails. (3) It must explain why Dante chose the numeral form DXV rather than a name, and why *veltro* rather than an emblem of the candidate's house; if the DVX anagram is invoked, the proposal should show Dante using anagram or letter-play elsewhere. (4) It must square with the rest of the *Commedia*'s political statements and with *Monarchia*, rather than with one passage. (5) It must accommodate the possibility, which the standard reference now favours, that there is no person to find — and a reading that argues for that must explain the numeral's specificity, since DXV is not a natural way to say "somebody." Verification here can only be scholarly consensus, and the seven-hundred-year record suggests it will not form. The most valuable achievable result is negative and computational: a systematic census of the commentary tradition, which is digitized, establishing exactly which identifications were available when, which arguments have actually been answered, and which constraints every surviving candidate violates.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the question is open and famous, but the field has partly resolved it in outline by concluding that the *office* is clear and possibly that no person was intended; this is "resolved in outline with a real residue of dispute."
- material 5: the best evidential situation in the catalog — some 800 manuscripts, a critical edition, full digitization, and seven centuries of digitized commentary. The constraint is not evidence.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both that Dante encoded a specific person and that he deliberately left the figure open; the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* leans to the latter.
- compute 2: the commentary tradition is searchable and a census of proposals and arguments is worth doing, but the question is one of authorial intent under perfect information, which is a humanistic judgement. There is no statistical or combinatorial core.
- verifiable 2: only by plausibility. Barring a new document — a letter, a contemporary gloss with authority — competing readings would remain, which is why the debate has not closed in 700 years.
- crowding 1: a continuous scholarly literature since the 1320s, hundreds of named proposals, and an active tradition of politically motivated claimants. This is the most picked-over item in the category.
