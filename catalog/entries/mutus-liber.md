# Mutus Liber (La Rochelle, 1677)

## What it is
A printed alchemical book with essentially no text: a sequence of engraved plates (commonly counted as fifteen) showing a man and a woman collecting dew on cloths, working sealed vessels, tending furnaces, and attending angelic and astrological scenes, with biblical chapter-and-verse references and scattered Latin tags but no discourse. The title page names "Altus" as the author and "Jacob Saulat, Sire des Marez" as its *inventor*; both names are fictions. Only a few dozen copies were printed at La Rochelle in 1677 by Pierre Savouret; twelve are reported to survive in Western libraries.

## What is unsolved
What operation the plates instruct. The book is the limiting case of alchemical allegory: it withholds language deliberately, so every element of its meaning — what substance the dew stands for, what the sealed vessels contain, how many stages the sequence has, whether the sequence is even linear — has to be supplied from outside. The secondary open question is authorship: modern scholarship points to Isaac Baulot, a La Rochelle apothecary, with Abraham Thévenin, Elie Bouhéreau and Elie Richard proposed as contributors, but the attribution is inferential.

## What survives
The 1677 La Rochelle edition, complete, in twelve reported copies; C. G. Jung's copy is digitized on e-rara, and the Library of Congress offers a downloadable scan. The plates were reprinted in Jean-Jacques Manget's *Bibliotheca Chemica Curiosa* (Geneva, 1702), which is the version most later readers saw, and again in 1867, 1914, 1943, 1966, 1967, 1991 and 2015. Because there is no text, there is nothing to transcribe; the evidentiary base is the plates themselves plus the biblical citations printed on them, which are a machine-readable index into Scripture and have never been systematically exploited.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Two literatures barely in contact. The claimant tradition: Eugène Canseliet's commentary (editions from 1967) reads the plates as instructions for a specific *magnum opus* beginning with the collection of May dew, and Serge Hutin follows him; both write as initiates and neither offers a falsifiable criterion. The historical tradition: Jean Flouret and Patrick Sembel have placed the book in the Protestant medical and apothecary milieu of La Rochelle and argued the Baulot attribution from local records. Jung used the plates in *Psychology and Alchemy* as evidence of projected psychic contents, which explicitly declines the question of a chemical referent. There is no critical edition and no scholarly monograph devoted to the plates as a sequence.

## What a solution would have to do
Name the substances and the apparatus, plate by plate, from evidence outside the book: seventeenth-century French apothecary and chymical practice, the Baulot circle's own writings, and the plates' own biblical citations, which constrain the intended allegorical register and are the one piece of hard text in the object. Then show the reading is a sequence — that plate *n* follows from plate *n−1* as an operation and not merely as an image — and that it accounts for every plate, including the astrological and angelic ones that claimant readings tend to pass over. A laboratory reconstruction that produced the visible phenomena (the dew-soaked cloths, the residues shown in the flasks) from the named materials would be the strongest available evidence. For authorship: a documentary link between Baulot and Savouret's press, not a shared town.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: genuinely unresolved, but the stakes are confined to the history of alchemy and to the book's small claimant following.
- material 4: the object survives complete and is openly digitized; there simply is no text, which caps what the material can bear.
- solvable 3: the plates show real apparatus in a real order, which argues for a determinate intended operation; but a wordless book may have been unreadable without oral instruction that is gone, and serious readers hold both views.
- compute 3: computation can do two useful things — index and analyse the biblical citations, and match the plates' motifs against the digitized alchemical image corpus — but neither can decide the operation.
- verifiable 2: no mechanical check exists. A lab reconstruction is suggestive, not decisive; competing readings would survive.
- crowding 3: a modest literature plus an energetic claimant tradition; the historical-milieu work is recent and not exhausted.
