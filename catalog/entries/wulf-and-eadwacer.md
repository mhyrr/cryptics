# Wulf and Eadwacer

## What it is
Nineteen lines of Old English alliterative verse, with a refrain and two half-lines that do not alliterate, standing on folios 100v–101r of the Exeter Book (Exeter Cathedral Library MS 3501), copied c. 970–990. A first-person speaker, grammatically feminine, addresses or laments an absent Wulf, names an Eadwacer, and speaks of "uncerne earne hwelp" — "our wretched/cowardly whelp" — being carried off to the woods. Benjamin Thorpe printed it in 1842 as "Riddle I" of the Exeter collection, since it sits immediately before the first riddle; modern editors print it among the elegies.

## What is unsolved
Who the three named or implied persons are and what situation the poem presupposes. Concretely: (1) is Wulf the speaker's lover, husband, brother or son; (2) is *Eadwacer* a second man's name, a byname for Wulf, or a common noun ("property-watcher") addressed to the same man; (3) is the *hwelp* a literal child, an illegitimate child, or a metaphor; (4) does the poem quote a lost Germanic legend (the candidates named in the literature include Signý, the Wayland–Beadohild matter, and a Theodoric/Odoacer pairing) or is it a free-standing lyric whose obscurity is the point. Also unsettled: whether the text is complete, whether it and *The Wife's Lament* are by one author, and whether the last two lines are a riddling coda.

## What survives
One witness only. There is no second copy, no source, and no analogue that names the same characters. The standard texts are Krapp and Dobbie, *The Exeter Book* (Anglo-Saxon Poetic Records III, 1936), Muir's *Exeter Anthology of Old English Poetry* (rev. 2000) and Klinck's *The Old English Elegies: A Critical Edition and Genre Study* (1992). The Exeter Book leaves are photographed and transcribed in the Old English Poetry in Facsimile project (Wisconsin); the poem's text is machine-readable in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus. The manuscript itself has fire and knife damage elsewhere in the codex, but these folios are legible.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Thorpe's own note — "Of this, I can make no sense" — is the honest baseline. Since then the poem has been read as: a *frauenlied* / woman's lament of continental type; a fragment of a lost heroic legend; a riddle whose answer is an object or animal (wolf, dog, millstone, and others have been proposed); a mother's mourning for a son; and a deliberately ambiguous lyric. No reading has become standard, and the standard handbooks describe the poem as the most obscure in the Old English corpus. A newcomer should read Klinck's edition (text, apparatus, survey of readings), Muir's commentary, and Peter Orton or Marijane Osborn on the riddle-reading, then Shiels on the Theodoric/Odoacer proposal.

## What a solution would have to do
Account for all nineteen lines under one construal, not just the two names. Specifically it must: give a single grammatically defensible construal of *Wulf min Wulf*, *uncerne earne hwelp*, *þæt mon huge to*, and the refrain *Ungelic is us*, rather than emending to fit; explain why two half-lines lack alliteration without assuming corruption unless it can independently show corruption; explain the poem's position in the manuscript immediately before Riddle 1, since scribal placement is the only external evidence there is; and, if it claims a legendary referent, produce that legend in a text or a pictorial witness where the same configuration of persons appears — not merely a story with a wolf in it. A reading that decides *Eadwacer* is Odoacer must show the Old English name-form is used that way elsewhere. Because the poem is nineteen lines long, no proposal can be confirmed by prediction of unread material; the achievable bar is a construal that requires no emendation, matches an attested narrative, and is then adopted by the standard editions.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the central question is wide open after 180 years and the poem is canonical in the field; not 5 because a solution would change Old English studies, not the world.
- material 2: nineteen lines, one witness, no analogue. The evidence itself caps what any method can do.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both that a determinate lost narrative lies behind it and that the ambiguity is constitutive.
- compute 2: the one computational move is a corpus sweep for the name-set and the collocations across Old English, Old Norse, Old High German and Latin; the decisive evidence is probably not in any surviving corpus.
- verifiable 2: absent a rediscovered source, competing readings would remain; consensus, not proof, is the ceiling.
- crowding 2: a very large literature and many capable failures, but no crank ecosystem and no exhaustive bibliography of proposals.
