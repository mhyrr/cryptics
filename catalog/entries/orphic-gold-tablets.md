# Orphic / Bacchic gold tablets (lamellae, Totenpässe)

## What it is
Small inscribed leaves of gold, typically a few centimetres across, folded or rolled and placed in graves — one Thessalian example in the Getty is a 26 × 38 mm rectangle. They carry instructions to the dead for navigating the underworld: where not to drink, what to say to the guardians, and who to tell that the rite has been performed. The best-known formulae are "I am a son of Earth and starry sky. I am parched with thirst and am dying; but quickly grant me cold water from the Lake of Memory to drink," and "Now you have died and now you have come into being, O thrice happy one, on this same day. Tell Persephone that the Bacchic One himself released you." They come from graves in Magna Graecia (Hipponion, Petelia, Thurii), Thessaly and Crete, and from as far as Palestine in the second century BCE and later. A related and earlier class, the fifth-century BCE bone tablets from Olbia, carry "short and enigmatic inscriptions" such as "Life. Death. Life. Truth. Dio(nysus). Orphics."

## What is unsolved
Not the readable tablets — those are transcribed and translated — but three things behind them. (1) The hyparchetype: the tablets share formulae in variant, abbreviated and corrupted forms, and what they are variants *of* (a hexameter poem? a ritual script? a memorized text transmitted orally?) is unrecovered. (2) The tablets whose text is not language: several of the Thurii leaves carry sequences of letters that do not resolve into Greek words, and whether these are abbreviations of known formulae, deliberate cipher, magical *voces*, or an illiterate copyist's imitation of writing is an open question. (3) The frame: whether "Orphic" is the right label at all, versus Bacchic, Pythagorean, or a local mystery cult with no single doctrine, and whether the instructions describe a ritual death at initiation, a physical death, or both inseparably.

## What survives
- Several dozen tablets, from at least eight sites, spanning some six centuries; the exact total is not fixed in the sources consulted here (see Unverified claims). Individual tablets are dispersed: the Petelia tablet is in the British Museum, one Thessalian tablet in the Getty, the Hipponion tablet in Italy.
- Günther Zuntz catalogued the material known up to 1971. Fritz Graf and Sarah Iles Johnston, *Ritual Texts for the Afterlife: Orpheus and the Bacchic Gold Tablets* (first edition 2007, second 2013), is the standard English edition with transcriptions and translations of the corpus. Richard Janko has published an English translation of the B-group texts (the Mnemosyne / Lake of Memory group).
- Transcriptions are printed, not machine-readable as a corpus; no single open digital edition of all the tablets was found.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The formulae and the ritual logic are understood: the dead person must avoid Lethe and drink from Mnemosyne's spring, and must make a declaration of divine descent and of initiation. What remains disputed is whether the tablets are fundamentally Orphic or Pythagorean, and whether their primary reference is to initiation ritual or to death; Christian Zgoll has argued for a synthesis in which ritual death and physical death are an inseparable unity. Radcliffe Edmonds has pressed the case that "Orphism" is a modern construct imposed on a scatter of local practices. Graf and Johnston is the entry point; Bernabé and Jiménez San Cristóbal's *Instructions for the Netherworld* is the other standard treatment.

## What a solution would have to do
A reconstruction of the common source would have to be an alignment, not a paraphrase: every tablet's text set against every other's, with each divergence classified as phonetic, dialectal, abbreviating, or scribal error, and the proposed hyparchetype required to generate each attested text by a stated rule. It must account for the short tablets as reductions of the long ones (or the long as expansions of the short) and say which, on the evidence of date and place. For the unintelligible Thurii sequences, a solution must produce a rule that resolves the whole sequence, not a phrase from the middle of it, and the same rule must apply to the other non-lexical passages on the same leaves; "magical letters" is a label, not a reading, and should be scored as an admission that the passage is unread. For the cult label, a claim would have to show that a single doctrine predicts the distribution of formulae across sites, rather than being compatible with it.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the readable texts are read and the ritual is broadly understood; the open questions are real but second-order.
- material 3: several dozen genuine primary objects with published transcriptions, but each is a few lines, the corpus is dispersed across museums, and there is no open digital edition.
- solvable 4: the formulae are demonstrably a transmitted text with variants, which is strong internal evidence that a determinate source existed.
- compute 3: aligning a few dozen short parallel texts to derive a hyparchetype is a well-posed computational task and no published treatment has automated it, but the corpus is small enough that hand collation is nearly adequate; the residue is dialectology and cult history.
- verifiable 3: a stemma that generates every attested text by stated rules could win consensus; the cult-label question could not.
- crowding 2: a large and capable literature (Zuntz, Pugliese Carratelli, Graf and Johnston, Bernabé, Edmonds), with the obvious approaches taken.

## Unverified claims
- The number of tablets. Figures between roughly 20 and 40 circulate depending on what is counted as a tablet and whether the bone tablets and the later Semitic-context leaves are included; no authoritative count was verified here. This is the main reason `confidence` is low.
- The specific claim that Thurii tablets carry non-lexical letter sequences, and which tablet numbers those are. This is widely repeated in the literature but was not confirmed against a citable source in this pass; the score for `solvable` would move if it turned out that every tablet is fully lexical.
