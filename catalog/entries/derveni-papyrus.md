# Derveni Papyrus

## What it is
A charred papyrus roll pulled from the remains of a funeral pyre at Derveni, Macedonia, on 15 January 1962, in a nobleman's grave in the necropolis of ancient Lete. The roll dates to about 340 BCE and copies a text composed in the late fifth century BCE, which makes it the oldest surviving European book; UNESCO entered it on the Memory of the World International Register in 2015. It survives as 266 fragments yielding 26 columns of text, and is held in the Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki. The text is a prose commentary that reads an Orphic theogony allegorically, arguing that the poem "does not mean what it literally says."

## What is unsolved
Three distinct questions, often conflated. (1) Who wrote the commentary: no name survives, and the proposals below are all inferences from doctrine and style. (2) What the commentary's program is — physical allegoresis in the manner of Anaxagoras, a ritual handbook, a defence of Orphic poetry, or a polemic — and whether the eschatological and ritual material in the first columns belongs to the same argument as the cosmological exegesis later. (3) What the Orphic poem itself said: the commentator quotes it in pieces, out of order, and the poem is otherwise lost, so its text, length and sequence must be reconstructed from the lemmata of a commentary whose own column-tops are gone.

## What survives
- The object: 266 charred fragments, 26 columns, Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki. The tops of the columns are lost, so no column is complete. No open digital edition or image set was found; the material is published in print.
- First complete critical edition: Kouremenos, Parássoglou and Tsantsanoglou (2006), in the Studi e testi per il corpus dei papiri filosofici series. Gábor Betegh, *The Derveni Papyrus: Cosmology, Theology and Interpretation* (Cambridge, 2004), gives a reading, critical edition and translation. Mirjam Kotwick's Greek–German edition with commentary appeared in 2017; Glenn Most edited a volume of studies in 2022.
- Richard Janko developed a method of digital microphotography that allowed passages to be read for the first time, and has published alternative reconstructions of the fragmentary columns. The consequence is that the transcription itself is a moving target: columns exist in materially different forms in different editions.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is consensus that the text is an allegorical commentary produced in the intellectual milieu of Anaxagoras, that it treats the Orphic poem as a physical cosmology in disguise, and that it is the most important new evidence for early Greek religion and philosophy found in the modern period. There is no consensus on the author. The editors of 2006 leave the commentary anonymous. Reconstruction of the underlying poem is partial by construction: the commentator quotes only what he wants to explain. A newcomer should read Betegh 2004 for the interpretation, Kouremenos–Parássoglou–Tsantsanoglou 2006 for the text, and Kotwick 2017 for the current state of the Greek.

## What a solution would have to do
For the text: a transcription that accounts for every one of the 266 fragments, with placements justified by fibre continuity, kollesis and column geometry rather than by sense alone, and that survives independent re-imaging. Janko's re-readings show that the current text is under-determined; a solution here is a published, reproducible fragment-placement with its physical evidence, not a new conjectural supplement. For the poem: a reconstruction that accounts for every lemma the commentator quotes, in an order the commentary's own sequence supports, and that is consistent with the Orphic theogonies known from Damascius and the Rhapsodies. For the author: a named candidate must explain the doctrinal mix (Orphic ritual plus Anaxagorean physics), the Ionic prose, the Macedonian find-spot, and the absence of the name from any ancient catalogue of the candidate's works. Absent new external evidence, an attribution can only be a best explanation, and should be labelled as such.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: author unknown, program contested, and the lost poem behind it is a first-order source for Orphism; not 5 because the commentary's broad character is agreed.
- material 3: substantial text, but every column is topless, there is no critical consensus transcription, and no open digitization was found.
- solvable 4: it is certainly a determinate prose argument, and much of it is read; the residue is real but bounded.
- compute 4: the two hard sub-problems, ink recovery from carbonized papyrus and combinatorial fragment placement, are the same problems the Vesuvius Challenge has shown to be tractable; the wall is that the object has not been volumetrically scanned or its images released.
- verifiable 3: fragment placement is checkable physically, but authorship and program would resolve only by scholarly consensus.
- crowding 2: a large dedicated literature (Laks–Most 1997, Betegh, KPT, Kotwick, Most) by capable people; not 1 because no crank ecosystem and the imaging work is fresh.

## Unverified claims
- The named candidates for authorship. Stesimbrotus of Thasos (Burkert), Euthyphron (Tsantsanoglou), Diagoras of Melos (Janko), Epigenes, Brotinus and Prodicus all circulate in the literature; none of these attributions could be confirmed against a citable source in this pass, and the Wikipedia article carries none of them. Treat the whole cluster as reported, not established.
- The number of verses of the Orphic poem recoverable from the lemmata. Figures in the range of 20 to 30 hexameters are commonly given; not verified here.
- Whether the first columns are ritual/eschatological rather than cosmological, and whether the column numbering in current editions is settled.
- Whether any high-resolution images or a digital edition have been released by the Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki.
