# The Wife's Lament

## What it is
Fifty-three lines of Old English alliterative verse on folio 115 of the Exeter Book (Exeter Cathedral Library MS 3501, copied c. 970–990). A speaker marked by feminine grammatical endings recounts exile, the departure of a *hlaford* over the sea, the plotting of his kinsmen, confinement in an *eorðscræf* under an oak, and closes with a generalising statement about a *geomor mod* and a *wine*. It is conventionally grouped with the Old English elegies and compared with continental *frauenlieder*.

## What is unsolved
What situation the poem describes and how many people are in it. The live cruxes: (1) whether the *hlaford*/*freond* and the *mon* whose kinsmen scheme are one man or two; (2) whether the closing lines (42–53) are a gnomic reflection, a prayer, or a curse pronounced on the absent man — Niles argues for a curse; (3) whether the speaker is alive in an earth-dwelling, a cave, a hermitage, or speaking from the grave as a dead voice, for which no Old English parallel is agreed; (4) whether the grammatical femininity is authorial or a copyist's, i.e. whether a male speaker has been feminised in transmission; (5) genre — literal lament, allegory (of the Church, the soul, or Israel), riddle, or charm/curse formula.

## What survives
One witness. Text is good; there are few physical difficulties on the leaf, and the interpretive problem is not damage but syntax and reference. Standard editions: Krapp–Dobbie, *The Exeter Book* (ASPR III, 1936); Klinck, *The Old English Elegies* (1992), which is the edition of record for apparatus and readings; Muir, *The Exeter Anthology*, 2nd edn (2000). Digital images and diplomatic transcription are in the Old English Poetry in Facsimile project; the text is machine-readable in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is no consensus on any of the five cruxes; the poem is routinely called one of the most disputed texts in medieval English studies. Positions a newcomer must know: Stanley B. Greenfield's argument that there is one man, not two, which most later readings either adopt or explicitly reject; Barrie Ruth Straus's speech-act reading, which shifts the question from "what happened" to "what the utterance does"; John D. Niles's reading of the ending as a curse rather than a maxim; and Klinck's edition for the range of construals of each clause. Allegorical readings (soul/Church) had currency in mid-twentieth-century criticism and are now minority. The male-speaker hypothesis is discussed but faces the obstacle that the feminine endings occur in the first two lines where the copyist had least reason to err.

## What a solution would have to do
Deliver a construal of the whole 53 lines in which every clause is grammatically possible without conjectural emendation, and in which the referents of *hlaford*, *freond*, *mon*, *monnes*, *leodfruma* and *wine* are fixed and consistent. It must decide whether lines 42–53 are spoken of the absent man or of a third party and show why the switch to the subjunctive and the gnomic present supports that decision. If it claims an allegorical layer, it must identify the allegorical key from a source the poet could have used, not from thematic resemblance. If it claims the speaker is dead, it must produce an Old English or Anglo-Latin parallel for a posthumous first-person lament. A reading that resolves the two-men problem should also predict something independent — for example that the same construal resolves the corresponding cruxes in *Wulf and Eadwacer* or in Riddle 60 / *The Husband's Message* — so that it is doing work beyond this one poem. Because the text is 53 lines with no external control, the realistic bar is adoption by the standard critical editions, not proof.
