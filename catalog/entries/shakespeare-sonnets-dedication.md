# The dedication of Shakespeare's Sonnets (1609): "Mr. W.H." and "the onlie begetter"

## What it is
A thirty-word dedication on the second leaf of the 1609 quarto *Shake-speares Sonnets*, set in capitals with a full stop after every word in imitation of Roman lapidary inscription: "TO.THE.ONLIE.BEGETTER.OF. THESE.INSVING.SONNETS. Mr.W.H. ALL.HAPPINESSE. AND.THAT.ETERNITIE. PROMISED. BY. OVR.EVER-LIVING.POET. WISHETH. THE.WELL-WISHING. ADVENTVRER.IN. SETTING. FORTH. T.T." The initials T.T. are the publisher Thomas Thorpe's. The dedication is not by Shakespeare.

## What is unsolved
Who "Mr. W.H." is, and what "onlie begetter" means. The two questions are not separable: "begetter" can mean the inspirer of the poems (hence the young man of the sequence), the person who procured the manuscript for Thorpe, or the person who "begot" the book in the sense of financing it. Whether the sonnets were published with Shakespeare's consent turns partly on the same evidence, since Thorpe signing the prefatory matter is usually taken to mean the author was absent.

## What survives
Thirteen copies of the 1609 quarto are reported to survive in good condition (Bodleian, Folger, British Library, Huntington, Rosenbach and others). The dedication leaf is present in all of them and its text is not in dispute; there is no variant reading to argue about. Transcriptions are on Wikisource, Gutenberg and the Internet Shakespeare Editions; page-image facsimiles are available through the Shakespeare Quartos projects. The primary external evidence is the Stationers' Register entry of 20 May 1609, Thorpe's other publications, and the biographical records of the candidates.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The two peerage candidates dominate: William Herbert, third Earl of Pembroke (whose initials fit, who was a dedicatee of the First Folio, and who was the right age) and Henry Wriothesley, third Earl of Southampton (dedicatee of *Venus and Adonis* and *Lucrece*, but whose initials must be reversed to fit). Against both stands the objection that "Mr." is an impossible address for an earl. Commoner candidates include William Hall, a stationer in Thorpe's circle, whose name yields the reading "Mr. W. Hall" if the following word is absorbed; Sir William Harvey, Southampton's stepfather, on the theory that he passed the manuscript on; William Haughton; and Shakespeare's nephew William Hart. Thomas Tyrwhitt and, famously, Oscar Wilde proposed a boy actor "Willie Hughes" from the puns of Sonnet 20; no such person is documented. Bertrand Russell, Jonathan Bate and Donald Foster have argued the initials are a misprint for "W.S." or "W.SH.," i.e. Shakespeare himself. Editors of the standard modern editions treat the question as insoluble on present evidence and decline to adopt any candidate; the honest statement of the consensus is that there is none.

## What a solution would have to do
It would have to be documentary, not onomastic. The space of persons in 1609 London with the initials W.H. is large enough that any candidate can be made to look plausible, so a solution needs: (a) an external document tying a named W.H. to Thorpe, to the manuscript, or to Shakespeare in or before 1609 — a Stationers' record, a letter, a transfer of copy, an inscribed presentation copy; (b) a reading of "onlie begetter" that is defended from contemporary usage in dedications rather than assumed, ideally from a corpus of early-modern English dedications where "begetter" appears; (c) consistency with the form "Mr.", which rules out peers unless the solution explains the solecism; (d) an account of why Thorpe, not Shakespeare, signs, and how that fits Thorpe's practice across his other imprints. A stylometric or textual argument cannot reach the answer, because the object is a paratext of thirty words with no internal signal.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: wide open, but the field has largely concluded that identifying W.H. would not much change how the sonnets are read; the stakes are biographical and bibliographical rather than interpretive.
- material 3: the primary object is complete, undisputed and digitized, but it is thirty words, and the evidence that could decide is external and thin.
- solvable 4: Thorpe was addressing a real person he knew; a determinate referent almost certainly existed, even if the wording was also a tease.
- compute 2: the useful computational move is a corpus study of "begetter" and of dedication formulae in early-modern print, which constrains the sense but cannot name the man.
- verifiable 2: short of a new document, competing candidates remain equally defensible; with a document this would be a 5.
- crowding 1: four centuries of attempts, an active amateur and anti-Stratfordian ecosystem, and every naive combinatorial approach to the initials exhausted.

## Unverified claims
- That the 1609 quarto was printed by George Eld, and the Stationers' Register entry of 20 May 1609, are reported from general reference and were not verified against a record in this pass.
- The figure of thirteen surviving copies comes from the Wikipedia article; no census (e.g. a Folger or ESTC count) was checked.
- The specific positions of Duncan-Jones and Burrow on W.H. are attributed from general knowledge of their editions, not from the texts.
- Whether Thorpe signed prefatory matter only when the author was absent or dead is stated in the secondary source as a tendency; it was not checked against his imprint list.
