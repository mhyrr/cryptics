# Sator Square (ROTAS / SATOR word square)

## What it is
A 5 × 5 Latin letter square that reads the same across, down, and both backwards: SATOR AREPO TENET OPERA ROTAS. It is a double palindrome — the whole grid is its own 180-degree rotation — and the words mean something like "the sower Arepo holds the wheels/works", if AREPO is a name. All securely dated ancient examples begin with ROTAS rather than SATOR; the SATOR-first orientation is medieval. The earliest known example was found in November 1936 on column LXI of the Palestra Grande at Pompeii, in the ROTAS form, and dates before the earthquake of 62 CE; a second Pompeian example from the house of Publius Paquius Proculus dates 50–79 CE. Matteo Della Corte's 1938 study placed the associated graffiti before the 62 CE earthquake, and Friedrich Focke's 1948 stucco analysis confirmed it. Other Roman-era finds: Cirencester (second century), Manchester (pottery, c. 185 CE), Dura-Europos in Syria (four examples, c. 200 CE), Conímbriga in Portugal (unfired brick, second century), Aquincum in Hungary (roof tile, second century). In the Middle Ages and after, the square appears everywhere as a charm against fire, fever, and demons.

## What is unsolved
Who made it, why, and whether the words mean anything beyond what is needed to close the palindrome. The specific cruxes: AREPO is a hapax legomenon, attested nowhere else in Latin; the square's ancient examples are pagan-context graffiti while its later life is overwhelmingly Christian and magical; and the single most celebrated proposal — that the letters are an anagram of PATERNOSTER written twice as a cross with A and O left over for Alpha and Omega — cannot be assessed because nobody has established how surprising that anagram is.

## What survives
The text itself is 25 letters and completely known, so this is a rare case where the primary evidence is total and the context is what is missing. Roughly a dozen ancient attestations survive as physical objects and graffiti with recorded find-spots and dates, plus hundreds of medieval and early modern instances on amulets, buildings and in manuscripts. Nothing needs transcribing; everything needs dating and contextualizing, which is an archaeological rather than a philological problem.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is no consensus. The Paternoster theory was discovered independently by Christian Frank (1924), the German priest Felix Grosser (who published it in 1926), and Sigurd Agrell (1927), and briefly produced a consensus for Christian origin. The Pompeian dating broke it. Guillaume de Jerphanion's objections are the standard list: few if any Christians at Pompeii; first-century Christians would write Greek, not Latin; the Alpha-and-Omega motif is later than the first century; the cross as a symbol emerges around 130–131 CE; cryptic Christian symbolism belongs to the third-century persecutions. Jérôme Carcopino's counter — that the Pompeian squares were cut later by looters — founders on the undisturbed volcanic deposits. On the statistical question, Focke, Hugh Last and William Baines (1987) all asked whether the Paternoster anagram could arise by chance "without reaching a conclusion"; Baines concluded his own analysis "proved nothing." By the 1980s the theory had lost most of its academic support. Live alternatives: a Latin word-puzzle in the tradition of the ROMA/AMOR palindromes also found at Pompeii (Frugoni, Guarducci; Charles Douglas Gunn's 1969 computer study found the Sator square the best of 2,264 possible variants); a Jewish cryptogram (Fishwick, David Daube, Mary Beard, with Nicolas Vinel in 2006 arguing for Pythagorean arithmetic in a Jewish setting); Pythagorean or Stoic (Hommel, Schneider); Gnostic; Orphic; Mithraic (Moeller, via numerology). On AREPO itself: Carcopino's Gaulish word for "plough" was discounted; Daube read it as Alpha-Omega; J. Gwyn Griffiths proposed the Egyptian *Hr-Hp*, "face of Apis"; Marcovich (1983) read it as an abbreviated Harpocrates; Fishwick argued it is simply the residual word the palindrome required and that no more should be expected of it. Megan O'Donald's assessment is that most interpretations of the ROTAS square have failed to gain consensus because the archaeology and the later religious associations cannot be reconciled.

## What a solution would have to do
First, settle the statistical question that three scholars started and none finished: given a realistic Latin lexicon weighted by attested frequency, enumerate the double-palindromic 5 × 5 squares (Gunn's 2,264 is the existing benchmark), and for each one compute the probability that its letter multiset yields a phrase as salient as PATERNOSTER twice with two A's and two O's left over. If salient anagrams are common in that population, the Paternoster theory is dead as evidence; if the Sator square is a strong outlier, it becomes a real argument. Either result is publishable and neither has been published. Second, any origin claim must explain all four of: the ROTAS-first orientation of every ancient example and the flip to SATOR-first later; the pre-62 CE Pompeian date; the pagan-context distribution of the early examples against the Christian and magical distribution of the later ones; and AREPO, either as a word in some language with an attestation, or as a filler, in which case the claim must accept that the square's "meaning" is a by-product of its form. Third, a claim of cryptographic intent must state what the encoder gained: a message readable only by someone who already knows it is not a message.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: a real and famous open question, but the stakes are the history of a charm, not of a field.
- material 4: the text is complete and trivially reproducible, with roughly a dozen dated ancient attestations; not 5 because the surviving evidence is 25 letters and the context is thin.
- solvable 3: serious scholars hold both that it encodes something (Christian, Jewish, Pythagorean) and that AREPO is filler and the square means only what it says.
- compute 4: the decisive sub-question is a combinatorial and statistical one on a fully specified search space, it has been attempted and abandoned, and a modern version would eliminate a whole hypothesis family; not 5 because even a clean result leaves the origin question open.
- verifiable 2: no reading can be mechanically confirmed; a statistical result constrains claims about intent without establishing it.
- crowding 1: a century of attempts, dozens of named theories, and an active popular and crank literature.

## Unverified claims
- Rose Mary Sheldon's 2003 description of it as "one of the oldest unsolved word puzzles in the world" and the 2022 Britannica characterization are reported by the encyclopedic source but not checked against the originals.
- Gunn's 1969 figure of 2,264 palindrome variants: the criteria he enumerated over (which lexicon, which symmetry class) were not verified, and the number cannot be reproduced without them. Any new statistical work must restate its own search space rather than inherit this figure.
- The claim that all securely dated ancient examples are ROTAS-first: the encyclopedic source lists every ancient example as ROTAS-form, which supports it, but a complete ancient corpus was not consulted.
- Whether a modern statistical treatment of the Paternoster anagram has been published since Baines 1987.
