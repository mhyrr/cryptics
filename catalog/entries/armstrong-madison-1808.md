# John Armstrong's letter to Madison in an unknown code, 20 February 1808

## What it is
John Armstrong Jr. was the American minister in Paris during the Embargo crisis. Most of his coded dispatches to Secretary of State James Madison use one standard code of about 1,600 word-and-syllable groups, in which "the" is 972. His letter of 20 February 1808 uses a different code that appears nowhere else. Madison wrote on 15 May 1808 that "no such Cypher is in the office, and must be one concerted with another correspondent." So the State Department itself apparently could not read it. The letter is in the National Archives (RG 59, Dispatches from US Ministers to France) and is transcribed on Founders Online.

## What is unsolved
The plaintext of the 20 February 1808 letter. Tomokiyo describes the code as numerical groups from 1 to about 1,900, possibly one-part, with some passages in graphic symbols that may be shorthand.

The related Armstrong items in Cryptiana's "American" section, and their status:
- **Postscript in code, 30 August 1808** (Library of Congress, James Madison Papers, Series 1, reel 10, frames 0520–0522; LoC item mjm015002): **solved**. Daniel Bourdeau notified Tomokiyo of a full solution on 15 September 2026. The postscript is 49 groups in the standard "972" code; 48 decode with sense and the last is probably a digit slip. The key was rebuilt from State Department pencil decodes and a known-plaintext letter of 4 May 1806. It is not entered separately; it belongs in EXCLUDED as solved.
- **Private letter of 22 February 1808** (Founders Online 99-01-02-2733): about 150 groups in the standard code. Bourdeau decodes it "with gaps" and uses it as an independent check on his table. It is not the target of this entry.
- **20 February 1808**: this entry. Bourdeau describes it as "a distinct system whose key has not been recovered."

## What survives
- The original letter in the National Archives, with images in its online catalog.
- A Founders Online transcription (Madison Papers, Secretary of State series, early-access document 99-01-02-2728).
- About four pages and roughly 450 or more code groups, according to the summary of Tomokiyo's article.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
- **Tomokiyo** (Cryptiana) studied it against Armstrong's standard code, found that it does not fit, and lists it as unsolved.
- **Yaacov Apelbaum**, an AFIO member, was declared winner of an AFIO "Armstrong-Madison Encrypted Letter Contest" in May 2025. His decryption is described as a word-based codebook reading about American seamen and trade with France. AFIO says it was checked for "grammatical structure, coherence, and stylistic alignment."
- **Tomokiyo's response:** "While a solution has been claimed, I believe this still remains unsolved." His objections are that the reading needs "further tweaking and extensive omission" and does not account for symbols that occur several times.
- **Bourdeau** (2026) independently treats the key as unrecovered.

The claim's own test is plausibility and coherence, which is not mechanical. Two specialists who have worked the Armstrong codes reject or ignore it. No specialist has endorsed it, so it is recorded as an unaccepted claim and not as a split field.

## What a solution would have to do
1. Give a code table under which every group decodes, with each recurring group keeping one meaning, and without omitting or "tweaking" groups.
2. If the code is one-part, respect the numeric-alphabetical ordering.
3. Account for the graphic-symbol passages: either read them as a known shorthand or show that they are something else.
4. Fit Armstrong's other dispatches of February 1808, decoded in the standard code. These include the 22 February letter, two days later, which is the natural cross-check on content.
5. Explain Madison's remark. Ideally, identify the "other correspondent" whose code this was, since that code's table may survive in someone else's papers.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: one dispatch from a documented crisis. Its general subject can be partly inferred from the neighbouring letters.
- material 4: complete, imaged by NARA, and transcribed on Founders Online. The shorthand-like passages make the transcription less than certain.
- solvable 4: a real diplomatic code used in earnest. Madison's remark leaves open that the code belonged to another correspondent and that the letter was misdirected, which would not make it meaningless but would put the key outside the State Department.
- compute 3: one message of several hundred groups from a code of about 1,900 entries resists purely statistical attack. Computation can test whether the code is one-part, can test ordering-constrained reconstructions against Armstrong's known vocabulary from the decoded standard-code letters, and can score claimed readings for consistency, which is exactly what is missing from the Apelbaum claim. It cannot decide the letter alone.
- verifiable 4: a full consistent table would be convincing. Short of finding the actual codebook, it stays a strong-criteria check, not a mechanical one.
- crowding 4: Tomokiyo, an AFIO contest, Apelbaum and Bourdeau.

## Unverified claims
- The group count (~450+) and the "one-part" characterisation come from a summary of Tomokiyo's article, not from counting.
- Whether the symbol passages are shorthand.
- The AFIO page was seen only as a search snippet. The content of Apelbaum's reading and the contest terms were not read.
- Whether Tomokiyo's "20 February" letter and Bourdeau's "20 February … distinct system" refer to exactly the same document. It seems so, but this was not checked against the NARA images.
