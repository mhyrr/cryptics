# Eggja stone (N KJ101)

## What it is
A flat gneiss slab bearing about 200 runes in three panels, found in 1917 on the farm Eggja in Sogndal, western Norway, lying face-down over a grave. It dates to roughly 650–700 CE and is the longest known inscription in the Elder Futhark, though several of its rune-forms are transitional toward the Younger Futhark. A horse is incised on the slab, with no agreed relation to the text. It is held by the University Museum of Bergen.

## What is unsolved
Most of the text. The runes are largely legible and the sequence is agreed; the language is not. The inscription is in stylised, partly metrical language — the editions describe it as verse or as *galdr*, a spoken charm — and only minor portions have received a translation the field accepts. The specific cruxes include the clause conventionally read *ni's solu sot*, "not sun-sought" or "not touched by the sun"; the accompanying *ni sakse stain skorin*, "not cut by a [sax-]knife"; a sequence read as describing blood or staining; and a panel that has been read both as a shipwreck narrative and as a mythological passage about a passage of the dead. Whether the protective clauses function as a curse against disturbing the grave or as ritual prescriptions for how the stone was prepared is itself disputed.

## What survives
The slab, damaged and partly worn, with some runes lost or ambiguous; one witness only, and no analogue of comparable length in Elder Futhark. Editions of record: Magnus Olsen's original publication and *Norges innskrifter med de eldre runer* (NIæR); Wolfgang Krause with Herbert Jankuhn, *Die Runeninschriften im älteren Futhark* (1966), which supplies the signum KJ101 and the standard German reading; Ottar Grønvik, *Runene på Eggjasteinen* (1985), which is the fullest alternative; Gerd Høst Heyerdahl's readings are the third main line. Rundata carries the inscription as N KJ101. No open digital facsimile of museum standard was located.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Every serious reading has had to make different segmentation decisions, and the translations diverge so far that they do not describe the same event. Krause and Jankuhn (1966) read a grave-protection formula with a ritual context; Grønvik (1985) read a considerably longer connected text involving a drowning and a funerary rite; Høst read a third way. What the field agrees on is narrow but real: that the text is at least partly metrical, that it contains a prohibition or protection attached to the grave, and that it describes or prescribes some funerary procedure. What it does not agree on is any continuous translation. This is the honest state of the object: partial consensus on genre, none on content.

## What a solution would have to do
Produce a continuous reading of all three panels that (1) states a single segmentation of the rune sequence and marks every place where a rune is uncertain or restored, and does not depend on the restorations; (2) yields Proto-Norse forms that are morphologically possible for c. 650–700 — the inscription sits exactly on the boundary of the syncope period, so a reading must take a defensible position on whether unstressed vowels have fallen, and apply it consistently; (3) accounts for the metrical shape, i.e. shows the alliteration and the line-division rather than asserting "poetic"; (4) explains the horse carving and the face-down placement over the grave, since the archaeological context is evidence and is usually discarded; (5) explains the transitional rune-forms rather than normalising them away. Because there is no second witness, the decisive test is internal: a reading that requires fewer emendations and fewer *hapax* interpretations than Krause's and Grønvik's, and that makes the same morphological assumptions throughout, would be recognised. A reading that translates the famous clauses beautifully and leaves the middle panel as "obscure" is what the field already has three of.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: the longest Elder Futhark text in existence is substantially untranslated; a continuous reading would be a significant event in runology and in early Germanic linguistics.
- material 2: about 200 runes on one damaged slab, with loss; the surviving portion is the binding constraint, and no open high-resolution imaging was found.
- solvable 4: the metrical form and the grave context make deliberate, determinate composition near-certain; not 5 because charm language may be referentially opaque by design.
- compute 2: the tractable computational move is exhaustive enumeration of segmentations scored against a Proto-Norse morphological model — a real but small contribution; the judgment is philological.
- verifiable 2: with one damaged witness and no external control, competing readings would persist; only relative economy of hypotheses can be compared.
- crowding 3: a modest, high-quality literature (Olsen, Krause–Jankuhn, Høst, Grønvik) rather than a crowded field; the obvious approaches have been tried but few people are working on it now.

## Unverified claims
- That the slab lay face-down over a grave is the standard account and was not confirmed in the source fetched, which mentions the grave context but not the orientation.
- The signum "N KJ101" is the conventional Krause–Jankuhn number; the Rundata form was not verified directly.
- Gerd Høst Heyerdahl's reading is named as a third line on the strength of the standard bibliography, not of a fetched source.
- No open digital facsimile was located; absence of a link here means "not found," not "does not exist."
- The dimensions of the slab and the exact rune count (~200) are from the Wikipedia article.
