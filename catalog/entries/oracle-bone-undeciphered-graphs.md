# Undeciphered oracle bone graphs (jiaguwen)

## What it is
The divination records of the late Shang court, incised on ox scapulae and turtle plastrons and buried at Anyang. Roughly 150,000 inscribed pieces have been recovered, the great majority from Yinxu; the inscriptions from Wu Ding's reign are radiocarbon dated to 1254–1197 BCE (±10 years). They are the earliest substantial body of Chinese writing and the direct ancestor of the modern script. The inscriptions are formulaic — a preface naming the day and diviner, a charge, sometimes a prognostication and a verification — which is why they can be read at all.

## What is unsolved
Two thirds of the sign inventory. Over 30,000 distinct character-forms have been catalogued, reducing to somewhere near 4,000 individual characters once allographs are collapsed; specialists have agreed on the form, sound and meaning of roughly 1,200 with certainty and can read between 1,500 and 2,000 in some sense, with several hundred more under active discussion. The rest — well over 2,000 graphs — have no accepted identification with any later character and no secure reading. Many are personal names, clan names, place names and sacrificial terms, so the cost of not reading them falls hardest on Shang political geography and prosopography: passages are readable in form while their content stays blank.

## What survives
An enormous and growing corpus. *Jiaguwen heji* (1978–82, under Guo Moruo and Hu Houxuan) reproduces over 55,000 fragments and remains the reference collection, with a 1999 supplement and a companion volume of transcriptions into standard characters. Later excavations added caches such as Huayuanzhuang (1993: 1,608 pieces, 579 inscribed). Holdings are split between mainland institutions, Academia Sinica in Taiwan (from the 1928–37 excavations) and collections in fourteen other countries. Digitization is well advanced: Yinqi Wenyuan (殷契文渊), launched October 2019 and run jointly by the MOE Key Laboratory for Oracle Bone Script Information Processing at Anyang Normal University and the Institute of Oracle Bone Studies and Shang History at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, is a free open platform of oracle bone records, images and scholarship, and describes itself as the largest such database in the world.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
A century of Chinese paleography, from Sun Yirang, Luo Zhenyu and Wang Guowei through Guo Moruo, Dong Zuobin, Chen Mengjia, Hu Houxuan, Yu Xingwu and Qiu Xigui, has taken the tractable graphs. The method is comparative and contextual: trace a graph forward through Western Zhou bronze inscriptions, Warring States forms and the *Shuowen jiezi* to a known character, or fix its meaning by showing that one sense fits every context in which it appears. Both routes are exhausted for the easy cases. The field's consensus is that the remaining graphs are hard for structural reasons — they are rare, they are often names, their later descendants are missing, and the pieces they appear on are fragmentary — and that the next gains will come from rejoining fragments, from better images, and from systematic comparison at a scale no individual can hold in their head. The existence of a dedicated state key laboratory for "oracle bone script information processing" is itself the field's statement about where it thinks progress lies. A newcomer reads Qiu Xigui's *Chinese Writing* for the method and Keightley's *Sources of Shang History* for the corpus.

## What a solution would have to do
Per graph, the bar is well defined and the field already enforces it. A proposed identification must: (1) fit every attested occurrence of the graph — all of them, listed, with the source publication number for each, since the classic failure mode is a reading that works on three inscriptions and is silent on the other thirty; (2) be paleographically continuous, exhibiting a plausible form-development path to a later attested character, or else argue explicitly that the graph died out; (3) respect the formulaic grammar of the inscription type it appears in, so that a graph occupying the diviner slot is read as a name and not as a verb; (4) be consistent with the divination calendar and the reign periodization, since graphs are diagnostic of diviner groups and a reading that crosses periods incoherently is wrong; and (5) for name readings, connect to the sacrificial or genealogical record rather than resting on graphic resemblance alone. At corpus scale, the undone work is different in kind: an allograph clustering over the full image corpus that says how many distinct characters there actually are rather than how many forms; automated fragment rejoining, which mechanically increases the number of complete contexts available for every rare graph; and a systematic report of which undeciphered graphs have enough surviving contexts to be solvable at all, which would turn a vague "2,000 remaining" into a triaged list.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the corpus is substantially readable and the undeciphered residue is concentrated in names and rare terms, so no single graph is a field-changing event; the aggregate would materially change Shang history, which is why this is not a 2.
- material 4: about 150,000 inscribed pieces, more than 55,000 reproduced in the reference collection, published transcriptions, and a free image database; not 5 because the corpus is dispersed across fourteen countries and not all of it is imaged at research quality.
- solvable 4: every graph was a graph for a determinate Shang word, so intent is not in question; not 5 because for many the referent is a person or place whose identity may be unrecoverable even once the reading is fixed.
- compute 5: the strongest compute case in the catalog's non-Western set. The corpus is large, imaged and openly served; the tasks — rubbing denoising, fragment rejoining, allograph clustering, modelling graph evolution to bronze and seal forms — are computational to their core; there is a hard check in the form of "fits all attested contexts"; and there is already an institution devoted to exactly this.
- verifiable 4: a proposed reading is tested against every occurrence across tens of thousands of inscriptions, which is close to the rubric's "predicts unread portions" criterion; not 5 because acceptance is still a judgment of the paleographic community rather than a mechanical check.
- crowding 2: a century of dense Chinese scholarship and thousands of papers, with the easy graphs long gone; the computational corner is much emptier than the philological one, but the axis scores the whole field.
