# Mallarmé, Sonnet en -yx ("Ses purs ongles très haut dédiant leur onyx") and the word "ptyx"

## What it is
A sonnet of Stéphane Mallarmé existing in two states: a version of 1868 titled "Sonnet allégorique de lui-même," sent in letters to friends, and the substantially rewritten version beginning "Ses purs ongles très haut dédiant leur onyx," published in 1887 and collected in the *Poésies*. It is known as the *Sonnet en X* or *Sonnet en -yx* because it is built on only two rhyme families, one in -ix/-yx/-ixe and one in -or/-ore, where a French sonnet conventionally uses four or five. Its rhyme words include onyx, Phénix, ptyx, Styx, nixe. The poem is the standard example of Mallarmé's constructive extremity.

## What is unsolved
Whether the poem refers outward at all, and what "ptyx" is. The word appears once in Mallarmé's works — a hapax — in the phrase "nul ptyx". In a letter of 3 May 1868 to Eugène Lefébure, Mallarmé asked for the word's real meaning or, failing that, for assurance that it exists in no language, since that would let him "create it by the magic of rhyme"; he said he preferred the latter. This is unusual authorial testimony: the poet states that the rhyme constraint generated the word rather than the word carrying a referent. The residual questions are (a) whether "ptyx" nevertheless has a source — Jean-Claude Milner identified Greek πτύξ, "fold," "shell," "leaf," available in dictionaries Mallarmé could have consulted, and Victor Hugo had already used "Ptyx" in 1859 in "Le Satyre" (*La Légende des siècles*) as a name for the Janiculum; and (b) what the 1868 title "allegorical of itself" commits the poem to.

## What survives
Both states of the poem, the letters that accompany them, and the *Poésies* text: all published, all in the standard Bertrand Marchal Pléiade edition, all digitized in French Wikisource. The correspondence with Lefébure and Cazalis in which Mallarmé describes the project is printed in the *Correspondance*. For a nineteenth-century literary object the documentation is as complete as it gets, including the author's own account of his method.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The reading now standard in French scholarship — represented by Marchal's edition and by the long line of commentary from Charles Mauron and Jean-Pierre Richard through to structuralist and deconstructive readings — is that the 1868 title is descriptive: the poem's subject is its own emptiness and reflexivity (mirror, absent room, the "nul ptyx", the constellation), and the two-rhyme constraint is the allegory. On "ptyx," Milner's Greek identification is the leading account and it converts the crux from "invented nonsense word" to "rare Greek word Mallarmé plausibly did not know he was reusing"; Hugo's prior use complicates any claim that the form was Mallarmé's invention. The poem is therefore best described as *contested* rather than *unsolved*: there is a working consensus on what kind of object it is and a real dispute about the status of its one unglossable word. It is included here as the catalog's reference case for "open by construction, with the author's testimony to prove it."

## What a solution would have to do
Three distinct claims can be made and they have different bars. (1) A claim that "ptyx" has a determinate source would have to identify the specific lexicon or text Mallarmé used, show it was in his reach in 1868 (Milner's argument), and reconcile that with his letter, which asks Lefébure whether the word exists — evidence that he had *not* found it in a dictionary. (2) A claim that the poem has an external referent — a room, a person, an occasion, an occult doctrine — would have to overcome the author's own statement of the poem's self-allegorizing design, and would have to explain the whole rhyme scheme, not a line. (3) A claim about the poem's meaning in the ordinary critical sense is an interpretation, not a solution, and the catalog does not score it. For this entry the only falsifiable target is (1): produce the 1860s dictionary or text, with evidence of Mallarmé's access.
