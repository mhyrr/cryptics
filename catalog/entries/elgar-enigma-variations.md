# Elgar, Variations on an Original Theme "Enigma", Op. 36 — the unplayed "larger theme"

## What it is
A theme and fourteen variations for orchestra, composed between October 1898 and February 1899 and premiered at St James's Hall, London, on 19 June 1899 under Hans Richter. Each variation portrays a friend, identified by initials or nickname: C.A.E. (Caroline Alice Elgar), H.D.S-P., R.B.T., W.M.B., R.P.A., Ysobel (Isabel Fitton), Troyte, W.N., Nimrod (August Jaeger), Dorabella (Dora Penny), G.R.S., B.G.N., the asterisked thirteenth (generally Lady Mary Lygon), and E.D.U. (Elgar himself). The "Enigma" of the title attaches not to the portraits, which Elgar explained, but to something he declined to explain.

## What is unsolved
Elgar stated that "through and over the whole set another and larger theme 'goes', but is not played," and separately that "the Enigma I will not explain — its 'dark saying' must be left unguessed, and I warn you that the connexion between the Variations and the Theme is often of the slightest texture." The open question is what that larger theme is: a specific pre-existing melody that can be played in counterpoint against Elgar's theme, a non-melodic idea (friendship, loneliness, a literary motto), or a deliberate feint. Elgar accepted none of the solutions put to him in his lifetime.

## What survives
Everything one could want, short of Elgar's answer. The autograph full score, sketches, the 1899 Novello publication, the 1899 programme note, Elgar's letters to Jaeger and Dora Penny, his own 1926 electrical recording, and the 1929 pianola-roll notes are all extant and published; the score is in the public domain and available on IMSLP. Dora Penny's memoir *Edward Elgar: Memories of a Variation* (1937) records Elgar telling her she "of all people" should have guessed. The evidence base is a fully digitized, machine-readable score plus a documented paratext — which is why the field's failure here is instructive rather than a material problem.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The literature divides into counterpoint candidates, allusion candidates and non-musical candidates. Counterpoint candidates include *Auld Lang Syne* (Richard Powell, 1934 — Elgar had already rejected it), *Rule, Britannia!* (Theodore van Houten, 1975), *Ein feste Burg* (Robert Padgett, from 2009), Pergolesi's *Stabat Mater* (Edward Newton-Rex, 2019), *Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star* (Patrick Turner), *Now the Day is Over* (Clive McClelland), Tallis's canon, and the slow movement of Beethoven's *Pathétique* sonata (Hans Westgeest). Allusion candidates include Mendelssohn's *Calm Sea and Prosperous Voyage*, Purcell's "When I am laid in earth", Boyce's *Heart of Oak* in the minor, Chopin's G minor Nocturne, *Pop Goes the Weasel*, and Bach's *Art of Fugue* (Marshall Portnoy, 1985). Non-musical candidates include 1 Corinthians 13:12 (Ian Parrott), Shakespeare's Sonnet 66 (Edmund Green), a cipher on "Carice" (Andrew Moodie) and the constant pi (Charles and Matthew Santa, 2010). None is accepted. Norman Del Mar's position — that Elgar probably did not wish the solution found — is widely shared, and much of the field now treats "the larger theme" as either a non-quotable concept or an unanswerable provocation. This is a claimant-saturated field: proposals arrive continuously, mostly self-published.

## What a solution would have to do
State the claim before testing it. A melodic solution must specify exactly how the candidate aligns with Elgar's theme — key, transposition, augmentation or diminution, bar-for-bar registration — and then show that the resulting counterpoint (a) is stylistically admissible by Elgar's own contrapuntal standards, (b) fits the whole theme and not only its first four bars, and (c) fits "through and over the whole set," i.e. remains valid across variations, which is what Elgar's word "goes" appears to demand. Crucially, it must be shown to be *unusual*: a corpus test against several thousand plausible 19th-century tunes, scored by the same alignment criterion, establishing that the candidate's fit is an outlier rather than a consequence of the theme's harmonic banality. No published claim known to this entry has done that outlier test, and its absence is the single reason the field ignores new candidates. A non-melodic solution must instead identify a documentary source — a letter, an annotated score, a dedication — because "friendship" and similar answers are unfalsifiable. Any solution must also explain why Elgar rejected *Auld Lang Syne* to Jaeger's face and told Dora Penny she should have known.

## Why the scores
- mystery 4: openly unresolved for 127 years, famous beyond musicology, and a demonstrated answer would be a real event; not 5 because the work's reception does not depend on it.
- material 5: score, sketches, letters, programme note and the composer's own recordings are complete, published and machine-readable.
- solvable 4: the author asserted in writing that a larger theme exists, which is strong intent evidence; short of 5 because he may have meant something non-quotable, and because his warning about "slightest texture" invites the suspicion of a feint.
- compute 4: alignment of a candidate tune against a known theme is exactly a computational search, and the corpus-level outlier test described above is available and, as far as this entry could establish, undone; not 5 because even a perfect fit does not establish intent.
- verifiable 2: no mechanical check exists — Elgar is dead, accepted nothing, and left no key; a plausible counterpoint will always be one plausible counterpoint among many.
- crowding 1: a century of attempts, a continuous amateur ecosystem, and every obvious tune already proposed.

## Unverified claims
- The exact wording and authorship of the 1899 programme note (usually attributed to C. A. Barry, working from Elgar's own words) was not verified; the two Elgar quotations above are taken from the Wikipedia article rather than from the note or from Moore.
- Van Houten's report that Elgar endorsed *Rule, Britannia!* is repeated in the secondary source and looks doubtful; it is not verified here.
- The identification of Variation XIII (\*\*\*) as Lady Mary Lygon is standard but qualified in the source as "likely".
- Whether any published claim has performed a corpus-wide outlier test of candidate tunes was not exhaustively checked; the statement above is a finding of absence from the reviewed secondary literature only.
