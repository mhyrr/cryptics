# Gospel of Thomas: the disputed logia (7, 114, and the hard sayings)

## What it is
A collection of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, with almost no narrative. It survives complete in Coptic as the second tractate of Nag Hammadi Codex II, in a codex of about 340 CE first published photographically in 1956, and in three Greek fragments from Oxyrhynchus dated between 130 and 250 CE: P.Oxy. 654 (the opening through logion 7, plus logia 24 and 36), P.Oxy. 1 (logia 26–33) and P.Oxy. 655 (logia 36–39). This entry is not about the collection's date or its relation to the canonical gospels; it is about the individual sayings whose intended sense is not recoverable from the text as transmitted.

## What is unsolved
The hardest case is **logion 7**, and it is hard for a textual reason, not only an interpretive one. As transmitted: "Blessed is the lion which the man eats, and the lion will become man; and cursed is the man whom the lion eats, and the lion will become man" (Blatz). The second clause repeats the first, which destroys the antithesis the saying's form demands, so either the Coptic is corrupt or the logic is not the one modern readers expect. Gerd Lüdemann proposed emending 2b to "the man will become lion" to restore the parallel; Layton and Doresse translate the received text in different directions, Doresse silently supplying "the man becomes a lion." Whether the saying is corrupt, and if so what it said, is unresolved. The second case is **logion 114**, where Peter demands that Mary leave and Jesus answers that he will "make her male, so that she too may become a living spirit resembling you males." What "male" means here — spiritual inferiority of women, a return to a prelapsarian undifferentiated state, an ascetic ideal — is genuinely disputed, as is whether the saying belongs to the collection's earliest layer. The broader unresolved class includes logia 30, 61 and 105, where the Coptic, the Greek and the sense pull in different directions.

## What survives
Excellent evidence by the standards of this catalog: a complete Coptic text of all 114 sayings in one fourth-century codex, three second- and third-century Greek fragments giving independent witness to about a sixth of the collection, photographic facsimiles, and many critical editions and synoptic concordances. Where the Greek overlaps the Coptic it is the control on the Coptic — but P.Oxy. 654 breaks off at exactly logion 7, so for the single most corrupt saying the Greek is not usable as a check. Both the Coptic and the Greek are digitized and available in machine-readable form through the standard corpora.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
On logion 7 there is no consensus and the proposals do not converge. F. F. Bruce reads it as elevation versus degradation on a scale of being. Funk and Hoover note the lion's double valence as royalty and as the passions, read the saying as ascetic, and doubt it comes from Jesus. Marvin Meyer connects it to the Platonic tripartite soul, with reason taming the lion of appetite. Jean Doresse takes the lion as the passions or the lying spirit of evil. Robert Grant and David Noel Freedman take it as the hostile world assimilated through knowledge. Lüdemann emends. On logion 114 the split is sharper and more public: critics read it as asserting women's spiritual inferiority; others read "male" symbolically as the prelapsarian state; Jorunn Jacobsen Buckley reads it as a reversal of Genesis in which the female becomes male before attaining that state; Melissa Harl Sellew offers a trans-centred reading in which outer transformation expresses inner reality. The encyclopedic survey records no consensus on whether 114 is a later addition.

## What a solution would have to do
For logion 7, the first requirement is to decide the textual question before the interpretive one, and to decide it on Coptic grounds: a claim that the text is corrupt must identify a plausible mechanism of corruption in Sahidic (dittography, a lost negation, a scribal harmonization) and show it operating elsewhere in Codex II, not merely observe that the saying reads badly. A claim that the received text is sound must produce a reading under which the repetition is intentional and must explain why the *same* clause follows both the blessing and the curse. Either way, a proposal has to (i) account for the whole saying including the second clause, (ii) fit the collection's attested rhetorical forms (Thomas' blessing-and-curse pairs are a finite set that can be enumerated), and (iii) be tested against the lion-devouring motif across the digitized Platonic, Hermetic, Manichaean, Mandaean and Egyptian corpora rather than against one parallel chosen to fit. For logion 114, a claim about the intended sense must show that "make male" is used in the same way in the comparative material it invokes, must square with the treatment of Mary and of gender elsewhere in Thomas (logia 22, 46, 61), and — if it claims the saying is a later addition — must offer evidence beyond its being uncongenial: lexical, syntactic or structural marks distinguishing it from the surrounding logia, measured against the rest of the collection.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: these are live disputes that bear on Thomas' theology and on the historical-Jesus question, but they are local cruxes inside a text that is otherwise read.
- material 4: complete Coptic plus three early Greek witnesses, fully digitized; not 5 because the Greek control fails precisely at logion 7.
- solvable 3: for logion 114 an intended meaning certainly exists and is being argued about; for logion 7, if the Coptic is corrupt then the intended wording may not be recoverable at all, which pulls the average down.
- compute 3: motif and parallel sweeps across digitized Greek, Coptic and Iranian-Manichaean corpora are real and largely undone, and Thomas' own forms can be enumerated mechanically; but the residue is exegetical judgement, so computation assists rather than decides.
- verifiable 2: only plausibility; competing readings of both logia would remain even after the best comparative work.
- crowding 1: an enormous literature on Thomas, with logion 114 in particular the subject of a very large body of work, and an active popular ecosystem.

## Unverified claims
- That logia 30, 61 and 105 belong in the genuinely disputed class. This is the compiler's judgement from the literature; the encyclopedic source consulted here discusses none of them, so the scope of this entry beyond logia 7 and 114 is not sourced.
- Howard M. Jackson, *The Lion Becomes Man* (1985), the standard monograph on the logion 7 motif — cited from memory and not verified in this pass.
- Whether the Coptic of logion 7 shows any physical damage or scribal correction in the codex, which would bear directly on the corruption hypothesis.
- The claim that P.Oxy. 654 breaks off at logion 7 rather than covering it: the source says the fragment runs "beginning through logion 7", which may mean it includes the saying. If P.Oxy. 654 does preserve logion 7 in Greek, the `material` and `verifiable` scores should rise and this entry needs re-checking against a papyrological edition. This is the single most important thing to verify next.
