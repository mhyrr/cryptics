# Toynbee tiles

## What it is
Several hundred licence-plate-sized panels embedded in the asphalt of city streets, made of layered linoleum and asphalt crack-filling compound, laid so that traffic wears away a covering layer and reveals the text. The standard text, in hand-cut block capitals with a characteristic lower-case i, reads in four lines: "TOYNBEE IDEA / IN MOVIE `2001 / RESURRECT DEAD / ON PLANET JUPITER". The first confirmed sighting is in Philadelphia in 1983. Tiles have been found in roughly two dozen cities in the United States and in three South American cities. A minority of tiles — the "side tiles" — carry much longer texts, some in Cyrillic transliteration, attacking named journalists, news organisations and government agencies, and referring to a plot against the tiler.

## What is unsolved
Who made them, and what exactly the proposition is. The referents are partly traceable: Arnold J. Toynbee wrote about resurrection, Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* ends at Jupiter, and Ray Bradbury's story "The Toynbee Convector" is a candidate intertext. But the specific doctrine — how Toynbee's writing plus the film yields a programme for resurrecting the dead on Jupiter, and what the side tiles' conspiratorial narrative claims — has not been reconstructed from the tiler's own statements, because no authenticated statement by the tiler exists. The attribution is the sharper question: the 2011 documentary *Resurrect Dead: The Mystery of the Toynbee Tiles* argues for a reclusive Philadelphia resident, Severino "Sevy" Verna, on the basis of a convergence of circumstantial evidence, and the identification is widely repeated but not confirmed by the man himself or by physical evidence made public.

## What survives
An unusually good corpus. Several hundred physical tiles, many in situ, photographed, geotagged and transcribed by an active community of documentarians; the standard texts and the much rarer long side-tile texts are transcribed; the construction method is understood well enough to have been replicated. Contemporary newspaper coverage from the early 1980s onward survives, as does the documentary and its research materials. Philadelphia's Streets Department has moved from treating the tiles as vandalism to preserving them where removal is not cheap, so some originals will persist. For an outsider-art inscription corpus this is close to ideal: the whole dataset is digital, time-stamped and spatially located.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The trail begins with a 1983 Philadelphia press report and with a Philadelphia man named James Morasco who in the early 1980s promoted a Toynbee-and-Jupiter resurrection idea to newspapers and to a Philadelphia institution; Morasco was long the leading candidate and was widely reported as the tiler until his death, after which tiles continued to appear. Justin Duerr's long investigation, presented in *Resurrect Dead* (2011), assembled the Verna case: a shortwave-radio and Cyrillic connection, a car modified to drop tiles through a hole in the floor, and an address consistent with the earliest tiles. The consensus is that Verna is the most probable tiler and that the case is strong but circumstantial; the doctrine remains unexplicated, and copycat tiles laid by others since the 2000s contaminate the later corpus.

## What a solution would have to do
Attribution: a confirmation would need either the tiler's own acknowledgement, or physical evidence — linoleum stock, tools, tile blanks, the vehicle — tied to a person, or documentary evidence such as a purchase record or a letter matching the side tiles' text. Short of that, a rigorous circumstantial case must handle the copycat problem explicitly, which is where computation enters and where the work appears undone: the photographic corpus supports a quantitative typology — letterform metrics, the distinctive lower-case i, linoleum colour and layer composition, text variants, panel dimensions — that would partition the corpus into hands and date each hand's active range and geographic reach. A defensible attribution claim should first establish that a single hand made the pre-2000 tiles, and only then argue who that hand belonged to. Doctrine: a reconstruction must be built on the side tiles, transcribed completely and read as a corpus, with the Toynbee and Bradbury sources quoted where they are actually used, and must explain the Cyrillic and the named targets rather than treating them as noise. A reading that explains the four-line standard text and ignores the side tiles is not a reading.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: partly resolved — a probable maker and traceable sources — with a real residue on both the identification and the doctrine.
- material 5: hundreds of surviving objects, comprehensively photographed, mapped and transcribed by an existing community; a complete machine-readable corpus.
- solvable 4: the tiler plainly intended a determinate proposition, and the side tiles state positions explicitly; not 5 because the doctrine may be idiosyncratic enough to resist reconstruction without the author.
- compute 3: letterform and material typology plus spatio-temporal clustering over the photographic corpus is a real and apparently unattempted contribution; it can separate hands but not name them.
- verifiable 4: an attribution is confirmable by strong criteria — physical evidence or acknowledgement — and the field would accept it.
- crowding 3: a documentary, a dedicated community and a modest press literature; the quantitative corpus work has not been done.
