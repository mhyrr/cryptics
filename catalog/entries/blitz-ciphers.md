# Blitz ciphers

## What it is
A set of handwritten pages in an unknown script, brought to public notice around 2011 by their anonymous owner via Nick Pelling's Cipher Mysteries site. The owner's account: a relative found them just after the Second World War in wooden boxes concealed in the wall of an East London cellar that German bombing had exposed. At least eight pages are known, on handmade paper, in at least two hands — a larger "presentation hand" in brown ink and a smaller "annotation hand" in darker ink — carrying cipher text, geometric diagrams resembling annotated tiles, and a 20×20 table. Many passages are water-damaged and faded. The alphabet runs to roughly 50–60 distinct glyphs. The owner has remained anonymous and the documents have never been placed with an institution.

## What is unsolved
Everything, starting with whether the objects are what they are said to be. Three questions stack. (1) Authenticity and date: Pelling's working hypothesis was a 16th-century presentation layer with 17th-century annotation, but he states plainly that the whole thing could as easily be much more recent and deliberately made to look old. (2) The cipher: the text shows a "peaky" frequency distribution with some glyphs much commoner than others, which resembles natural language and suggests something in the monoalphabetic family, combined with a disorder more characteristic of polyalphabetic ciphers — an unusual pair of properties. (3) Content: the tile-like diagrams and the 20×20 table suggest a technical, possibly craft or ritual, subject, but nothing has been read.

## What survives
Not enough, and not accessibly. Eight or more pages exist in private hands; a subset has been photographed and published online at moderate resolution; no complete image set, no institutional custody, no physical examination, no paper or ink analysis, and no published transcription. This is the catalog's clearest case where the limiting factor is access rather than method: paper and ink dating would settle question (1) in a week and would cost less than a single scholar's month, and it has never been done. A catalog entry can therefore say something useful that no analysis of the text can: the next move is custodial, not cryptanalytic.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is no scholarly literature at all — no journal article, no monograph, no institutional assessment that I could locate. The entire record is Nick Pelling's blog series and discussion in its comments, together with community attempts at glyph transcription. Pelling's own position is careful and is the most defensible one available: the ciphers remain unbroken, they have unusual statistical properties, the provenance story is unverifiable, a hoax of this quality would be "such a classy job," and the authenticity question is therefore open. He does not claim them genuine. No one else has published a substantive assessment. That combination — a genuinely unexamined object with a single serious commentator — is why `crowding` is 5, the highest in this category, and simultaneously why every other score is low.

## What a solution would have to do
Two separable deliverables, and the first must come before the second or nothing that follows is worth anything.
*Material.* Get the documents examined: radiocarbon or at minimum watermark and chain-line analysis of the handmade paper; ink analysis distinguishing iron-gall from modern formulations; high-resolution multispectral imaging of all pages including the water-damaged passages; and a stated provenance chain from the finder to the present owner, with names and dates. Any of these could kill the object outright, and a hoax verdict reached this way would be a full success by this repository's standards.
*Textual.* Publish a complete glyph inventory and transcription of all pages with a stable page-and-line numbering — this does not exist and no statistic computed before it does is trustworthy. Then a proposed decryption must: read continuous text across at least two pages including one not used in fitting; account for both statistical anomalies (the peaky monoalphabetic-looking frequencies *and* the polyalphabetic-looking disorder) under one system; explain the 20×20 table as part of the system rather than as decoration; and produce content that coheres with the tile diagrams on the same pages. A claimed reading that ignores the diagrams is ignoring the only crib the documents offer.

## Why the scores
- mystery 2: if authentic, a genuine unread early modern cipher; but the field has not engaged, and the balance of expectation among the few who have looked leans toward doubt.
- material 2: eight-odd pages in private hands, partial images, water damage, no transcription, no physical analysis. The surviving accessible portion severely limits any method.
- solvable 2: the best available evidence does not favour a determinate recoverable meaning — the provenance is unverifiable, and a well-made hoax is explicitly on the table per the only serious commentator.
- compute 2: with no complete transcription and 50–60 glyphs over a few pages, there is very little to compute, and the decisive questions (paper, ink, provenance) are not computational at all.
- verifiable 2: even a plausible reading of a few pages could not be confirmed against anything external; and if the object is modern, "correct" has no referent.
- crowding 5: almost nobody has worked this. One blogger, no monograph, no paper, no institution. This is the emptiest field in the category, and the entry is worth keeping for that reason alone — but the `attackable` ranking should not be allowed to promote it on crowding when `material` and `solvable` are both 2.

## Unverified claims
- The entire discovery narrative — East London cellar, wooden boxes in a wall, exposed by bombing, found by the owner's relative just after the war. This is the owner's account, reported at one remove, with the owner anonymous. Nothing in it has been independently corroborated and the entry should be read as reporting a claim, not a history.
- The number of pages. "At least eight" is what the source supports; the total held by the owner was not established.
- The glyph count (~50–60) and the "peaky" frequency characterization are Pelling's, computed on a partial transcription of partial images.
- The dating hypothesis (16th-century presentation layer, 17th-century annotation) is Pelling's explicitly provisional suggestion, which he himself immediately qualifies.
- Whether any physical, paper, ink or radiocarbon analysis has ever been performed. None found; asserted here as a gap.
- Whether the owner still holds the documents, and whether any institution has been offered them.
- Whether Klaus Schmeh has published a substantive assessment, as opposed to coverage; the Cipherbrain URL above is the site root and the specific post was not located.
- Whether any complete image set or transcription has been released anywhere since the original blog series.
