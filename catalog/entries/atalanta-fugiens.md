# Atalanta Fugiens (Michael Maier, 1617)

## What it is
An alchemical emblem book by Michael Maier (1568–1622), physician and sometime imperial counsellor, printed by Johann Theodor de Bry at Oppenheim in 1617 with a second edition in 1618. It has fifty numbered units, each consisting of a copperplate engraving by Matthäus Merian, a Latin epigram, a Latin prose discourse, a German verse epigram — and a piece of three-voice vocal music, a *fuga*, printed in mensural notation. The three voices are labelled for Atalanta (the fleeing stone), Hippomenes (the pursuing adept) and the apple (the impediment). It is the only alchemical book that is also a printed songbook, and the pun on *fuga* is the organising joke.

## What is unsolved
Whether the fifty fugues carry information beyond illustration, and if so what kind. Nobody disputes that Maier constructed the work deliberately and to a scheme; what is open is (a) the relation of each fugue to its emblem and epigram — the canons are strict, and it is not established whether their intervals, entries or mensuration track anything in the chemical content; (b) whether the fifty-unit sequence is a single operation, a commonplace anthology, or a mnemonic curriculum; (c) the referents of individual emblems, many of which quote earlier alchemical imagery whose sources are only partly identified; and (d) what Maier's fugue models were, since a large majority are reported to derive from existing melodic material.

## What survives
Both editions, complete, in many copies. This is the best-resourced object in the alchemical-emblem category: *Furnace and Fugue: A Digital Edition of Michael Maier's "Atalanta fugiens" (1618) with Scholarly Commentary*, edited by Tara Nummedal and Donna Bilak (University of Virginia Press, 2020, Mellon- and Delmas-funded, open access), supplies zoomable plates, the Latin and German text, the fifty fugues in modern transcription and in newly commissioned recordings, and roughly a dozen scholarly essays. The Science History Institute holds high-resolution scans of the original. The 1617 text is therefore machine-readable in both words and music notation — a rare combination, and the reason this entry scores as it does on `compute`.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
H. M. E. de Jong's *Michael Maier's "Atalanta fugiens": Sources of an Alchemical Book of Emblems* (Brill, 1969; repr. Nijmegen, 2002) is the standard study and did the first systematic source-identification of the discourses; it remains the necessary first reading and is also the demonstration that the method works, since Maier quotes constantly. The *Furnace and Fugue* essays (Bilak on the design and the musical structure, Nummedal on Maier's career and patronage, with contributions on the music by Loren Ludwig and on Maier's Rosicrucian context) are the current state of the art, and they are candid that the music's relation to the alchemy is unsettled. The consensus: Maier was a learned compiler whose sources are largely recoverable; the whole is more tightly designed than a commonplace book; and no one has yet shown that the fugues encode rather than ornament.

## What a solution would have to do
Three concrete, checkable things. (1) Finish de Jong: identify the source of every quotation in all fifty discourses by machine comparison against the digitized Latin alchemical corpus (*Theatrum Chemicum*, *Artis auriferae*, the pseudo-Lullian and Senior corpora), and publish the alignment. A claim about the program that rests on the discourses without knowing which sentences are Maier's is unsupported. (2) Test the music as data: with all fifty fugues encoded, state a hypothesis about what a fugue's structure would encode (canon interval, voice entry order, mensuration, mode, text-setting), fit it, and then predict the structural feature of a fugue withheld from the fitting. Identify the borrowed melodic models and show whether Maier's choices among them correlate with emblem content. (3) Show the fifty-unit order is not arbitrary, by predicting the position of a unit removed from the sequence from its content alone. Anything less than a held-out prediction is pattern-finding on fifty items.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the emblems are read in outline and the sources partly identified; the sharp open question (the fugues) is narrower than a wholly unread text, though it reaches musicology as well as the history of alchemy.
- material 5: two printed editions, complete, high-resolution digitization, an open-access scholarly digital edition with text, music transcription and recordings. Nothing else in this category is this well served.
- solvable 4: Maier was a documented author with a stated method, the 50×3 architecture is demonstrably deliberate, and the *fuga* pun proves intentional design; not 5 because "the fugues encode the chemistry" is a hypothesis, not the author's claim.
- compute 5: the words and the music are both machine-readable, the source corpus is digitized, and the two obvious moves — a mass source-parallel sweep of the discourses and a structural analysis of fifty encoded canons — are exactly what nobody has finished.
- verifiable 4: source matches are word-for-word checkable and a held-out structural prediction on the fugues would be strong evidence; not 5 because a global "program" claim would still settle by consensus.
- crowding 2: one monograph, one substantial multi-author digital edition, and an active group of specialists; not 1 because the source alignment and the music analysis remain open.
