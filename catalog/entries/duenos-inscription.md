# Duenos inscription (CIL I² 4)

## What it is
Three lines of Old Latin scratched right-to-left around the base of a *kernos*: three small globular vases of dark brown bucchero joined by clay struts. Heinrich Dressel found it in 1880 in the valley between the Quirinal and Viminal hills in Rome, near the modern Via Nazionale. It is now in the Staatliche Museen, Berlin, inventory 30894,3, and is conventionally dated about 550 BCE, though proposals range across the seventh to fifth centuries. It is one of the handful of earliest Latin texts of any length, and the only one of them that is a connected utterance rather than a list or a name.

## What is unsolved
The middle line. As transmitted, without word division:

- `IOVESATDEIVOSQOIMEDMITATNEITEDENDOCOSMISVIRCOSIED`
- `ASTEDNOISIOPETOITESIAIPAKARIVOIS`
- `DVENOSMEDFECEDENMANOMEINOMDVENOINEMEDMALOSTATOD`

Line 1 is read with broad agreement as an oath formula: "he swears by the gods, whoever gives me away, that unless the girl is kind/favourable towards you…" Line 3 is read as a maker's formula: "Duenos made me; let no evil come to (or from) Duenos" — with `EN MANOM EINOM` disputed between "in(to) a good hand" and a reference to the craftsman. Line 2 is the crux. Every element of `ASTED NOISI OPE TOITESIAI PAKARI VOIS` is contested — its segmentation, the morphology of *toitesiai*, whether *pakari* is an infinitive, whether *vois* is a verb or a dative — and no reading has been accepted. Two further questions follow from it: what the object was for, and what kind of legal or ritual act the whole text records.

## What survives
One object, intact, with roughly 120 letters in three continuous lines and no word separation. There is no second witness, no parallel text in the same formula, and no ancient citation. The inscription is published in CIL I² 4 and in every handbook of archaic Latin; the object is in Berlin. Photographs and drawings are in print; no open high-resolution imaging or 3D scan of the object was found in this pass. This is a case where the entire evidential base is one artefact, and the text is short enough that every scholar works from the same 120 letters.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
There is agreement on the shape of the text — an oath followed by a maker's signature — and on most of line 1 and line 3. Beyond that the readings diverge sharply. One family, current from the 1950s to the 1980s, took the vessel to have held cosmetics or a love philtre and read the text erotically. Georges Dumézil in the 1960s read it as a formula of matrimonial suretyship, with guarantors standing behind a girl's conduct. Pennisi (1992) read a marriage compact involving nuptial gifts (*edna*); Simon and Elboj (1996) read a matrimonial *sponsio* turning on *utilitas*. The fair summary of the field is that the text contains an oath structure and probably records a matrimonial legal obligation, while "all attempts at interpreting the segment AST…VOIS remain conjectural."

## What a solution would have to do
Because the evidence is 120 letters and everyone has them, a solution cannot be a new conjecture; it has to be a demonstration of constraint. Concretely: (i) enumerate every segmentation of `ASTEDNOISIOPETOITESIAIPAKARIVOIS` and score each against an explicit inventory of attested and reconstructible sixth-century Latin morphology, so that the winning reading is shown to be the best of a stated finite set rather than merely a possible member of it — no published treatment has done this systematically; (ii) account for *every* letter, including the ones existing readings quietly emend or redivide; (iii) make the proposed grammar of line 2 consistent with the grammar the same reader assigns to lines 1 and 3, which several published readings are not; (iv) produce at least one independent prediction — a parallel formula in Italic epigraphy, a legal act attested in the later Roman sources, or a function for a three-vase vessel — that the reading entails and that can be checked outside the object; (v) be consistent with autopsy of the object, since letter forms and damaged strokes remain a live source of disagreement. Absent a second witness, the honest ceiling is a ranked list of readings with their costs, not a decipherment.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: it matters to Latin historical linguistics and to the history of archaic Roman law and marriage; it is not a question the wider world is waiting on.
- material 2: one undamaged but tiny witness; the surviving portion is the whole text, and it is still too short to constrain the grammar.
- solvable 4: this is certainly a determinate Latin utterance made to be understood, and two of its three lines are read.
- compute 3: exhaustive segmentation plus morphological scoring is a genuinely computable and genuinely unattempted move that could eliminate hypothesis families; it cannot decide between the survivors, because that depends on reconstructed sixth-century morphology, which is itself contested.
- verifiable 2: competing readings would remain; nothing here can be mechanically confirmed.
- crowding 2: a long and capable philological literature with dozens of proposed readings; not 1 because it is a specialist rather than a popular puzzle.

## Unverified claims
- The exact letter-by-letter transcription. The line-2 ending is given as `PAKARIVOIS` in the source used here, while `PACARIVOIS` is also commonly printed; the difference is not trivial for any reading that turns on the phonology. Autopsy or a published photograph is needed.
- The dating. "c. 550 BCE" is the conventional figure; the range 7th–5th century reflects genuine disagreement that was not resolved here.
- Whether the object has been imaged at high resolution or scanned by the Antikensammlung, and whether an open image exists.
- Whether any reading published since 2000 has gained ground; the literature consulted here stops at 1996.
