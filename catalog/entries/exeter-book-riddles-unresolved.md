# The unresolved Exeter Book riddles

## What it is
Roughly ninety-four short Old English riddles, copied in two blocks in the Exeter Book (Exeter Cathedral Library MS 3501, c. 970–990). The count is editorial rather than authorial: the divisions between poems are not always marked, so the total varies with the editor, and the three main numbering systems — Krapp–Dobbie (ASPR III, 1936), Williamson (1977), and Baum — do not agree. None of the riddles carries a solution in the manuscript. The great majority now have a solution the field accepts (Storm, Shield, Swan, Ox, Iceberg, Bookworm, Key, Bellows, and so on); a residue does not.

## What is unsolved
Which answers the unsolved riddles want, and in some cases whether they are riddles at all. Verified from the ASPR/Williamson concordance, the riddles with either no proposed solution or a field of mutually exclusive ones are:

- **28 / W26** — fourteen proposals on offer: "John Barleycorn, Wine cask, Beer, Ale, Mead, Harp, Stringed instrument, Tortoise lyre, Yew horn, Barrow, Trial of soul, Pattern-welded sword, Parchment, Biblical codex."
- **39 / W37** — "Dream, Death, Cloud, Speech, Faith, Day, Moon, Time, Comet."
- **55 / W53** — "Shield, Scabbard, Harp, Cross, Gallows, Sword rack, Sword box, *Hengen*."
- **70 / W67–68** — "(Church) Bell, Shawm/Shepherd's Pipe, (Double) Flute, Harp, Lyre, Organistrum, Shuttle," with lines 5–6 sometimes taken as a separate riddle ("Lighthouse, Candle") — so the division of the text is itself at issue.
- **74 / W72** — "Cuttlefish, Boat and oak, Quill pen, Ship's figurehead, Siren, Water."
- **89 / W86**, **91 / W88**, **95 / W91** — listed with no proposed solution at all.
- **4 / W2** — "Bell, Millstone, Necromancy, Flail, Lock, Bucket of Water, Dog, Devil, Plough Team, Sword," described by the Riddle Ages editors as having no consensus.

Riddles 89, 91 and 93–95 fall on the badly damaged final leaves (fols 129v–130v), so part of their difficulty is physical loss rather than concealment.

## What survives
One witness, complete for the riddles except for the fire/knife damage to the last gathering. Editions of record: Krapp–Dobbie (ASPR III) for the standard numbering; Craig Williamson, *The Old English Riddles of the Exeter Book* (1977) for the fullest commentary and the alternative numbering, with his *A Feast of Creatures* (1982) as the readable version; Muir, *The Exeter Anthology*, 2nd edn (2000); John D. Niles, *Old English Enigmatic Poems and the Play of the Texts* (2006); Dieter Bitterli, *Say What I Am Called* (2009) on the Latin enigmata background; Patrick J. Murphy, *Unriddling the Exeter Riddles* (2011); Andy Orchard, *The Old English and Anglo-Latin Riddle Tradition* and its companion commentary (2021), now the fullest survey of proposed solutions. The Riddle Ages (ed. Megan Cavell, Birmingham) publishes translations and per-riddle commentary online, keyed to Krapp–Dobbie with Williamson numbers in brackets. Texts are machine-readable in the Dictionary of Old English Corpus; the Anglo-Latin analogues (Symphosius, Aldhelm, Tatwine, Eusebius, Boniface, the Bern riddles) are machine-readable in the Library of Latin Texts and Corpus Corporum.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Solution-hunting has run continuously since Thorpe (1842) and Dietrich (1859–65); Williamson's and Orchard's commentaries catalogue the proposals. The methodological shift of the last thirty years, associated with Niles, Bitterli, Murphy and Orchard, is away from "guess the object" and toward asking what the riddle's own language and its Latin models license: Niles argues that the intended answers are Old English words, often compounds, and that the riddles frequently encode them in the text; Bitterli grounds the collection in the Anglo-Latin *enigmata* tradition; Murphy argues for metaphorical "riddle patterns" shared across the collection. Current consensus is that the hard residue is hard for three different reasons — deliberate over-determination (28, 39, 55), physical loss (89, 91, 95), and uncertain textual division (70, 93) — and that these need different treatments.

## What a solution would have to do
For a single riddle: propose an Old English word (not a modern category), show that every descriptive clause in the riddle is true of it, show that the clauses that are *not* true of rival candidates are the ones rivals must explain away, and place it in the collection's own idiom — i.e. name the metaphor pattern and, where relevant, the Latin *enigma* it answers or inverts. Where the riddle contains runes, a cipher, or a Latin tag (as 90 does), the solution must explain that apparatus rather than treat it as decoration. For the damaged riddles, a solution must state explicitly which words are read from the leaf and which are conjectural, and must not rest on the conjectures.

For the residue as a class, the concrete unattempted move is a systematic source-and-parallel sweep: align every Old English riddle clause against the full Anglo-Latin and Late Antique enigmata corpora and against the glossary tradition, at the level of motif and of collocation, and report which of the competing solutions for 4, 28, 39, 55, 70 and 74 have a parallel and which have none. That is a computable, checkable result, and it would be a real contribution even if it solved nothing: it would eliminate hypothesis families. A claimed cipher or acrostic in any of these riddles must be shown to occur above chance given the letter frequencies of the text, from a script, not by inspection.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the collection is largely solved and the open residue is a handful of poems; a solution to 74 or 55 would be a notable event inside the field and invisible outside it.
- material 4: complete, well-edited, digitized, machine-readable, with a large corpus of analogues — but the specifically unsolved riddles 89/91/95 sit on damaged leaves.
- solvable 4: the genre guarantees the author had an answer in mind; not 5 because for the damaged riddles the answer may be unrecoverable by construction, and because some scholars read a few items as not riddles at all (89 is flagged in the concordance as possibly not a riddle).
- compute 4: the texts and the whole analogue tradition are digitized, and a mass motif/collocation sweep against the Latin enigmata and the glossaries is the obvious next move that has not been done at scale. Not 5 because the sweep would constrain rather than decide.
- verifiable 3: recognition would come by scholarly consensus — a solution that explains every clause and has a Latin parallel would be adopted — but not mechanically.
- crowding 1: 180 years of solution proposals, dozens per riddle, catalogued in Williamson and Orchard; every naive approach has been tried repeatedly.

## Unverified claims
- The concordance of proposed solutions quoted above is taken from the Wikipedia table, which cites the editions but which was not checked against Williamson or Orchard directly. The lists should be treated as a survey of what has been proposed, not as complete.
- Riddle 93's entry in that table gave no Williamson number and no solution list; whether it belongs in the unsolved set was not established.
- The characterisation of the methodological shift (Niles on Old English answers, Murphy on riddle patterns, Bitterli on the Latin background) is from the books' known theses, not from a fetched source.
- Whether any of riddles 4, 28, 39, 55, 70, 74 has in fact acquired a consensus solution since 2021 was not checked against recent journal literature.
