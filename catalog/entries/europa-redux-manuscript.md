# Europa Redux manuscript

## What it is
An anonymous illustrated manuscript, apparently made in Switzerland between about 1940 and 1946, containing some 7,000 miniature paintings of about 3 × 3 cm each. Many are captioned with one to three words in Latin letters that are pronounceable but belong to no known language: `ENVERAD`, `WORNDÄH`, `KOUNDRUIHA`, `TRADI MEO`, `OASTI EASTI`, `GUGIM DEHM`. The early panels copy European tourist brochures, and the draughtsmanship suggests a professional illustrator or copyist. It surfaced with the Brooklyn rare-book dealer Honey & Wax (Heather O'Donnell), was shown at a New York antiquarian book fair, and is known publicly through an Instagram account.

## What is unsolved
Whether the captions are a language, a cipher, a private or children's language, dialect distortion, or decorative nonsense; and who made it, and why, in wartime Switzerland. If the captions encode anything, it is one person's running commentary on 7,000 images, a diary-like object.

## What survives
The manuscript, in private hands (possibly sold since 2020). Only the images posted on Instagram, and those reproduced by Schmeh and on Medium, are public. No caption list or transcription exists.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Schmeh's 2020 post collected the hypotheses readers offered: anagram (tested without success), children's language or Jenisch, Dada-style nonsense or *grammelot*, filler text like lorem ipsum, and deliberate distortion (*Verballhornung*) of Swiss German, French or Italian words. A Medium essay (Merzmensch) covers the same ground. No analysis has used more than a handful of captions.

## What a solution would have to do
First, a caption corpus: every caption transcribed with the image it labels. Then any reading must show the caption words are systematic, meaning the same word under images of the same subject, which the pictures make testable. A cipher or distortion hypothesis must map words to a real language in a way that names what the images show. A nonsense hypothesis must show the reverse: no word–image consistency beyond chance, and phonotactics matching the invented-word profile of a German, French or Italian speaker.
