# Cad Goddeu (The Battle of the Trees)

## What it is
A Middle Welsh poem of some 248 short lines — mostly five syllables plus a rest — in the Book of Taliesin (National Library of Wales, Peniarth MS 2), a manuscript Daniel Huws places in the first quarter of the fourteenth century. It falls into several sections of very different character: a first-person sequence of shape-shifting and knowledge boasts; a catalogue in which named trees are enlisted and fight, the enchanter Gwydion animating them; passages naming Blodeuwedd and possibly Arthur; and cosmological and eschatological material. The National Library of Wales publishes colour images of the manuscript.

## What is unsolved
Whether the poem has a determinate content at all, and if so what the tree-battle refers to. The problems, in the order they bite: (1) the text is a composite — scholarly opinion has moved toward treating it as several originally separate pieces copied as one, so "the meaning of *Cad Goddeu*" may be the wrong question; (2) the Middle Welsh is, in the standard formulation, "difficult to translate because of its laconic allusiveness and grammatical ambiguity," so even the literal sense of many lines is unfixed; (3) the tree catalogue has been read as allegory of a historical battle, as a mnemonic for an alphabet, as a pharmacological or arboreal lore-list, and as bardic burlesque; (4) the relation of the first-person boasts to the battle narrative is unexplained; (5) no external witness or analogue fixes the referent.

## What survives
One witness, the Book of Taliesin, which contains roughly 56 to 60 poems, digitized in colour by the NLW. The critical apparatus is Ifor Williams's foundational work on the Taliesin corpus (*The Poems of Taliesin*, 1968, which excludes the legendary poems as later) and, decisively for this poem, Marged Haycock, *Legendary Poems from the Book of Taliesin* (2007), which supplies the edition, translation and commentary a newcomer must start from; her *Prophecies from the Book of Taliesin* (2013) covers the related material. John Carey has written on the poem's mythological content. Robert Graves's version in *The White Goddess* (1948) is a reconstruction, not a translation, and is not a usable text.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
The popular understanding of this poem is almost entirely Graves's: he rearranged the lines, read the trees as the letters of the ogham alphabet, and treated the result as encoded druidic doctrine. That reading has no standing in Celtic studies. Haycock's position — that the poem is a *burlesque*, a "grand parody of bardic language," and that the manuscript text is a composite of separate sections — is the current scholarly reference point, and it reframes the puzzle: the obscurity is the performance, not a cipher over a message. Against this, the poem demonstrably contains real mythological material (Gwydion, Blodeuwedd, the Fourth Branch matter), so a reading that makes it pure noise fails too. The honest state is: the field agrees the text is composite and that the Graves reading is worthless; it does not agree on how much determinate content the sections retain.

## What a solution would have to do
First, settle the textual question, because nothing else can proceed past it: establish by metrical, orthographic and linguistic criteria where the joins are and how many pieces the manuscript text contains, and publish the segmentation so others can test it. Then, per section: give a translation that construes every line without recourse to "obscure," mark every emendation, and state the section's genre. A claim that the tree-catalogue encodes something — an alphabet, a battle-order, a calendar — must (1) work on the manuscript line order, not a rearranged one, (2) cover all the trees named rather than the convenient ones, and (3) predict something independently checkable, for instance the same encoding in another Welsh or Irish tree-list. A claim that the poem alludes to a historical event must identify the event from a source and explain the shape-shifting sections, which allegorical readings ignore. Because the poem is a single composite copy with no analogue, the realistic bar is a segmentation plus translation that the next edition adopts — and a demonstration, from a script rather than by inspection, that any proposed tree/letter mapping occurs above chance.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: genuinely obscure and famous, but the field has partly resolved it in outline — composite, parodic — leaving a residue rather than a wide-open question; and the impact of a solution is confined to Celtic studies.
- material 3: a complete 248-line text in a digitized manuscript with a modern critical edition, but a single witness, no analogue, and a text whose very unity is doubted.
- solvable 2: the best current evidence favours deliberate obscurity and composite transmission rather than an encoded determinate message. This is the score that separates this entry from *Preiddeu Annwfn*.
- compute 2: a computational contribution is real but narrow — stylometric and metrical testing of the composite hypothesis to locate the joins, and chance-baseline testing of any tree/letter mapping. Neither decides the meaning.
- verifiable 2: with one witness and no external control, competing readings would remain; only the segmentation is mechanically testable.
- crowding 3: a modest scholarly literature (Williams, Haycock, Carey) around a very large popular one; the obvious scholarly approaches have been tried, but the poem is not picked over by specialists.
