# Aurora Consurgens

## What it is
A Latin alchemical treatise in the register of a biblical commentary: seven *parabolae* preceded by a prologue heavy with Scripture, Wisdom literature and liturgy, describing the work in the language of the Song of Songs and the Mass. Its most famous witness, Zürich Zentralbibliothek MS Rhenovacensis 172, carries roughly thirty-seven watercolour miniatures in which alchemical substances appear as human and animal figures — mercury as a serpent, gold as the sun, silver as the moon, an ouroboros in concentric circles. It circulated under the name of Thomas Aquinas; modern scholarship calls it pseudo-Aquinas.

## What is unsolved
Two linked questions. (1) Authorship and date: whether the text has any connection to Aquinas or his circle, and when and where it was composed. Marie-Louise von Franz argued for Aquinas on the grounds of scriptural and liturgical fluency, Dominican habits of citation, and a late-life dating; the field's working position is that it is a later pseudepigraph, but the arguments on both sides are stylistic rather than documentary. (2) Register: whether the parables describe operations on matter, a spiritual or mystical itinerary, or a deliberate fusion in which the alchemical vocabulary is the vehicle of a theological argument. The miniatures compound this, since they visualise the substances as persons.

## What survives
The illuminated Rhenovacensis 172 at Zürich is the principal witness and is fully digitized on e-codices. Further Latin manuscripts exist, and the text was printed in the *Artis auriferae* collection (Basel, 1572), which gives it wide sixteenth-century circulation. The standard modern edition is Marie-Louise von Franz's *Aurora Consurgens: A Document Attributed to Thomas Aquinas on the Problem of Opposites in Alchemy* (Bollingen/Pantheon, 1966), with Latin text, English translation and a long psychological commentary — the text is therefore available and translated, but the edition is a Jungian document as much as a philological one and its apparatus is not a full critical stemma. There is no openly machine-readable critical text.

## Prior attempts and current consensus
Von Franz's 1966 edition remains the only full edition and translation and is the necessary first reading, with the caveat that its authorship argument is minority. Barbara Obrist's *Les débuts de l'imagerie alchimique* (Le Sycomore, 1982) situates the miniatures in the emergence of alchemical illustration in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and is the necessary corrective on the images. Joachim Telle's lexicon articles on German and Latin alchemical literature give the transmission. A crucial philological fact for any attack: the *Aurora* draws heavily on Senior Zadith's *Tabula chemica* and on the Latin Hermetic and pseudo-Aristotelian corpus, so much of its apparently original scriptural-alchemical idiom is quotation. The consensus is pseudo-Aquinas, fifteenth-century transmission, with the composition date and milieu genuinely unsettled.

## What a solution would have to do
Authorship is the tractable question, and it has a hard test. A claim for or against Aquinas must be run as a stylometric comparison against the fully digitized Aquinas corpus (Corpus Thomisticum) with proper controls — Dominican contemporaries, the pseudo-Aquinas apocrypha, and the alchemical Latin the *Aurora* demonstrably quotes — and must report how it handles the fact that a large share of the text is borrowed material, which contaminates any authorial signal. Independently, a complete source apparatus would settle the date from below: identify every quotation and the latest datable source, and the composition window closes. For the register question, a reading must account for all seven parables and for the miniature program, and must explain why the text's operations are described only in scriptural vocabulary — a reading that chemicalises parable four and spiritualises parable six has chosen twice.

## Why the scores
- mystery 3: the field has a working consensus (pseudo-Aquinas) with substantial dissent, and the composition question is open; the stakes are real for the history of alchemy but bounded.
- material 4: a fully digitized illuminated witness, a printed sixteenth-century transmission, a complete edition and translation; not 5 because the edition is not a critical stemma and there is no open machine-readable text.
- solvable 3: the text is determinate Latin prose with recoverable sources, but whether it intends a chemical operation or a mystical itinerary is exactly where serious readers split.
- compute 4: two well-posed computational moves are available and undone — stylometric authorship testing against a fully digitized Aquinas corpus, and a mass source-parallel sweep against the digitized Latin alchemical and biblical corpora. Not 5 because the borrowing density degrades the stylometric signal.
- verifiable 4: a source identification is checkable word for word and would date the text; a stylometric result with controls would be accepted as strong evidence.
- crowding 3: one edition, one art-historical monograph, a modest philological literature, and a large Jungian readership that has not done the philology.

## Unverified claims
- The count of "roughly thirty-seven" miniatures in Rh. 172 is from encyclopedic report and was not counted against the facsimile.
- The dependence on Senior Zadith's *Tabula chemica* is standard in the literature on the *Aurora* but was not verified against a source-critical study in this pass.
- The 1572 *Artis auriferae* printing of the *Aurora* was not verified against the volume's table of contents.
