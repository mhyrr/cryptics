# BnF fr. 16106 ff. 208r–209v: the court decipherment of Saint-Goard's letter of 23 December 1579

Found 2026-11-10 (main thread). Gallica `btv1b90096628`, views 216 (f. 208r), 217 (ff. 208v, 209r), 218 (f. 209v).
PRIMARY. Working transcription by the main thread from full-size scans; no paleographer; `[?]` marks doubt, `[…]`
unread. Abbreviations expanded silently except where shown.

## What it is

A sheet headed by a later archivist "23 avril 1579 / Dechifré de la precedente", bound after a letter from Lisbon of
15 March 1579 (f. 206). **The date is wrong.** The text is the contemporary decipherment, in a French court
secretary's hand, of the cipher part of Saint-Goard's letter to Henri III of 23 December 1579 (ff. 363r–366r,
150 folios later). It begins where that letter's cipher begins and ends where it ends ("monarque de tout le monde").
It cites "la mienne du xij^e", which is his dispatch of 12 [November/December] 1579. Gachard's and Brémond d'Ars's
lists therefore contain no dispatch of April 1579, and the 23 December letter is not undeciphered: its decipherment
is misdated and misplaced. Mousset (1912, p. xlviii) noted the same kind of misplacement in fr. 16105.

Consequences for experiment 10: (1) the cipher reading has a complete sixteenth-century check; (2) the half of the
letter the readers could not read is available in clear; (3) the claim "no decipherment" must be restated as "no
decipherment with the letter; one exists at ff. 208–209v under a wrong date".

## English summary

Spain is swelling its war preparations on the frontiers and coasts of Portugal; 3,000 men raised in Valencia and
Murcia are marching to Cartagena to embark on Doria's galleys for Cádiz and Puerto de Santa María. The talk is that
this is to force the Portuguese people to take Philip as king, since the King of Portugal and the nobility want it.
The nuncio presses for an answer and is being strung along; "by love or by force they mean to be kings of
Portugal". Letters long on the road go with this one: those of Dom António de Castro, lord of Cascais, who asked
that the substance be passed to the King; Saint-Goard sends the letter itself, vouches that Castro is devoted and "a
very determined man", and notes that he holds Cascais, the whole importance of the entrance to the Lisbon river.
He will write Castro a word to keep him well disposed and tell him not to come and confer until told. He encloses
Pierre Dor's letter about the infante Dom António and has told Dor to keep him in hand until the King commands.
Spain has seized a castle from the Genoese[?] and tried others; extra companies have gone to Roussillon, Aragon,
Catalonia and Fuenterrabía; French governors should be at their posts and Provence and Languedoc watched; the
galleys at Cartagena are inside the Mediterranean and could join those of Italy. The Spanish are alarmed by troops
on the Burgundy and Lorraine frontiers and believe Henri III is forming leagues with England, Orange, the Germans
and the Swiss to stop Philip taking Portugal. A man Saint-Goard had reported, who was dealing with Philip and his
ministers behind his back, is in prison for debt and blames Saint-Goard. A passage, only partly read, concerns a
"Prieur" whom Saint-Goard wishes would give up his "communication" with "these people", and who conducts "all his
dealings" with Francisco de Ibarra, councillor of the Council of War, through "this good rebel". He ends: if the King
does not want his service, let him be sent somewhere so remote that no occasion arises.
