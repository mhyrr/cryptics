# Dom António de Castro, senhor de Cascais: context for Saint-Goard's 23 Dec 1579 letter

Compiled 2026-11-10. Scope: (A) who Castro was, which side he took in 1579–80,
what happened at Cascais in 1580; (B) whether historians already know of a
Castro / Crato-party approach to France through Saint-Goard or Pierre Dor in
Nov–Dec 1579 with Cascais on offer. Search strings and texts used are listed at
the end. Archive.org full texts were downloaded with Python urllib and searched
with regular expressions; quotations come from that OCR, lightly normalised.

## A. Who he was and which side he took

**Identity.** He was D. António de Castro, senhor (lord) of Cascais. Later he
was conde de Monsanto: pt.wikipedia lists him as "António de Castro, 4.º Conde
de Monsanto" (c. 1530–1602). His family's title became marquês de Cascais in
1643 under his grandson Álvaro Pires de Castro e Sousa.
- https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conde_de_Monsanto (SECONDARY)

**Captive in Morocco until late 1579.** Queiroz Velloso lists "D. António de
Castro, senhor de Cascais" among the noble captives taken after Alcácer Quibir
(1578). His ransom was set high:
> "subiram outros a dez mil cruzados, como D. António de Castro, senhor de Cascais"
> (English: "others rose to ten thousand cruzados, such as D. António de Castro, lord of Cascais")

- J. M. de Queiroz Velloso, *O reinado do Cardeal D. Henrique*, vol. 1 (Lisboa, 1946), ch. "A miseranda situação do reino". archive.org id `dli.ernet.535670`, https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.535670 (SCHOLARLY)

**Side: Philip II's party.** Danvila is clear on this, and he describes Castro
as a target of Moura's patronage before Henrique died:
> "Antonio de Castro, Señor de Cascaes, villa la más fuerte de Portugal, se ofrecía á su servicio"
> (English: "Antonio de Castro, lord of Cascais, the strongest town in Portugal, offered himself to his [Philip's] service")

The footnote cites Philip II to Osuna and Moura, San Lorenzo, 14 April 1579
(*CODOIN* VI, 344). Danvila later writes:
> "negociación … realizada cerca de D. Antonio de Castro, Señor de la villa de Cascaes, villa la más fortificada de Portugal y de otros muchos lugares, que dió por resultado el asegurar aquellas posesiones al servicio de España"
> (English: "the negotiation … carried out with D. Antonio de Castro, lord of the town of Cascais, the most fortified town in Portugal, and of many other places, which resulted in securing those possessions for Spain's service")

Tension to check: by Velloso's account Castro was still a captive in April 1579.
The April approach may therefore have gone through his family or agents, or
Danvila's dating may be loose. This is unresolved.

In June 1580, after the prior of Crato entered Lisbon, D. António de Castro
embarked with governor João Telo, who took his galley to Setúbal and the
Spanish side (Danvila, p. 627). Castro was received at Elvas among the nobles
who joined Philip. Philip's first mercedes (grants) included:
> "conceder títulos de Condes de Linares y de Monsanto á Don Fernando de Noronha y a D. Antonio de Castro, Señor de Cascaes, respectivamente"
> (English: "to grant the titles of Count of Linares and Count of Monsanto to Don Fernando de Noronha and to D. Antonio de Castro, lord of Cascais, respectively")

Philip later sent Castro to receive the Empress María (Danvila calls him
"Señor y Conde de Cascaes") "por la fidelidad con que siempre le había servido"
(English: "for the loyalty with which he had always served him").
- Alfonso Danvila y Burguero, *Don Cristóbal de Moura, primer marqués de Castel Rodrigo* (Madrid, 1900). archive.org id `don-cristobal-de-moura-primer-marques-d` (SCHOLARLY; it rests on Moura's correspondence)

The Cascais municipal heritage sheet agrees:
> "o Senhor de Cascais, D. António de Castro, conde de Monsanto, optou pelo partido filipino e juntou-se ao seu comando em Setúbal, avisando os espanhóis da existência de um boqueirão não defendido"
> (English: "the lord of Cascais, D. António de Castro, count of Monsanto, chose Philip's party and joined his command at Setúbal, warning the Spaniards of an undefended cove")

- Câmara Municipal de Cascais, "Recursos educativos – Invasão espanhola", https://www.cascais.pt/sites/default/files/anexos/gerais/new/invasao_espanhola.pdf (SECONDARY/POPULAR; it says "de acordo com várias fontes", "according to several sources", without naming them)

**Cascais, July–August 1580** (from the same municipal sheet):
- 30 July: Alba's fleet passes Cascais and lands at an undefended cove near the Guia (Laje do Ramil).
- The townspeople flee. Castro has his valuables stored in the Igreja de N.ª S.ª da Assunção, the parish church, so they survive the sack.
- The fortress of N.ª S.ª da Luz is besieged. Its alcaide (commander) Pereira de Lacerda and two gunners are hanged on 2 August.
- D. Diogo de Meneses, commander of the Portuguese forces and a former viceroy of India, is found hiding in a bastion and beheaded.
- Afterwards, the surrender of S. Julião da Barra "começou com conversações com o senhor de Cascais, António de Castro" (English: "began with talks with the lord of Cascais, António de Castro").

Danvila (p. 627 onward) also names the capture of Cascais and the death
sentence on "D. Diego de Meneses" as the high point of Alba's campaign.

**Conflict on the title.** Danvila presents Monsanto as a new grant from Philip.
pt.wikipedia numbers Castro as the 4th count of a creation dating from 1460.
That fits a renewal or confirmation of a lapsed family title better than a new
creation. Not resolved here; see Unverified.

## B. Was a Castro / Crato-party approach to France in Nov–Dec 1579 already known?

**Partly known.** The 25 Nov 1579 letter itself is known and has been cited.
Queiroz Velloso (1946) wrote:
> "Na Biblioteca Nacional de Paris existe uma carta autógrafa de D. António de Castro para João de Vivonne, senhor de Saint Gouard, embaixador da França em Madrid — carta datada de Cascais, a 25 de Novembro de 1579, isto é, logo que regressou do cativeiro — contando-lhe que Mulei Ahmede, não obstante cotar o jovem duque em quatrocentos mil cruzados, o dera de presente a Felipe II, sem exigência de resgate."
> (English: "In the Bibliothèque nationale in Paris there is an autograph letter of D. António de Castro to Jean de Vivonne, lord of Saint-Gouard, ambassador of France in Madrid, dated Cascais, 25 November 1579, that is, as soon as he had returned from captivity, telling him that Mulay Ahmad, despite valuing the young duke [of Barcelos] at 400,000 cruzados, had made a present of him to Philip II without demanding ransom.")

Velloso cites the letter a second time for Castro's claim that the sharif freed
"dez ou doze fidalgos mui principais de graça" (English: "ten or twelve very
leading noblemen free of charge") as a favour to Castile. Velloso disputes that
claim.

Velloso uses the letter only as a source on Moroccan ransoms. He does not
discuss why Castro was writing to the French ambassador, and he says nothing of
Cascais being offered.

The footnote markers did not survive OCR. Among the footnotes on that page is
Henry de Castries, *Sources inédites de l'histoire du Maroc*, 1re série, France,
t. II, pp. 69–78, which may be where the letter was printed. Not verified.

**The French channel in 1579 is well known; Cascais in it is not.** Bremond
d'Ars (1885) used BnF fr. 16106–16107, the same volumes that hold our 23 Dec
letter. He describes:
- Saint-Gouard's correspondence with the consul Pierre Dor (letters of 15 and 25 March and 8 October 1579);
- Dor as "entièrement gagné par don Antonio" (English: "entirely won over by Dom António") and a "petit brouilleur et finot" (English: "a petty troublemaker and sly one");
- Dor's journey to France to ask for help for the prior;
- night visits from Rodrigo de Lencastre on behalf of Bragança in February 1580.

His text contains no mention of Cascais. "Castro" occurs only as an OCR form of
"don Rodriguez de Castro", the Bragança envoy that Danvila calls D. Rodrigo de
Lencastre.
- Guy de Bremond d'Ars, *Le père de Madame de Rambouillet, Jean de Vivonne: sa vie et ses ambassades près de Philippe II et à la cour de Rome* (Paris, 1884/85). archive.org id `lepredemadamede00dargoog` (SCHOLARLY, older)

Danvila also knows the Saint-Gouard–Dor channel, the prior's offer to France
of Brazil for 12,000 infantry, and Spanish spies reading Saint-Gouard's
correspondence. His entries for "Cascaes" connect Castro only to Philip.

**Closest prior statement found:** the Velloso passage above. It knows that the
letter exists and that it went from Cascais to Saint-Gouard, but it reads the
letter only for Moroccan news. I found no historian who says that Castro, or the
prior's party, offered Cascais to France, or that France had designs on Cascais
before Henrique died (31 Jan 1580). Danvila's picture of Castro as Philip's man
from April 1579 makes Saint-Goard's framing in Dec 1579 notable: it suggests
either double-dealing by Castro or a misreading by Saint-Goard.

## Unverified / could not fetch

- Whether the 25 Nov 1579 letter is printed in Castries, *SIHM* 1re sér., France, t. II, pp. 69–78. The OCR footnote linkage is ambiguous and no copy of the volume was found online.
- Velloso's exact shelfmark for the letter. The task brief says BnF Espagnol 336; Velloso says only "Biblioteca Nacional de Paris".
- Velloso, *O interregno dos governadores e o breve reinado de D. António*: not found on archive.org.
- Durand-Lapie, *Dom Antoine Ier* (1905): not found on archive.org.
- Not reached: Léonardon; Bouza; Ribera, *Diplomatie et espionnage*; Valladares, *La conquista de Lisboa*.
- The date and form of the Monsanto grant (new creation or renewal), and Castro's death date c. 1602 (pt.wikipedia only).
- How Castro could offer service to Philip in April 1579 while still a captive (Danvila against Velloso).
- A search snippet mentioned a Calendar of State Papers index entry for "Don Antonio de Castro, governor of Cascaes", described as wishing to help the prior of Crato. The page returned HTTP 401 and was not seen. This may be a later or different person; treat it as unverified.
- The Cascais municipal sheet does not name its sources for the landing and execution details. It relies on the contemporary account of António de Escobar.
