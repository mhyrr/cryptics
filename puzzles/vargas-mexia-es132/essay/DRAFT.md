# Into My Hands

*Philip II's cipher letters and the fall of Antonio Pérez*

## Madrid, March 1578

Juan de Escobedo was stabbed to death in a Madrid street on the night of Easter Monday, 31 March 1578.
He was secretary to Don John of Austria, governor of the rebellious Netherlands and half-brother of Philip II, King of Spain.
Suspicion fell at once on Antonio Pérez, the King's own secretary.
In the standard account, Pérez arranged the killing with Philip's authorization: poison failed twice, and hired swordsmen finished it.
Who killed him is not much disputed.
Why is the part historians still argue over.[1]

Philip wrote his instructions to Pérez in his own hand.
We know the note through a copy presented in the murder proceedings.
He calls Escobedo “el Verdinegro,” literally “the green-black one,” a name for a sour man capable of dark deeds:

> “Cierto convendrà abrebiarlo de la muerte del Verdinegro, antes que haga algo con que no seamos despues a tiempo … Acedlo y daos priessa antes que nos mate.”
>
> “Certainly it will be well to hasten the death of the Verdinegro, before he does something that leaves us too late … Do it, and hurry, before he kills us.”[2]

Pérez had served as secretary of state since 1567, succeeding his father.[3]
Among the letters that passed through his hands were dispatches from Juan de Vargas Mexía, Philip's ambassador in Paris.
Vargas sent them to Pérez, and Pérez showed them to the King.
Two weeks before the murder, Philip told Vargas that Pérez had shown him the ambassador's report and papers on what one Diego de Alarcón was attempting.
Pérez had also shown him Vargas's account of affairs in France:

> “**Antonio Pérez me ha mostrado un discurso que l[e] haveys embiado sobre las cosas desse reyno.**”
>
> “**Antonio Pérez has shown me a discourse that you sent him on the affairs of that kingdom.**” (f. 34v)[4]

Between receipt and presentation there was room for the secretary's judgment.[5]
Vargas was also his friend, someone to whom he could write about his family and his troubles at court.

By the spring of 1579 the dead man's family was pressing a suit, the King would not stop it, and Pérez was writing to Paris to explain himself.
His letter survives in a volume at the Bibliothèque nationale de France, shelfmark Espagnol 132.
It holds the correspondence Vargas received from Philip and Pérez between December 1577 and May 1580.
Much of it is in cipher, sometimes for a few words in the middle of a sentence, sometimes for an entire letter.
Pérez's explanation is there, written three months before his arrest.
For a long time, readers could follow him only as far as the numbers began.

## Without the key

In 1844, the cataloguer Eugenio de Ochoa described letters written in conventional signs, mostly numbers arranged in a particular order, “que es imposible descifrar no teniendo la clave”: “which it is impossible to decipher without the key.”
He could see where the cipher interrupted the prose: “precisamente cuando se va á hablar de materias delicadas” — “precisely when delicate matters are about to be discussed.”[6]

In 1950 the historian J.-P. Devos published the Spanish diplomatic ciphers of Philip's reign, three of this volume's systems among them, without connecting them to it.
One he had reconstructed from a letter Vargas sent in the other direction, to the King.
In 2012 Samantha Rubino, an undergraduate at Ohio State, transcribed eight of Pérez's letters from the volume.
Where the clear writing gave way to cipher, her transcription said “[CIFRA].”
She thought the letter of 15 April 1579 might help explain the fall of Pérez, and most of what he says in it lay inside those brackets.
In 2020 Satoshi Tomokiyo matched Devos's three keys to the Paris volume and solved the fourth cipher himself.[7]

With a key, a line can be read in small pieces.
Tomokiyo published a worked example from folio 81 (a folio is a manuscript leaf).
These are its signs, rendered here in type:

| Sign | 28 | 20p̆ | 24p | 6+̄ | 12. | 25σ̄ | 24p | 15p | u. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reading | y | por | to | das | he | vis | to | lo | que |

“**y por todas he visto lo que…**” means “**and from all of them I have seen what…**”
The number 24 supplies *t*; the hook beside it adds *o*.
The number 6 supplies *d*, the plus sign adds *a*, and the bar adds *s*.
Together the two groups make *todas*.
Other signs stand for whole words: `u.` means *que*.
The difficulty is often deciding what the scribe actually drew.
The curl used for *i*, represented here by σ, can look like a joined numeral 6; reading it as another digit changes the result.[8]

We came to the volume in October 2026 with the published keys.
Language models transcribed the page images sign by sign, without seeing the keys or one another's answers.
Programs then applied the keys, keeping the reading of the signs separate from the Spanish they would produce.
Many letters were sent in duplicate, each copy enciphered separately, and nineteenth-century editors printed some of the King's drafts; where we had either, we checked our reading against it.

We were not the first.
A project called cabinet-noir, also working with models, had posted readings of thirty of these letters a few days before we began.
We found it after making our initial readings; where the projects overlap, ours are independent replications.[9]
For two passages below, we rely on cabinet-noir alone: the quotation from f. 34 and one name in f. 198, both identified in the notes.
Tomokiyo had already read several words of Pérez's April defence in 2020, including Escobedo's name and the claim of innocence.
Neither project's readings have been checked by a paleographer, a specialist in old handwriting, so the exact wording here is provisional.
Gaps and uncertain readings stay marked.

In the manuscript quotations below, **bold marks spans written in cipher**, with the corresponding English also bold; ordinary handwriting stays in plain type.
Brackets mark words supplied or uncertain in our reading.
Bold does not mean first deciphered by us: some passages had already been read by others, and quotations taken from printed books are identified in the prose.
The English translations are ours.[10]

## Reports from Paris

Years after the murder, defending himself in the proceedings against him, Pérez gave reasons.
One was that Vargas had reported a secret confederation between Don John and the Guises, a family of French nobles.
He offered it among the causes that had led Philip to order Escobedo's death.
In 1846 the historian François Mignet checked the dates and threw the claim out.
Pérez placed the warning around spring 1577, yet Vargas did not reach Paris until that December, and Mignet found that almost all his reports about the supposed understanding came after the murder.[11]

This volume holds the King's replies to Vargas from exactly those months.
On 24 January 1578 Philip acknowledged a report about Don John's envoy, Alonso de Sotomayor, and his dealings with the Guises:

> “**y de todo lo [que] los Guysas trataron con él cerca del proceder de mi hermano y con Alanson** … porque cierto son cosas que tienen mucha consideración.”
>
> “**and of everything the Guises discussed with him concerning my brother's conduct and Alençon** … for these are certainly matters that deserve serious consideration.” (f. 12v)

Philip was taking the report seriously two months before Escobedo died.
Pérez countersigned the reply.
Mignet dates Sotomayor's mission to the Guises to May; this January letter records an earlier encounter.[12]

Other reports were more extravagant.
In a February dispatch printed by Alexandre Teulet in 1862, Vargas passed on a story, through the Venetian ambassador, that the Scottish ambassador was working through the Guises to arrange Don John's marriage to Mary, Queen of Scots.
Don John would then take his army to England, with Guise assistance, and seize the kingdom.
Vargas called these “grandes quimeras”: “grand fantasies.”
Philip's March reply judged the marriage talk “**de poco fundamento**”: “**of little foundation**.”
That verdict is in cipher in the Paris volume, but Mignet had already printed it from the King's draft.
He still wanted to hear what people were saying, and he wanted Vargas to keep the Guises well disposed towards him.[13]

The words closest to Pérez's story come from a dispatch of 13 April, also printed by Teulet from an official decipherment.
Vargas had sat through a long talk with the Duke of Guise, who wished for “la union destas dos coronas” — “the union of these two crowns” — which together “podria dar ley al mundo, y partirsele entre sí”: “could give law to the world and divide it between them.”
Mignet records Philip's marginal “Ojo!”: look at this.
The two crowns were Spain and France.
Don John is not named in the passage, and Escobedo had been dead for two weeks.[13]

The January letter brings the dealings back before the murder, but it does not establish Pérez's claimed confederation.
No letter we can read from those weeks speaks of a league, a confederation or a union between them; the leagues mentioned in March concern other princes.
Some March leaves are missing from the scan, and we have not read Vargas's own dispatches, which are in the Spanish state archive at Simancas.

## The dead man's papers

Don John died on 1 October 1578.
Nineteen days later Philip wrote to Vargas in cipher about a matter they had been discussing.
He allowed that the latest event, which we take to be his brother's death, might have settled the matter:

> “**aunque con este último successo ha[n] cessado aquellas [dudas]**”
>
> “**although with this last event those [doubts] have ceased**” (f. 123)

He still wanted Vargas to find out:

> “**lo que en el negocio huviere passado, y hasta dónde se llegó en él, y las personas que en él intervenían … y aun si pudiéssedes aver algunos papeles o cartas del teatino o de otras personas, sería conveniente.**”
>
> “**what had happened in the business, how far it had gone, and the people who had taken part in it … and if you could obtain some papers or letters from the Theatine or other people, that would be useful.**” (f. 123)

The man called the Theatine in our reading remains unidentified.
Vargas was to try to obtain the correspondence itself.

On 4 December he named the business.
It was Guise's dealings with Don John, reported to Vargas by the Scottish ambassador:

> “**la cifra que tenía con mi hermano, y lo que el mismo embaxador discurrió con vos de la unión que avía entre ellos, y el de Lorrena, y de los 800 mil ducados que tenían juntos.**”
>
> “**the cipher he had with my brother, and what the same ambassador discussed with you about the union between them and the [Duke] of Lorraine, and the 800,000 ducats they held together.**” (ff. 154v–155r)

The King wanted that report tested:

> “**el fundamento que tiene lo de los 800 mill ducados, y dónde y cómo los tienen, y si mi hermano avía recibido alguna parte dellos.**”
>
> “**what basis there is for the matter of the 800,000 ducats, where and how they hold them, and whether my brother had received any part of them.**” (f. 154)

The King added:

> “**sin que persona ninguna pueda entender que vos hazeys esta diligencia por orden mía.**”
>
> “**without anyone being able to understand that you are conducting this inquiry by my order.**”

The reported union here involves Guise, Don John and Lorraine.
Eight months after Escobedo's death, the King was still asking what basis the report had.
His questions leave both the money and Don John's receipt of it to be established.
Our transcription of this letter has only one reader, though cabinet-noir's separate reading supports its general content.[14]

Pérez countersigned the order.
Four days later he wrote in his own name to repeat the request for papers, “**con el mayor recato**”: “**with the greatest discretion**.” (f. 157)
The man who would one day give Don John's French dealings as a reason for the killing was passing on the King's order to find out what they had been.

## Only in the private letter

Vargas and Pérez exchanged news about their families as well as affairs of state.
In September 1578 Pérez wrote to say he was sorry for the miscarriage of Vargas's daughter-in-law, and reported on his own household.[15]
Before leaving Madrid in 1577, Vargas had named Pérez an executor of his will.
He also provided that, if no member of his own family survived to use an educational trust, Pérez's sons could benefit from it.[16]

In January 1579 Pérez gave his friend instructions about the handling of letters.
He assured him that there was care over papers, and said that sensitive material should come by a particular route:

> “**solo** venga en la **[carta] particular** … y con esta forma no avrá peligro.”
>
> “let it come **only** in the **private [letter]** … and in this way there will be no danger.” (f. 179)

The reason he gives is that someone has too much business to examine all the letters.
Who that person is remains unread.
We cannot yet tell whether Pérez meant the King or another official, and what the instruction means turns on the answer.[17]

## Stay where you are

Pérez began his letter of 15 April 1579 in clear Spanish, acknowledging that Vargas would have heard the rumours.
Rubino had transcribed this opening in 2012:

> “Porque no dudo sino que avrá llegado por allá la grita y mentiras que han corrido por aquí estos días de cosas mías, diré aquí brevemente a V.m. la verdad de lo que passa, por su satisfación.”
>
> “Since I do not doubt that the outcry and lies circulating here these days about my affairs will have reached you, I will tell you briefly the truth of what is happening, for your satisfaction.” (f. 198)

Much of that promised truth follows in cipher.
Philip, he says, had meant to give him an office, which he calls the office “**de Vargas**”: “**of Vargas**.”
We cannot securely identify the office or the Vargas attached to it.
Pérez names the three men the King had told to organise the office: “**el Marqués de los Vélez, Quiroga y [Chinchón]**.”
Quiroga was the Archbishop of Toledo; the Count of Chinchón is reported to have opposed Pérez's claim to the post.[18]
All Madrid said the place was his.
Then the King held back and decided to divide it.
The cause, in Pérez's account, was “**la demanda que Escovedo me puso**” — “**the suit Escovedo brought against me.**”
Juan de Escobedo was dead; his eldest son, Pedro, and his widow were pressing the family's suit.
Pérez says the suit ended with his innocence established, and blames the false testimony and the time spent investigating it for his loss of advancement.[19]

In his later defence, Pérez said the Marqués de los Vélez had backed Escobedo's killing, declaring that even “with the Host in his mouth” he would name Escobedo as the man to sacrifice.
Mignet, following Pérez, dates the remark to Holy Thursday, 27 March 1578, four days before the murder.
Los Vélez died before the arrests, “full of suspicion and sadness,” in Mignet's words.[20]

Pérez calls the loss of the office a public blow.
The King should have rewarded him, he writes, “**honrrarme y acrecentarme para reparo de mi honrra y testimonio de mi inocencia**”: “**to honour and advance me, to repair my honour and bear witness to my innocence.**”
Being, he says, a “filósofo, como V.m. sabe” — “a philosopher, as you know” — he has asked to withdraw from service and live a quiet Christian life.
He wants to leave the people who “**me han procurado quitar la honrra y la vida**”: “**have tried to take my honour and my life**.”
Out of that request “**el [diablo] y** sus ministros,” “**the [devil] and** his ministers,” have made the rumour that he is to be banished.
Then comes the assurance:

> “**la verdad es que V.Magd no me ha querido dar licencia; antes dessea que yo me sossiegue y esté quedo.**”
>
> “**The truth is that His Majesty has not wished to give me leave; rather he wishes me to calm myself and stay put.**” (f. 199r)

According to Pérez, the King had the Archbishop of Toledo ask the Princess of Éboli, Pérez's ally at court, to persuade him to remain.
The archbishop had sworn great oaths that the King would feel his going.
If Pérez went on serving, “**dexándome en manos de V.Magd**” — “**leaving myself in His Majesty's hands**” — there would be favours and honours.
This intervention rests on the provisional reading of this letter; we found no separate historical source confirming it.[19]

Philip's own note to Pérez from the same month comes from a different manuscript, quoted by Mignet in 1846.
It was already in print; we did not recover it from cipher.
Pérez had complained that the King was avoiding him and had asked him to stop the Escobedo family's prosecution.
Philip put their fewer meetings down to his Easter devotions.
He would not halt the proceedings, and gave his reason:

> “mientras se pueda escussar que lo que se ha hecho de la muerte de Escobedo no asido con interbencion mia, bien será que se escusse … y assi os ruego mucho que os aquieteis y sosegaeis.”
>
> “so long as it can be maintained that what was done in the death of Escobedo was not by my intervention, it is well that it be maintained … and so I beg you earnestly to quiet yourself and be calm.”[21]

The same verb, *sosegar*, to calm or settle, is in both.
We do not know which letter came first.
Philip tells Pérez that he will not protect him from the prosecution, because protecting him would show the King's hand in the killing.
Pérez tells his friend that the King values him too much to let him go.

Pérez tells Vargas the suit has ended with his innocence established; in the exchange Mignet dates to April, he is asking Philip to stop it.
In his later account, Pérez says Antonio Pazos, President of the Council of Castile, told Pedro de Escobedo that Pérez and Éboli were “sin culpa como yo”: “as blameless as I am.”
Pedro gave his word “por mi, por mi hermano y por mi madre” — “for myself, my brother and my mother” — to speak no more of his father's death against either of them.
Mateo Vázquez, another royal secretary and Pérez's enemy, found another relative to keep pressing the King.[22]
The son's withdrawal could explain Pérez's claim that the suit had ended, while Vázquez's pressure would explain his appeal to Philip.
The account is Pérez's own, and Mignet gives no date for it.

Pérez ended by saying he did not know how the business would turn out.
He promised Vargas news, then returned to ordinary handwriting and his family.
This farewell, too, was already in Rubino's transcription:

> “Doña Juana y nuestros hijos tienen salud y besan las manos de V.m. muchas vezes.”
>
> “Doña Juana and our children are well and kiss your hands many times.” (f. 199r)

## Another hand

Pérez and Éboli were arrested on the night of 28 July 1579.
By the King's order the Archbishop of Toledo went to console Doña Juana.[1]

As recently as 13 July, Philip had written to Vargas, in clear Spanish, “Antonio Perez me ha mostrado la carta”: “Antonio Pérez has shown me the letter.” (f. 215)
Pérez signed that one beneath the King.

The next royal letter to Vargas in the file is dated 24 August.
It is written entirely in clear Spanish, and no secretary signs beneath the King.
Philip does not mention the man he had ordered arrested less than a month before.
He writes that Vargas's letters have come “a mis manos”: “into my hands.” (f. 218)[5]
Philip tells Vargas to stay in Paris until Don Juan de Idiáquez arrives, or until he receives other orders.
Idiáquez had been named to replace Vargas as ambassador.
He never went: by 13 September he was signing Philip's letters as secretary in Pérez's place, and that month's cipher tells Vargas to expect a letter from him.[23]

By October Philip acknowledges, again in clear Spanish, the letters Vargas has directed “a mis manos, como a las de Çayas”: “into my hands, as well as those of Zayas.” (ff. 228/231)
Zayas was the other secretary of state.
Pérez's part in receiving and presenting Vargas's correspondence is gone from what Philip writes.

We read every dated royal letter to Vargas from 24 August 1579 through May 1580 and found no reference to him, to Éboli, to Escobedo, or to Pérez's papers.
A brief reference could still lie in a disputed or unread passage; the witnesses do not agree on every word.
Vargas's own dispatches are known to us chiefly through printed excerpts and catalogue summaries.
The silence belongs to this side of the correspondence, as far as we can read it.
It shows what the King chose to write to Paris, and leaves open what Vargas already knew.[23]

## A paper in Paris

Philip continued to ask for news from Paris.
Twice that autumn he wanted to know how news had travelled the other way.

In September he asked in cipher about information reaching the French ambassador in Madrid “**de las cosas de acá**”: “**about affairs here.**”
If Vargas knew where it came from, he should say.
Philip also wanted to know whether an informant had named someone or given a more definite indication. (ff. 222/226)

In November the inquiry concerns a document.
A paper had reached Paris and was passing as the Council of War's opinion about Portugal.
Vargas sent it to Philip, who compared it with opinions submitted to him by private individuals and recognized it as one of those: “**del consejo no lo es**” — “**it is not the Council's.**”
Vargas was to find out:

> “**con destreza por qué vía y medio ha ido a parar allá este papel.**”
>
> “**with skill, by what route and means this paper has ended up there.**” (ff. 245/247)

Neither letter names anyone.
The King was hunting leaks in the months after he removed the man through whom his Paris correspondence had passed, and the letters go no further than that.[24]

Pérez was held for years.
He was tried for corruption and then for the murder, tortured in 1590, and escaped that April to Aragon and the next year to France.
He died in Paris in 1611.[25]

Vargas did not live to see those trials.
In 1580 he revised his will; Pérez was no longer an executor, and his sons no longer stood to benefit from the trust.
Nothing we have read says why.[16]
Vargas was buried in the second half of that year.
The embassy papers were in the care of his executors, and Philip's nephew, the Prince of Parma, asked that they be kept safe.
Julián Paz's catalogue summarizes the instruction with the words “sin comunicarlos a nadie”: “without sharing them with anyone.”[26]

The letters are in Paris still, scanned, where anyone can look at the numbers.

## For historians and cryptographers

### The volume and the keys

The manuscript is BnF Espagnol 132, available on [Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b10032556x) (PRIMARY).
Folio references above use its written foliation; *r* and *v* designate the front and back of a leaf.
The digital images are spreads, and their sequence numbers differ from folio numbers.
The scan omits ff. 26v–31r, including the rest of a March 1578 letter and its duplicate.
The undated f. 273 is an Italian Savoy memorial copied for Vargas, now read by two witnesses; it is outside the series of royal letters.

Cipher numbers follow [Tomokiyo](https://cryptiana.web.fc2.com/code/spanish3D.htm) (SECONDARY technical account).
He identified Ciphers 1, 3 and 4 among keys printed in J.-P. Devos, *Les chiffres de Philippe II* (1950), and solved Cipher 2 in August 2020.
Devos reconstructed Cipher 4 from Vargas's letter of 16 May 1579.
Cipher 3 is the system designated Cp.30; cabinet-noir reports its nomenclature, or whole-word codes, in Alcocer's 1921 publication as well as Devos.
We have not inspected Alcocer's printed table directly.

### From ink to text

Claude Sonnet 5.5 readers transcribed page images without seeing the key, a decoded text, or another reader's transcription.
Five Cipher 4 letters were reconciled sign by sign by a further reader working without the decoded text.
The f. 154 reading has one local transcription; ff. 165 and 167 have one reader per duplicate.
For the after-arrest letters, a second copy could supply the second witness instead of a second reader of the same image.
The clear f. 218 was read directly from its image.
Decoder versions and proposed additions to the keys were recorded in commits before the transcriptions used to test them.
Word division, punctuation, accents and English translations remain editorial interpretation.
Spanish quotations from Espagnol 132 follow the joined readings in the research record; quotations from printed editions follow those editions.
Square brackets mark supplied or uncertain text and ellipses mark omissions.
The quotation ledger records spelling variants, clear/cipher boundaries and known prior readings; bold type reports the manuscript's use of cipher, not a claim of priority.

Agreement between model readers does not establish accuracy: they can make the same mistake.
Spot-checks of the Cipher 4 transcriptions found shared errors even in agreed signs.
A Cipher 3 calibration matched 68% of Tomokiyo's labelled syllables on f. 83.
For the after-arrest letters, comparisons between decoded witnesses range from 62–91%, measured on retained alphabetic characters, including clear text and excluding unknown signs.
These are measures of agreement, not estimates of the proportion of the manuscript correctly read.
Some early readers stopped without completing a second pass; the later prompt required one, and readers who declared it incomplete were excluded and replaced.

### Values tested elsewhere

New Cipher 4 values were tested on letters transcribed after the values had been committed: `2H = que` fitted 83/84 occurrences; `Σ = o`, 37/38; `H = ne`, 14/16, with two unclear.
Devos's rule that a cross above a sign doubles its letter fitted 36/36 held-out occurrences.
These results come from `analysis/05-cipher4-v2/tally-judged.md`.
The proposed `21. = que` did not generalize reliably beyond f. 198; `21_ = qui` and `ra = oficio` remain insufficiently tested.

In Cipher 3, `u. = que` came from Tomokiyo's labelled specimen and worked in subsequent letters.
Cabinet-noir distinguished `35 = pr` from underlined `35 = pl`; our first guide had failed to ask readers to preserve underlines.
The guide was corrected before the after-arrest readings.
Duplicate encipherments also allow a code word in one copy to be checked against the same word spelled out in the other.
The proposed code for *particular(es)* has that support, but only two held-out occurrences.

### Checks beyond this reading

Mignet prints two passages of the royal letter of 8 March 1578 from its Simancas draft.
His April note from Philip is a separate check on Pérez's account: our cipher reading preceded consultation of Mignet's quotation.
The two April texts have separate transmission histories; their order within the month is unknown, and neither is established as a direct answer to the other.
Our f. 17 readings matched those passages at 78–87%; the duplicate f. 22 scored less well, 70–77%, with only one of four comparisons reaching the preselected 75% threshold.
Controls using another letter scored 36–40%.
For the letter of 28 March 1580, the draft printed by Teulet, vol. 5, pp. 213–214, matched f. 255 at 77–82%; its duplicate, f. 261, scored 70–72%, below the same threshold.
Controls scored 28–41%.
Teulet dates the draft “Février,” annexed to Vargas's dispatch of 21 February; the sent letter is dated 28 March.

These scripts measure the share of a printed passage's normalized letters matched in order within a window of the decode.
They do not require exact word agreement and do not measure historical truth.
Paz's catalogue provides a different check: its summaries independently name business present in the letters, including the archbishop of Cambrai, a Toulouse opinion on Portugal, and Hamilton's pension.
It is a selective finding aid, not a transcription of Vargas's dispatches.

The earlier readings in [cabinet-noir](https://github.com/el-descifrador/cabinet-noir) and [pangoleen/cipher-readings](https://github.com/pangoleen/cipher-readings) are CLAIMANT sources, produced with models and not checked by a paleographer.
Comparisons were made after our relevant decodes were fixed.
Two quotations come from cabinet-noir alone and were not tested by us: the f. 34v sentence, from a letter we have not read, and the name “Chinchón” on f. 198r, where our readers left the sign group unresolved.
For cabinet-noir, the pre-murder comparison reports median passage matches of 94% and 82% for its two scored letters; after-arrest comparisons report medians of 76–89%.
Pangoleen comparisons report 91–95%.
Each experiment includes controls on other letters; the figures describe selected passages, not whole-volume accuracy.
The contribution here is the comparison of the pre-murder chronology, the April accounts, and the after-arrest correspondence, with the reading process recorded for inspection.

### What remains unchecked

No human paleographer has checked our transcriptions.
Vargas's dispatches in AGS Estado K 1544–1558, and the earlier K 1543 conference material, have been consulted only through published excerpts or catalogue summaries.
The date of Mignet's report of a close understanding between Don John and the Guises (B.44 n° 89) remains unresolved.
The missing March leaves restrict the pre-murder result.
In September's leak inquiry, “Consejo” is a candidate reading of one noun, suggested by a spelled-out duplicate; it is not established.
