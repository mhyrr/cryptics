## Working titles

Working subtitle: *Philip II's cipher letters and the fall of Antonio Pérez.*

## Cold open (two sentences)

On 24 August 1579, Philip II wrote to his ambassador in Paris without mentioning the secretary he had ordered arrested less than a month before. Antonio Pérez had countersigned the King's letters and received the ambassador's private confidences; now his name was absent, and the King wrote that the letters had come “a mis manos”: “into my hands.”

Sources: `../OVERVIEW.md:90`; `../sources/history-2026-10-04.md` §1.3; `../analysis/07-after-arrest/countersignatures.md`. The absence in this opening concerns this one letter, f. 218, written in clear. The larger negative finding comes later, with its limits.

## Shape

About 4,800 words of narrative, followed by about 1,100 words “For historians and cryptographers.” Eight sections. Open after the arrest, show how we can read the file, then follow events from the murder to the custody of Vargas's papers. April 1579 gets the most space: Pérez describes the King's refusal to let him leave as reassurance, while the King's own note explains why he will not stop the prosecution.

The surprise belongs to the reader encountering this history through the letters. Use “we” sparingly for what this project did. The scenes come from documents and dated actions; no invented rooms, weather, gestures, or private thoughts. Introduce people by their relationship to the correspondence before adding titles. Short Spanish quotations receive an English rendering immediately. Explain “folio” once as a manuscript leaf.

## 2. Without the key · 650 words

**Scene:** Ochoa catalogues the volume in 1844 and reaches the passages he cannot read. Follow the separation of letters and keys: Devos prints three of the keys in 1950; Tomokiyo matches them to this volume and solves the fourth system in 2020. Briefly show Rubino's 2012 transcription stopping at “[CIFRA].” Credit cabinet-noir's earlier model-assisted readings in September 2026, which we found after our initial work. Call our overlapping readings independent replications.

**Quotation:** “imposible descifrar no teniendo la clave” — “impossible to decipher without the key.” (Ochoa, pp. 220–224)

**Demonstration:** One line from f. 81: “y por todas he visto lo que…” — “and from all of them I have seen what…” Show the signs above `y | por | to | das | he | vis | to | lo | que`. Explain one number and its added marks, then `u.` for the whole word *que*. Use the existing specimen in `../overview-build/template.html:76`; describe its glyphs as a typographic transcription.

State the qualification here: AI models transcribed the handwriting; code applied fixed keys; other copies and printed drafts supplied checks; no human paleographer has yet checked these readings. Save the scores for the specialist section. End by returning to the people whose correspondence this was.

## 3. Madrid, March 1578 · 850 words

**Scene:** Escobedo is killed in a Madrid street on 31 March. Explain the two secretaries: Escobedo served Philip's half-brother Don John, governor of the Netherlands; Pérez served Philip. Suspicion falls on Pérez. Give the received account of the killing and royal authorization with attribution, then isolate the question these letters can address: Pérez later claimed that Vargas had exposed a Don John–Guise confederation before the murder.

**Quotation:** “cosas que tienen mucha consideración” — “matters that deserve serious consideration.” (f. 12v, 24 January 1578)

Go back through the dated evidence. Philip already knew of the Guises' dealings with Don John's envoy Sotomayor in January. Marriage-and-England talk follows in February; Philip dismisses its foundation in March. Mignet's chronological objection to Pérez needs this qualification: an earlier report existed, though it does not establish the claimed confederation. The “two crowns” passage concerns Spain and France and comes after the killing. Pérez countersigned the January reply himself.

Keep the negative finding bounded by the surviving scanned pages. The missing March leaves and unread originals of Vargas's dispatches prevent a claim that no such report could have existed. Do not turn a failed justification into a recovered motive.

**Evidence:** `../OVERVIEW.md:385`, `:414`; `../analysis/06-premurder/README.md:78`; `../analysis/06-premurder/teulet-check.md`. [Manuscript: PRIMARY][ms]; [Mignet: SCHOLARLY, reporting Pérez's CLAIMANT defence][mignet]; [Teulet: PRIMARY texts in a scholarly edition][teulet].

## 4. The dead man's papers · 550 words

**Scene:** After Don John's death on 1 October 1578, Philip tells Vargas to obtain papers and find out how far the dealings went. By December he asks about Guise's cipher with his brother and a reported 800,000 ducats. Pérez countersigns the inquiry and sends his own instructions four days later.

**Quotation:** “sin que persona ninguna pueda entender que … es por orden mía” — “without anyone being able to understand that … it is by my order.” (f. 154)

Keep the questions as questions: the sum is reported money whose basis and disposition Philip wants established. His inquiry does not prove a payment or a conspiracy. Note locally that our f. 154 transcription has only one reader, although another project's reading supports the general content. Leave the unidentified “Theatine” out of the narrative.

**Evidence:** `../OVERVIEW.md:263`, `:271`, `:289`; `../hypotheses.md`, H6. [Manuscript: PRIMARY][ms], provisional readings; [cabinet-noir: CLAIMANT corroboration][cabinet].

## 5. Only in the private letter · 450 words

**Scene:** In January 1579 Pérez instructs Vargas about which letter should carry sensitive business. Establish their friendship through the correspondence and Rubino's account of Vargas's 1577 will, which named Pérez an executor. Let the practical instruction reveal how much depended on the secretary deciding what to show the King.

**Quotation:** “solo venga en la [carta] particular … y con esta forma no avrá peligro” — “let it come only in the private [letter] … and in this way there will be no danger.” (f. 179)

The identity of the busy person who cannot examine all the letters remains unresolved. Say so. The passage establishes a private route; it does not establish that Pérez was hiding this particular matter from Philip. Move into April with Vargas established as someone Pérez could address personally.

**Evidence:** `../OVERVIEW.md:307`; `../sources/history-2026-10-04.md` §8.4; H7. [Manuscript: PRIMARY][ms]; [Rubino: SCHOLARLY undergraduate thesis, citing printed will clauses][rubino].

## 6. Stay where you are · 900 words

**Scene:** On 15 April 1579 Pérez writes his defence to Vargas. Read it beside Philip's note to Pérez, dated to the same month by Mignet. Pérez says the Escobedo suit cost him an expected office, insists on his innocence, and describes the King's refusal of leave. Philip's note declines to halt the prosecution because intervention would expose his own involvement in Escobedo's death.

**Paired quotations:** “dessea que yo me sossiegue y esté quedo” — “he wishes me to calm myself and stay put.” (f. 198) And “os aquieteis y sosegueis” — “quiet yourself and be calm.” (Philip's note, Mignet p. 120 n. 2)

Let the two accounts occupy the same space. Their language overlaps; they are not a proven exchange of replies to this particular letter. Pérez presents remaining in service as favour. His report of the Archbishop of Toledo working through Éboli belongs explicitly to this provisional reading; no separate historical source checked in the dive confirms that intervention. Retain the unresolved discrepancy: Pérez tells Vargas the suit is over, while Mignet's April note has him seeking its termination.

Keep “the office of Vargas” brief and its identification tentative. End with the letter's domestic close, his wife and children well, before returning to July. Do not assign Pérez a state of mind he did not record.

**Evidence:** `../OVERVIEW.md:317`, `:392`, `:400`; `../analysis/05-cipher4-v2/readings-v2.md`; H3, H5. [Manuscript: PRIMARY; Pérez's defence: CLAIMANT][ms]. [Mignet: SCHOLARLY, printing Philip's PRIMARY note from the Hague manuscript][mignet].

## 7. Another hand · 450 words

**Scene:** Pérez and Éboli are arrested on 28 July. Return to August's letter: Vargas must stay in Paris until Juan de Idiáquez arrives to replace him. On the September letters Idiáquez instead appears as Philip's secretary. By October Vargas is sending letters to Philip himself and to Zayas.

**Quotation:** “a mis manos, como a las de Çayas” — “into my hands, as well as those of Zayas.” (ff. 228/231)

Describe the full after-arrest reading: no identified reference to Pérez, Éboli, Escobedo, or Pérez's papers in the dated royal letters through May 1580. A short reference could remain in a damaged or disputed reading. The finding concerns what is legible in these letters, not everything Vargas knew. His own dispatches remain largely beyond our view. The changed signatures can be seen without solving any cipher.

**Evidence:** `../analysis/07-after-arrest/README.md:67`; `../analysis/07-after-arrest/countersignatures.md`; `../canon.md`, “After the arrest.” [Manuscript: PRIMARY][ms]; successor's career context as qualified in the canon.

## 8. A paper in Paris · 650 words

**Scene:** In November Philip recognizes a paper circulating in Paris as one of the private opinions submitted to him about Portugal. It has been called the Council of War's opinion; he denies that attribution and asks Vargas how it got there. Place this beside September's inquiry into the French ambassador's source for news from Madrid. Neither letter identifies a culprit.

**Quotation:** “por qué vía y medio ha ido a parar allá este papel” — “by what route and means this paper has ended up there.” (ff. 245/247)

Paz lists a paper under the Council of War title in Simancas; we have not inspected it to establish the identification. Keep “Consejo,” the candidate noun in September's passage, in the specialist section. Do not cast suspicion on Pérez by arranging these inquiries as clues against him.

Finish with Vargas. Rubino records his removal of Pérez from the 1580 will; the reason is not established. Paz's catalogue records his burial later that year and Parma's request that his executors keep the embassy papers “sin comunicarlos a nadie” — “without sharing them with anyone.” Attribute that final wording to the catalogue's summary. Stop with the instruction about the papers; no closing lesson about AI, secrecy, or history.
